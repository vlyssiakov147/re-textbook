## 8.1 What is Construction Management?

<div id="ol-ch8-0"></div>

Construction management is the discipline of planning, coordinating, and controlling a construction project from first mobilization through to substantial completion and handover. It integrates cost, schedule, quality, safety, and scope into a single continuously managed system — and when any one of those elements shifts, the others move with it.

In real estate development, construction management exists at two levels. The first is the **contractor's internal management** of the work — how the general contractor (GC) or construction manager (CM) organizes labour, trades, materials, submittals, and site logistics to execute what the contract requires. The second is the **developer's management of the contractor** — how the owner monitors performance, controls changes, maintains budget discipline, communicates with lenders, and protects the project from scope creep, delay, and cost overrun.

Both levels matter. A skilled contractor with a negligent owner-side team is a project waiting to go over budget. A diligent developer team managing a poorly qualified contractor will spend the entire project fighting fires. Construction management at the owner level is not about micromanaging the contractor's means and methods — it is about maintaining visibility, enforcing accountability, and making good decisions when circumstances change.

### The Construction Management Hierarchy

On most mid-to-large residential or mixed-use development projects in Ontario, the owner-side construction management team includes:

**The Development Manager / Project Manager (Owner-Side)** is the developer's primary representative throughout construction. This person attends site meetings, reviews cost reports, approves change orders within their authority, manages the consultants, and coordinates between the contractor, lenders, and internal stakeholders.

**The Owner's Representative (Owner's Rep)** may be retained when the developer lacks internal construction management capacity or when project scale demands dedicated oversight. The Owner's Rep acts on behalf of the developer in day-to-day construction dealings — reviewing submittals, chairing meetings, tracking schedules, and escalating issues.

**The Architect of Record** administers the contract between the owner and contractor under the standard CCDC form. The architect reviews and certifies payment applications, issues site instructions, reviews shop drawings and submittals, and issues certificates of substantial performance. The architect is not the developer's employee — they hold a professional duty to both parties under the contract.

**The Project Monitor (Lender's Representative)** is engaged by and reports to the construction lender. They review monthly draw packages, conduct site visits, confirm construction progress against the draw schedule, and advise the lender on whether to fund each advance. The project monitor does not work for the developer — but their approval is required for every draw advance.

Understanding who each party works for is fundamental. The architect certifies to both parties; the project monitor certifies to the lender only; the Owner's Rep certifies to the developer only. Confusing these reporting relationships leads to misjudging whose approval you actually need.

## 8.2 Pre-Construction: Mobilization and Setup

<div id="ol-ch8-1"></div>

Before a shovel enters the ground, a significant body of pre-construction work must be completed. This phase — sometimes called **mobilization** — determines the quality of the execution that follows. Projects that rush through pre-construction because the schedule is pressing typically pay for it through avoidable problems in months three through twelve.

### The Construction Schedule

The first deliverable the contractor produces after award is the **baseline construction schedule**. This is a detailed, logic-linked sequence of all activities required to build the project, showing durations, dependencies, milestones, and the critical path.

The critical path is the sequence of activities that determines the minimum project duration. Any delay to a critical path activity delays the project completion date by the same amount. Activities that are not on the critical path have **float** — a buffer of time they can slip before they affect the overall schedule. Managing float is a key part of schedule control.

For a high-rise residential project in Toronto, the baseline schedule will typically identify:

- **Subgrade milestones**: excavation complete, shoring complete, tieback decommissioning
- **Foundation milestones**: caisson completion, mat slab pour
- **Structural cycle milestones**: floor-per-cycle targets (e.g., one floor per eight working days)
- **Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing (MEP) rough-in floors**: trades following the structure by a defined number of floors
- **Envelope milestones**: glazing and cladding start, envelope completion
- **Interior milestones**: drywall, flooring, fixture installation, suite completion floors
- **Systems commissioning**: elevators, HVAC, fire suppression, electrical energization
- **Occupancy milestones**: temporary conditional occupancy (TCO) by floor or zone, full occupancy permit

The baseline schedule becomes the reference document for the entire project. Monthly schedule updates compare actual progress against the baseline and calculate schedule variance. Lenders covenant off key schedule milestones — a delay past a covenant date triggers a lender notice requirement and potentially a default cure mechanism.

### Permits and Pre-Construction Submissions

The building permit is typically issued before construction begins, though in some cases a **shoring and excavation permit** is issued first to allow early earthwork while the full building permit is finalized. The developer's project manager tracks the permit status and ensures that no work begins without the appropriate permit authority.

In parallel, the contractor prepares and submits a volume of pre-construction documentation:

**Shop drawings** are detailed fabrication or installation drawings prepared by trade contractors or manufacturers, showing exactly how a specific element will be built. The concrete formwork system, the curtain wall system, the mechanical equipment — all require reviewed and approved shop drawings before fabrication or installation can begin. The architect reviews shop drawings for conformance with the contract documents; this review is not a re-design exercise.

**The submittal log** tracks the status of every required shop drawing, product data sheet, sample, and mock-up. A submittal log with outstanding items at the critical path is a schedule risk — material lead times mean delays in submittal approval cascade into delays in material availability.

**The site logistics plan** describes how the contractor will organize the site: crane locations, hoisting strategy, temporary facilities (site trailer, washrooms, worker amenities), material staging areas, concrete pump locations, and traffic management. For urban infill projects, site logistics is a constant challenge — the public sidewalk, adjacent buildings, and neighbouring properties all create constraints that a well-organized site logistics plan manages proactively.

**The quality plan and safety plan** are submitted to the owner and the applicable authority (Ontario Ministry of Labour, Infrastructure and Jobs for construction safety) before work begins. The safety plan designates the constructor (typically the GC or CM) who holds primary responsibility for site safety under the Ontario Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA).

### Lender Pre-Conditions and Loan Closing

The construction loan does not automatically fund when the permit is issued. The loan closes — and the first advance becomes available — only after a checklist of pre-conditions is satisfied. These typically include:

- Executed construction contract (with price, schedule, and scope confirmation)
- Issued building permit
- Fully funded equity contribution (or evidence that equity is available and will be funded first)
- Updated project cost budget reviewed and approved by the project monitor
- Contractor's insurance certificates naming the lender as additional insured
- Performance and labour and material bonds (if required by the lender)
- Environmental insurance or risk acknowledgement (depending on site history)
- Executed loan agreement, mortgage, assignment of contracts, and general security agreement

The developer's project manager and legal counsel manage the loan closing checklist. Delays in satisfying any pre-condition delay the first advance — and therefore delay the ability to pay the contractor and trades.

## 8.3 The Construction Meeting Cadence

<div id="ol-ch8-2"></div>

Construction projects are managed through a structured cadence of meetings, reports, and reviews. Experienced developers treat these rhythms as non-negotiable — the discipline of the meeting cadence is what keeps a complex project from drifting into chaos.

### The Weekly Site Meeting

The weekly site meeting is the operational heartbeat of the project. Attendees typically include the GC or CM's project manager and superintendent, the owner's project manager (or Owner's Rep), the architect, and key trade foremen or subcontractor project managers when agenda items require their presence.

The standard agenda covers:

- **Safety** — incident reports, near-misses, upcoming high-risk activities
- **Schedule review** — three-week look-ahead schedule, comparison to baseline, flagging of emerging delays
- **Submittals and RFIs** — status of outstanding shop drawings and open requests for information
- **Deficiencies and site instructions** — open items requiring resolution
- **Coordination issues** — trade conflicts, sequencing problems, change order initiation

Site meeting minutes are the legal record of project decisions. They are distributed within 24–48 hours, and any attendee who disagrees with the recorded minutes must issue a written correction. Minutes that go unchallenged are treated as agreed-upon fact in any subsequent dispute.

### The Monthly Draw Meeting

Once per month — typically aligned with the billing cutoff for the draw cycle — the project team meets to review the draw package prior to its submission to the lender. This meeting reviews:

- Actual costs incurred to date vs. budget by line item
- Cost-to-complete forecast and projected final cost
- Contingency status and drawdown
- Changes approved in the period and their budget impact
- Schedule progress and any schedule-related cost implications

The draw meeting is where budget variance is surfaced and addressed. A line item that is tracking 8% over budget after three floors does not get better on its own — the draw meeting is the forum in which the developer, contractor, and cost consultant examine why the variance exists and what is being done about it.

### The Lender's Monthly Site Review

Separately from the developer's draw meeting, the project monitor conducts their own monthly site review. They inspect construction progress, verify that the work in place matches the draw request, review the cost report, and prepare their report to the lender. The lender will not advance funds until the project monitor's report is received and reviewed.

The project monitor's report is not shared with the developer as a matter of course — it is an internal lender document. However, experienced developers maintain a collaborative relationship with the project monitor, address their questions proactively, and treat their concerns seriously. A project monitor who has concerns about schedule, cost, or quality will flag those concerns to the lender, and lenders act on those flags.

## 8.4 Changes and Change Order Management

<div id="ol-ch8-3"></div>

No project is built exactly as drawn. Design changes, unforeseen site conditions, owner-directed scope changes, and regulatory requirements all generate changes to the contracted scope and price. How those changes are identified, priced, negotiated, and incorporated into the contract is one of the highest-risk areas of construction management.

### Why Changes Are Financially Material

A single change order on a large project is rarely the problem. The problem is the accumulation of changes over the construction period. On a $120M construction contract, a cumulative change order volume of 8–12% is not unusual. That represents $9.6M–$14.4M of additional costs that, if not properly controlled and budgeted, will surface as overruns at the end of the project when contingency has been exhausted.

The financial exposure from changes is compounded by the contractor's ability to claim **delay and disruption** as a consequence of owner-directed changes. A significant scope change issued mid-construction does not just cost what the change itself is worth — it may also compress the schedule, require overtime, displace planned work, and increase general conditions costs. These secondary effects are often larger than the direct cost of the change.

### The Change Order Process

A formal change order process provides the discipline to control costs and prevent disputes. The standard process is:

**Step 1 — Request for Information (RFI):** When the contractor encounters an ambiguity, discrepancy, or missing information in the contract documents, they issue an RFI to the architect. The architect responds, and if the response results in a scope change, a Contemplated Change Notice is issued.

**Step 2 — Contemplated Change Notice (CCN):** The architect or owner issues a CCN to the contractor, describing the proposed change and requesting a price and schedule impact assessment. The CCN is a notification that a change is being considered — it does not authorize the work.

**Step 3 — Change Order (CO):** Once the price and schedule impact are agreed, a formal Change Order is executed between the owner and contractor. The CO adjusts the contract price and, if applicable, the contract completion date. Work should not proceed on a change without an executed CO or a written direction from the owner acknowledging the scope.

**Step 4 — Change Directive (CD):** In situations where time is critical and price has not been agreed, the owner may issue a Change Directive directing the contractor to proceed with the work. The cost is then determined after the fact, either by time-and-material records or by negotiation. CDs create cost uncertainty and should be used sparingly.

### Change Order Red Flags

Experienced owner-side project managers watch for:

**Unpriced work proceeding:** A contractor beginning work on a change before a CO is executed or a CD is issued. This creates disputes about what the work cost and whether it was authorized.

**Scope creep through submittals:** Design changes introduced through the shop drawing review process rather than through formal contract amendments. If a contractor's shop drawing proposes a substitution or a design deviation that changes cost, the substitution should be processed as a CO, not simply approved on the submittal.

**Bundled change orders:** Multiple changes combined into a single CO in a way that obscures the individual pricing. Each change should be traceable from initiation through approval.

**Retroactive CO submissions:** Contractors presenting a large batch of change orders at the end of the project, or late in the construction period, for work that was performed months earlier. Retroactive COs are difficult to audit and are a frequent source of disputes.

**Schedule-impact claims attached to small COs:** A contractor routinely claiming schedule extensions for minor changes. The cumulative effect of accepted schedule extensions can push the completion date well past the original contract date while relieving the contractor of delay penalties.

## 8.5 Schedule Management and Delay

<div id="ol-ch8-4"></div>

In development, time is money in the most literal sense. Every month the project is delayed is a month of additional interest on the construction loan, a month of additional general conditions costs for the contractor, and a month of deferred revenue. On a $150M project with a $100M construction loan at 7%, a two-month schedule delay costs approximately $1.17M in interest carry alone — before accounting for contractor extension costs, sales office operating costs, and investor carry.

### Types of Delay

Not all delays are the same, and the developer's exposure depends on who caused the delay and whether it was foreseeable.

**Excusable, non-compensable delays** are caused by events outside both parties' control — force majeure events such as extreme weather, government-imposed work stoppages, or labour disruptions (where not caused by the contractor). The contractor is typically entitled to a time extension but not to additional compensation. The developer absorbs the carrying costs.

**Excusable, compensable delays** are caused by the owner. If the owner fails to provide timely design decisions, issues late RFI responses, directs a scope change that disrupts the critical path, or delays providing access to the site, the contractor may be entitled to both a time extension and compensation for the costs of the delay. These delays are the most damaging because the developer is paying the contractor's extended costs on top of their own carrying costs.

**Non-excusable delays** are caused by the contractor — poor labour productivity, trade coordination failures, material procurement errors, or inadequate supervision. The contractor is not entitled to a time extension, and if the delay damages the developer (through lost revenue, increased carry, or liquidated damages), the developer has a claim against the contractor.

### Liquidated Damages

Many construction contracts include a **liquidated damages (LD)** clause specifying a predetermined amount the contractor will pay the developer for each day of unexcused delay past the contract completion date. LDs are not a penalty — they are a pre-agreed estimate of the developer's damages from late completion, which avoids the need to prove actual damages in litigation.

To be enforceable, liquidated damages must be a genuine pre-estimate of loss, not a punitive figure. For residential projects, LDs are often set by reference to the carrying costs of the construction loan and the opportunity cost of delayed occupancy revenue.

The presence of an LD clause creates an incentive for the contractor to aggressively document and claim time extensions — every approved extension reduces the days for which LDs can be assessed. The developer's project manager must be equally disciplined about resisting unjustified extension claims.

### Recovery Schedules

When the project falls materially behind the baseline schedule, the owner can require the contractor to submit a **recovery schedule** — a revised plan demonstrating how the contractor proposes to make up the lost time. Recovery typically involves increasing labour resources, adding shifts, working extended hours, resequencing work, or a combination of these measures.

Recovery comes at a cost. If the delay is the contractor's fault, the recovery cost is the contractor's problem. If the delay is excusable and compensable, the owner may be required to fund acceleration. The question of who pays for recovery is often heavily contested.

## 8.6 Cost Reporting and Budget Control

<div id="ol-ch8-5"></div>

Construction cost reporting is the system by which the developer, contractor, and lender maintain a shared understanding of the project's financial status at any point in time. A well-functioning cost reporting system provides early warning of overruns, creates accountability for cost decisions, and maintains the discipline required to deliver a project within budget.

### The Cost Report Structure

The construction cost report — also called a project cost report (PCR) or job cost report — is typically prepared monthly by the contractor (for construction costs) and by the development team (for soft costs and owner-side costs), then consolidated by the cost consultant or quantity surveyor into a single project-wide report.

The report is structured around the approved project budget, with columns showing:

- **Original budget**: the approved cost for each line item at the time the budget was locked
- **Approved changes**: the sum of all executed change orders affecting each line item
- **Revised budget**: original budget plus approved changes
- **Committed costs**: the value of all contracts and purchase orders issued to date
- **Costs incurred to date**: actual costs paid or accrued
- **Cost to complete**: the contractor's estimate of remaining costs to finish each item
- **Projected final cost**: committed costs plus estimated cost to complete
- **Variance**: projected final cost vs. revised budget (positive variance = overrun)

The variance column is where the developer focuses attention. A variance that is small or favourable early in the project may not indicate a problem; a variance that is growing over successive monthly reports in a particular trade package requires investigation.

### Contingency Management

Every approved project budget includes a construction contingency — a reserve for unforeseen costs that cannot be anticipated at the time of budget preparation. The contingency is not a license to overspend; it is a buffer for genuinely unforeseen events.

Construction contingency is typically sized at 3–8% of hard construction costs depending on the stage of design development and the contract type. A lump-sum GC contract with 100% construction documents supports a lower contingency than a CM at-risk contract priced from design development drawings.

The contingency drawdown pattern tells a story. If the contingency is being consumed steadily in the early months of construction, it signals that the budget was underfunded, the scope was not well-defined, or the contractor's initial pricing was aggressive. If contingency is preserved through mid-construction and then drawn down toward the end, that may reflect normal late-project scope resolution.

Lenders track contingency drawdown closely. A construction loan agreement will typically specify that lender approval is required for contingency drawdowns above a certain threshold, and that the developer must fund cost overruns above the total contingency from equity before the lender will advance additional funds.

### Developer's Contingency vs. Contractor's Contingency

Sophisticated developers maintain a distinction between the contingency held within the contractor's contract and an **owner's contingency** held outside the contract. The contractor's contingency (if a CM at-risk contract) covers unknowns within the contractor's scope. The owner's contingency covers risks that are the owner's responsibility: owner-directed changes, design development scope increases, unforeseen owner costs, and items excluded from the contractor's contract.

In the project cost report shown to the lender, both contingencies are typically disclosed, but their drawdown has different implications for the project's financial health.

## 8.7 The Draw Process in Detail

<div id="ol-ch8-6"></div>

The draw process — the mechanism by which the developer draws down the construction loan to pay for work completed — was introduced in Chapter 3. In the context of construction management, the draw process is the monthly operational cycle that connects site progress to cash flow.

### The Monthly Draw Cycle

The draw cycle typically runs on a 30-day calendar aligned with the billing cutoff date specified in the loan agreement. The sequence is:

**Days 1–5: Contractor billing preparation**
The general contractor or CM collects billing from all subcontractors and prepares their monthly payment application (also called a progress billing or Schedule of Values billing). The payment application allocates the month's billings across the contract's Schedule of Values — the pre-agreed breakdown of the contract sum into discrete cost components (e.g., concrete structure, mechanical rough-in, exterior cladding).

**Days 5–10: Consultant review and certification**
The architect reviews the payment application, confirms that the billed percentage of each Schedule of Values item reflects actual work in place, and issues a Certificate for Payment. The cost consultant independently reviews the application and confirms or adjusts the certified amount. Discrepancies between the contractor's billing and the consultant's certification are resolved through dialogue before the draw package is finalized.

**Days 10–15: Draw package assembly**
The developer's project manager assembles the full draw package for submission to the lender. The package includes:
- The architect's Certificate for Payment
- The cost consultant's cost report (actual costs, cost-to-complete, projected final cost, contingency status)
- A schedule update confirming progress against the baseline
- Copies of all new change orders executed in the period
- Invoices and payment receipts for soft cost items (professional fees, permits, municipal levies)
- Statutory declarations from the contractor confirming all subcontractors have been paid from prior draw advances
- Any updated insurance certificates or lien-related documentation required by the lender

**Days 15–20: Lender and project monitor review**
The lender's project monitor reviews the draw package, conducts a site visit (or reviews photos and documentation if a site visit is not required every cycle), and prepares their recommendation to the lender. The lender's credit department reviews the project monitor's report, confirms that all loan covenants are in good standing, and approves the advance.

**Days 20–30: Advance and payment**
The lender advances the approved draw amount to the developer's construction account. The developer releases payment to the contractor within the time period specified in the loan agreement and the Ontario Construction Act. The contractor, in turn, pays the subcontractors within the holdback and payment periods prescribed by the Act.

### Holdbacks Under the Ontario Construction Act

The Ontario Construction Act (formerly the Construction Lien Act, renamed in 2018) governs payment on all construction contracts in Ontario. It imposes mandatory holdback requirements that affect every draw cycle:

**Basic holdback:** The owner must hold back 10% of every progress payment made to the contractor throughout the project. The contractor must hold back 10% from every payment made to subcontractors, and so on down the payment chain. The holdback is security for potential lien claims and cannot be released until the lien period has expired.

**Prompt payment obligations:** The Act imposes strict timelines for payment. The owner must pay the contractor within 28 days of receiving a proper invoice. The contractor must pay subcontractors within 7 days of receiving payment from the owner. Failure to pay on time, without a proper notice of non-payment, creates an entitlement to interest and potentially adjudication.

**Adjudication:** The Act introduced a mandatory rapid adjudication mechanism for payment disputes. Either party can refer a payment dispute to an adjudicator, who must issue a decision within a prescribed timeframe. Adjudication decisions are binding and enforceable while the project continues — they do not wait for litigation.

The holdback mechanics mean that the developer is always holding 10% of amounts certified by the architect. These holdbacks accumulate throughout the project and are released — in accordance with the Act's prescribed procedures — once the lien period expires after substantial performance or after the project is fully completed.

## 8.8 Substantial Performance and Occupancy

<div id="ol-ch8-7"></div>

The end of the construction phase is not a single event but a series of milestones, each with distinct legal, financial, and regulatory significance.

### Substantial Performance

Under the Ontario Construction Act, **substantial performance** is reached when the project is ready for use for its intended purpose, or when the remaining incomplete items represent less than 3% of the first $1,000,000 of the contract price plus 2% of the balance (subject to the Act's specific formula). Substantial performance is a legal trigger, not a subjective judgment.

Upon substantial performance:

- The architect issues a **Certificate of Substantial Performance (CSP)**
- Publication of the CSP triggers the 45-day period during which subcontractors and suppliers may register liens on the project
- Once the 45-day lien period expires without liens, the basic holdback retained throughout the project can be released
- Warranty periods under the contract typically begin to run from substantial performance

For high-rise residential projects, substantial performance often occurs floor-by-floor or zone-by-zone through a **phased substantial performance** process, enabling the developer to achieve occupancy of lower floors while upper floors are still under construction.

### Occupancy Permits

Residential condominium and rental projects in Ontario require specific occupancy permissions before any unit can be occupied:

**Temporary Conditional Occupancy (TCO)** is issued by the municipality when a portion of a building is ready for occupancy but the building as a whole has not yet received its final occupancy permit. TCOs are granted floor-by-floor or zone-by-zone in staged occupancy scenarios. They permit occupation subject to conditions — typically that certain life safety systems are fully operational and that outstanding work is completed within a defined timeframe.

**Final Occupancy Permit (FOP)** is issued by the municipal building department when all construction is complete and all conditions of the building permit have been satisfied. The FOP signals that the building meets the Ontario Building Code requirements for permanent occupation.

For condominium projects, **Tarion** (administered under the Home Construction Regulatory Authority) has its own occupancy-related requirements. The condominium developer must provide purchasers with a statement of critical dates including the estimated occupancy date. Late occupancy triggers statutory compensation obligations under the Ontario New Home Warranties Plan Act.

### Deficiency Management

At substantial performance, a **deficiency list** (sometimes called a punch list) is compiled documenting all incomplete or non-conforming work that must be rectified before final completion. The deficiency list is prepared by the architect, the owner's project manager, and in residential projects, unit-by-unit by the purchasers or their representatives.

Deficiency management is operationally intensive on large residential projects. A 300-unit condominium may generate thousands of individual deficiency items — ranging from paint touchups to mechanical adjustments to cabinet hardware — each of which must be assigned, rectified, and confirmed closed.

Deficiency holdbacks — amounts retained from the contractor's final payment until deficiencies are closed — are a standard mechanism for ensuring completion. The developer should not release final payment until the deficiency list is substantially resolved and the warranty period has commenced.

<div id="ol-ch8-ae"></div>

### AE-8.1 — Interpreting a Cost Report Variance

**Scenario:**
You are the Senior Analyst on a 28-storey residential rental project in Mississauga. The project is 14 months into a 36-month construction schedule. The most recent monthly cost report shows the following for two trade packages:

| Trade Package | Original Budget | Approved COs | Revised Budget | Projected Final Cost | Variance |
|---------------|-----------------|--------------|----------------|----------------------|----------|
| Concrete Structure | $18,400,000 | $620,000 | $19,020,000 | $20,180,000 | +$1,160,000 |
| Mechanical Rough-In | $7,200,000 | $85,000 | $7,285,000 | $7,310,000 | +$25,000 |

The construction contingency was originally $3,200,000. $1,100,000 has been drawn to date. The remaining contingency is $2,100,000.

**Questions:**

**a)** What is the concrete structure variance as a percentage of the revised budget? What questions should you be raising about this variance at the next draw meeting?

**b)** Is the remaining contingency sufficient to absorb the projected concrete overrun? What does the contingency drawdown pattern suggest about the overall project budget?

**c)** At what point would the lender's project monitor likely require a formal written explanation of the concrete variance from the contractor?

---

**AE-8.1 Answers:**

**a) Concrete structure variance:**

Variance % = $1,160,000 / $19,020,000 = **6.1% over revised budget**

At the draw meeting, the project manager should be asking:

- What is driving the variance — is it a productivity issue (labour hours per unit of work above estimate), a scope issue (more concrete than the drawings reflected), a materials cost issue (concrete pricing above tendered rates), or a combination?
- Was the concrete contract a fixed-price lump sum or a unit-price contract? Unit-price contracts are more exposed to quantity overruns.
- At 14 months into a 36-month schedule, approximately 39% of the schedule has elapsed. Is the structure 39% complete? If the structure is 30% complete but 46% of budget is consumed, the overrun signal is stronger.
- Has the contractor submitted a formal variance explanation? If not, one should be requested in writing.

**b) Contingency sufficiency:**

Remaining contingency: $2,100,000
Projected concrete overrun: $1,160,000
Remaining headroom after concrete: $940,000

The remaining contingency is technically sufficient to absorb the projected concrete overrun — but only if no other trade packages develop overruns. With 22 months of construction remaining, having $940,000 of contingency headroom on a project with a full mechanical, electrical, and cladding scope still ahead is a thin margin.

The drawdown pattern is concerning: $1,100,000 of contingency consumed in the first 14 months of a 36-month project suggests budget pressure is appearing early. This warrants a broader review of cost-to-complete estimates across all trade packages to assess whether the contingency will survive the full construction period.

**c) Lender notification threshold:**

Most construction loan agreements require the developer to notify the lender in writing when any line item is projected to overrun by more than a defined threshold — commonly 5–10% or a fixed dollar amount (e.g., $500,000). A 6.1% projected overrun on a $19M concrete package almost certainly exceeds the notification threshold in the loan agreement. The project manager should review the covenant language and, if a notification requirement is triggered, prepare a variance explanation letter for the lender proactively rather than waiting for the project monitor to flag it.

### AE-8.2 — Change Order Scenario

**Scenario:**
During concrete forming on floor 18 of a 32-storey tower, the GC's superintendent discovers that the structural drawings show a 400mm slab at the mechanical room level, but the mechanical drawings show ductwork routing that physically conflicts with the slab depth as drawn. The conflict has not previously been identified. The GC brings the RFI to the site meeting.

The architect reviews the conflict and issues a CCN directing the GC to reduce the slab depth to 300mm in the affected zone and re-route the mechanical ductwork. The GC responds with:
- Direct cost of slab revision: $38,000
- Direct cost of mechanical re-routing: $52,000
- Delay claim: 4 working days on the critical path, costing $28,000/day in general conditions

The total CO request is $342,000 ($90,000 direct + $252,000 delay).

**Questions:**

**a)** Who is most likely responsible for this conflict — the owner, the architect, or the contractor? How should this affect how the developer responds to the CO request?

**b)** Is the $252,000 delay claim reasonable on its face? What information would you request before accepting, negotiating, or rejecting it?

**c)** The developer's project manager is considering issuing a Change Directive rather than a CO to avoid delay while pricing is disputed. What are the risks of this approach?

---

**AE-8.2 Answers:**

**a) Responsibility:**

A conflict between the structural drawings and mechanical drawings is a design coordination error. Proper BIM (Building Information Modelling) coordination or manual drawing review during the design phase should have identified this conflict before the drawings were issued for construction. The conflict is most likely the responsibility of the **architect and design consultants** — specifically a failure of inter-discipline coordination.

This matters because: (i) the developer has a potential claim against the design consultants for the cost of the change; (ii) the developer's obligation to pay the GC for the change does not automatically create a loss — if the design consultant's professional liability insurance responds, the developer may recover the costs; (iii) the developer should not accept the CO's costs as simply absorbed before exploring whether the design consultant is responsible.

**b) Delay claim analysis:**

$28,000/day in general conditions is within a plausible range for a large commercial GC on a high-rise project (superintendent, project manager, site trailer, crane, security, temporary utilities, insurance, and overhead). But before accepting 4 days at that rate, the developer should request:

- Detailed general conditions cost breakdown to verify the daily rate
- A schedule impact analysis demonstrating that this conflict is actually on the critical path, not on a trade with float that could absorb the delay
- Labour productivity records or time-and-material records from the affected period
- Confirmation that the GC mitigated the delay to the extent possible (e.g., redeploying labour to non-affected areas rather than standing idle)

A 4-day delay on the critical path is plausible for a floor-level structural conflict; but if the structural work can proceed on the rest of the floor while the mechanical zone is resolved, the critical path impact may be less than 4 days.

**c) Risks of a Change Directive:**

A CD is appropriate when time is genuinely critical and price cannot be agreed quickly. Its risks include:

- **Cost uncertainty:** Work proceeds without an agreed price. The final cost is determined by time-and-material records, which the developer cannot audit in real time. The final cost often exceeds the contractor's original estimate.
- **Scope creep:** Without an agreed scope in writing, there is room for the contractor to include work in their T&M records that the developer considers outside the directive.
- **Weakened negotiating position:** Once the work is done and the developer has benefited from it, their leverage to dispute the cost is reduced.

In this scenario, the direct costs ($90,000) are agreed; only the delay claim is in dispute. The better approach is to execute a CO for the $90,000 direct costs while formally reserving the right to dispute the delay claim. This allows the work to proceed, limits cost uncertainty, and preserves the developer's ability to negotiate or litigate the delay component separately.

### AE-8.3 — Occupancy and Holdback Mechanics

**Scenario:**
A 22-storey, 240-unit purpose-built rental tower in Etobicoke achieves substantial performance as defined under the Ontario Construction Act. The architect issues the CSP. The total contract sum (inclusive of all approved change orders) is $64,200,000. The developer has been retaining the 10% basic holdback throughout construction.

**Questions:**

**a)** What is the total basic holdback that should have accumulated over the construction period?

**b)** When can the developer release the basic holdback, and what must happen before release is appropriate?

**c)** A subtrade is claiming it was not paid by the GC for $180,000 of electrical work performed in months 8–11 of construction. The CSP has just been published. What are the subtrade's options, and how does this affect the developer's ability to release the holdback?

---

**AE-8.3 Answers:**

**a) Total basic holdback:**

Basic holdback = 10% x $64,200,000 = **$6,420,000**

This amount has been retained from progress payments throughout the project. It sits in the developer's construction account (or a designated holdback account) pending the conditions for release under the Ontario Construction Act.

**b) Conditions for holdback release:**

The basic holdback cannot be released until:

1. The Certificate of Substantial Performance has been published (done)
2. The **45-day lien period** following publication of the CSP has expired without any liens being registered against the project
3. If liens are registered, those liens must be vacated (by payment into court, by bond, or by resolution with the lien claimant) before the holdback attributable to that subcontract can be released
4. The developer has received statutory declarations from the GC confirming that all subcontractors and suppliers have been paid in full for the period covered by the holdback release

Only once the 45-day period has run and the project is lien-free (or liens have been vacated) can the developer safely release the holdback.

**c) Subtrade's options and effect on holdback:**

The electrical subtrade has a right to register a **construction lien** against the project within the 45-day period following publication of the CSP. If registered within that window, the lien is valid and attaches to the owner's interest in the land.

This lien directly affects the developer: the basic holdback cannot be released while the lien is outstanding. The developer's options are:

- **Vacate the lien by paying the amount into court** — this releases the lien from the title and allows the holdback to be released, but the funds in court remain subject to the lien claim outcome
- **Post a lien bond** — a surety bond in the amount of the lien, which vacates the lien from title and substitutes the bond as security
- **Negotiate a resolution** with the subtrade directly, which may require the developer to withhold a corresponding amount from the GC's holdback release while the dispute is resolved

The developer's exposure here arises because the GC appears to have failed to pay a subtrade — a risk the holdback mechanism was designed to address. The developer did everything correctly by retaining the holdback; the Act now provides the mechanism to protect the subtrade while the dispute with the GC is resolved.

## Chapter Summary

Construction management is the discipline that converts a permitted design and an executed contract into a finished building — and for the developer, it is the phase where the financial model faces its most sustained test. The key insight running through this chapter is that construction management at the owner level is not about directing the work; it is about maintaining visibility, enforcing accountability, and making good decisions under pressure. That requires understanding the management hierarchy and who each party — the architect, the project monitor, the Owner's Rep — actually works for, because the answer shapes every approval, certification, and payment decision made over the construction period.

Pre-construction discipline pays dividends throughout the project. A well-prepared baseline schedule, a complete submittal log, and a satisfied lender pre-condition checklist prevent the majority of early-project cash flow and permitting problems before they arise. Once construction begins, the weekly site meeting and monthly draw cycle are the operational rhythms that keep cost, schedule, and scope aligned — and it is in those forums where variance trends must be surfaced and addressed before they compound.

Change orders are the primary mechanism by which construction budgets are eroded. The cumulative volume of changes on a large project is always larger than any single change suggests, and the delay and disruption claims attached to owner-directed changes often exceed the direct cost of the change itself. Disciplined change management — requiring a CO before work proceeds, resisting retroactive submissions, and auditing delay claims rigorously — is one of the highest-value activities an owner-side project manager can perform. The same discipline applies to schedule management: understanding the three types of delay and their different compensation consequences determines who bears the cost of lost time.

The Ontario Construction Act creates the legal framework within which every payment decision is made. The 10% basic holdback, the prompt payment timelines, the adjudication mechanism, and the 45-day lien period following the Certificate of Substantial Performance are not administrative formalities — they are the rules of the payment system, and failure to follow them creates direct financial and legal exposure for the developer. Substantial performance and the occupancy milestone sequence that follows it mark the transition from construction to operations, but only after the deficiency list is managed to closure and the holdback conditions for release are properly satisfied.
