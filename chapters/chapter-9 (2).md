> **In Practice — Senior Analyst Perspective**
> Budget management is the daily heartbeat of the construction phase. It is also where the Senior Analyst role lives most fully. Chapter 8 introduced the cost report, the draw process, and the contingency log as components of the broader construction management discipline. Almost every output you produce in a given month traces back to the discipline introduced in this chapter.

## 9.1 What Is Budget Management?

<div id="ol-ch9-0"></div>

Budget management is the discipline of tracking, forecasting, and controlling project costs across the full development lifecycle, from the initial pro forma through to the final reconciliation at project closeout. Cost control is its operational counterpart — the daily and monthly activities of approving expenditures, authorizing change orders, monitoring trade billings, releasing draw advances, and ensuring that the project completes within the funding envelope established by the capital stack.

The two terms are often used interchangeably in industry conversation, but they describe different functions. Budget management is the strategic activity: setting the budget, structuring contingencies, defining what counts as in-scope, deciding when to update the forecast, and communicating the financial state of the project to lenders and investors. Cost control is the tactical activity: reviewing invoices, checking that change orders are properly priced and authorized, comparing trade billings to the schedule of values, and flagging variances for action.

A development project of any meaningful size will have several budgets in circulation at the same time. Understanding which budget is being referenced in any given conversation is the first discipline of budget management.

**The proforma budget** is the budget produced during feasibility, before land closing. It is heavily reliant on benchmarks, comparable projects, and high-level estimates. The proforma budget is the basis on which the project was approved by the developer's investment committee and on which the equity raise was conducted.

**The pre-construction budget** is the budget produced after design has progressed through schematic design and design development, and after the cost consultant has issued one or more Class C or Class B estimates. It is the budget against which entitlements work was conducted and against which the construction loan was underwritten.

**The construction budget** is the budget that is locked in once construction contracts have been awarded and trades have been bought out. It is the operating budget for the construction phase. Unlike the prior two budgets, this budget is grounded in signed contracts and tender results rather than estimates and benchmarks.

**The current forecast** is the budget as it stands today, after applying all approved change orders, all reforecasted trade costs, all known contingency drawdowns, and all updated soft cost projections. The current forecast is the moving target that is reported each month in the project cost report and submitted to the lender as part of each draw package.

**The final cost** is the closed-out budget, produced at the end of the project once all contracts have been finalized, all change orders settled, all holdbacks released, and all soft costs reconciled. The variance between the construction budget and the final cost is the project's total budget performance — and the basis on which the development manager's bonus, the GP promote, and the lender's takeout assessment are all evaluated.

The role of budget management is to maintain a disciplined relationship between these budgets — knowing which one applies to which decision, knowing when a variance has crossed a threshold that requires escalation, and knowing how to communicate budget status to each audience (lender, investor, sponsor, project team) in the way that audience requires.

## 9.2 The Project Cost Report (PCR) — Architecture and Mechanics

<div id="ol-ch9-1"></div>

The Project Cost Report, or PCR, is the central operating document of construction-phase budget management. Every developer, cost consultant, and project monitor in the industry produces or consumes a PCR on a monthly cycle. The PCR is the single source of truth for cost status — and the document around which the entire monthly draw cycle is built.

A well-structured PCR is organized around a column architecture that allows the reader to see at a glance how the project is performing. The columns appear in the order described below, reading from left to right.

**Column 1 — Original Budget.** This is the construction budget as adopted at the start of the construction phase. It does not change for the life of the project, regardless of subsequent reforecasts or change orders. The Original Budget column anchors the variance analysis: it is the baseline against which performance is measured.

**Column 2 — Approved Change Orders to Date.** This column reflects the cumulative dollar value of all change orders that have been formally executed and approved through the change order process described in Chapter 8. It includes both additive change orders (which increase the budget) and credit change orders (which decrease it). The sum of Column 1 + Column 2 is sometimes referred to as the **revised budget** or **approved budget**.

**Column 3 — Pending Change Orders.** This column tracks change orders that have been issued, priced, and submitted but not yet formally approved. Some PCR formats split this further into "Pending Approval" and "Anticipated Change Orders" — the latter being known cost issues for which a change order has not yet been issued but which the project team expects to formally process. Whether to include anticipated change orders in the forecast is a judgment call: most cost consultants include them so that the forecasted final cost reflects management's best understanding of the project's true exposure.

**Column 4 — Forecasted Final Cost (Estimate at Completion, or EAC).** This is the column that matters most. It represents the project team's current best estimate of what each line item will cost at final close-out, taking into account the original budget, all approved and pending change orders, all known reforecasts of trade costs, and all expected contingency drawdowns. The total of Column 4 across all line items is the **forecasted final cost** of the project — the number the lender, the investors, and the development manager all care about most.

**Column 5 — Actual Costs to Date.** This is the cumulative dollar amount that has been billed and paid (or certified for payment) on each line item to date. It is sometimes split between "billed and paid" and "billed but unpaid" to reconcile against the developer's accounts payable. Actual Costs to Date is the basis on which the lender's draw advances are calculated.

**Column 6 — Cost to Complete (CTC).** This is the dollar amount required to complete each line item, calculated as Forecasted Final Cost (Column 4) minus Actual Costs to Date (Column 5). The CTC column is critical because it is the basis of the project's funding adequacy analysis: total CTC across all line items must be less than or equal to the remaining unfunded budget for the project to be considered "in balance" by the lender.

**Column 7 — Variance.** Variance is calculated as Forecasted Final Cost (Column 4) minus Original Budget (Column 1). It is the cleanest single measure of how much each line item has moved against its starting point. The variance column is often shown both in dollar terms and as a percentage, with the percentage being the primary signal for escalation decisions.

A complete PCR will include these seven columns at the line-item level, organized by cost category — typically following the developer's own internal budget structure, which often parallels the project loan budget structure prescribed by the lender.

### Cost Categories Within the PCR

The PCR's line items are organized into cost categories that reflect the structure of the development budget itself. Three high-level categories are universal across all real estate development projects: hard costs, soft costs, and contingency. A fourth category — the land cost — is typically reported separately because it is fixed at land closing and does not vary during construction.

**Hard costs** are the costs of physical construction: site work, structure, envelope, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, finishes, and the contractor's general conditions and fee. Hard costs are typically organized using one of two systems:

- **MasterFormat (CSI Divisions)** — the construction industry's traditional 50-division taxonomy, with major divisions such as Division 03 (Concrete), Division 05 (Metals), Division 23 (HVAC), and Division 26 (Electrical). MasterFormat is the dominant organizing system for trade contracts, specifications, and shop drawings.

- **UniFormat** — an alternative taxonomy organized by building element rather than trade discipline (Substructure, Shell, Interiors, Services, Equipment & Furnishings, Special Construction). UniFormat is more common in conceptual estimating and for owner-side cost control because it maps better to design decisions.

In Ontario residential high-rise practice, MasterFormat is the more common organizing structure for the construction-phase PCR because it aligns directly with how trade contracts and tender packages are structured. The cost consultant typically issues monthly cost reports using MasterFormat divisions, and the developer's own PCR mirrors that structure for internal consistency.

**Soft costs** are all costs that are not direct construction work. They include: professional fees (architect, structural engineer, mechanical engineer, civil engineer, landscape architect, cost consultant, planner, lawyer, accountant), municipal fees and levies (development charges, parkland dedication, building permit fees, planning application fees, education development charges), consultant fees (geotechnical, environmental, traffic, acoustic, wind, energy), construction-period financing costs (loan interest, lender fees, project monitor fees, holdback fees), insurance (course of construction insurance, liability insurance, performance bonds), marketing and sales costs (presentation centre, marketing materials, sales commissions, legal fees on agreements of purchase and sale), and developer/owner expenses (corporate overhead allocation, project management staff, contingency for unidentified soft costs).

**Contingency** is reserved budget held to absorb unforeseen costs that materialize during construction. It is reported in the PCR as a separate line, but its role is to fund variances elsewhere in the budget. Contingency management is treated in detail in Section 9.4 of this chapter.

### The PCR as a Communication Document

It is worth noting that the PCR serves multiple audiences with different needs, and its structure must accommodate all of them.

For the **construction project manager**, the PCR is a tool for managing trades and identifying problem areas — they will scan the variance column and Cost to Complete column to flag where action is required.

For the **development manager and Senior Analyst**, the PCR is the basis of monthly investor reporting and lender reporting — they will focus on forecasted final cost, contingency status, and the trend of variances over time.

For the **lender's project monitor**, the PCR is the document that justifies each draw advance — they will reconcile the actual costs column against the certified percentage of completion in the architect's certificate for payment, and they will verify that the project remains in balance based on the cost-to-complete analysis.

For the **investors**, the PCR (or rather, a summary of it presented in the monthly investor report) is the lens through which they assess whether the project is on track to deliver the returns underwritten in the original investment memorandum.

A PCR that is structured to serve only one of these audiences will fail the others. The discipline of producing a PCR that works for all four is a core skill of construction-phase budget management.

## 9.3 Buyout — How the Construction Budget Gets Locked In

<div id="ol-ch9-2"></div>

Before the construction budget exists in any meaningful, defended form, it has to be **bought out**. Buyout is the process by which the developer (or the construction manager acting as the developer's agent) takes the pre-construction budget — which is built largely on cost consultant estimates and benchmark pricing — and converts it into a budget that is grounded in signed trade contracts at agreed prices.

Buyout is the single most consequential phase of budget management on any development project. Decisions made during buyout determine, more than any other single factor, whether the project will complete on budget.

### The Mechanics of Buyout

Buyout typically begins immediately after the construction contract has been awarded — whether a CCDC 2 stipulated price contract, a CCDC 5A or 5B construction management contract, or another delivery model. The construction manager or general contractor takes the project's tender packages (typically organized by MasterFormat division) and proceeds to issue, receive, evaluate, and award trade contracts one division at a time.

For each trade package, the buyout sequence is:

**Step 1 — Tender release.** The trade package — drawings, specifications, scope summary, schedule expectations, and form of contract — is issued to a bidder list. The bidder list is typically pre-qualified by the construction manager and approved by the developer.

**Step 2 — Tender period.** Bidders prepare their tenders over a period of typically two to four weeks, depending on trade complexity. During this period, addenda are issued to clarify drawings or scope changes, and bidders may submit requests for information.

**Step 3 — Tender opening and review.** Tenders are submitted by the closing deadline, opened, and reviewed by the construction manager and the cost consultant. Tenders are evaluated for compliance with the tender requirements, completeness of scope, and reasonableness of pricing.

**Step 4 — Levelling.** The construction manager prepares a tender comparison sheet — sometimes called a "tender summary" or "bid levelling" — that puts all bidders on a like-for-like basis. Levelling is necessary because bidders often interpret scope differently, include or exclude different items, propose different alternates, or carry different assumptions about coordination with adjacent trades. The levelled tender comparison shows the true delta between bidders after these inconsistencies are normalized.

**Step 5 — Recommendation.** The construction manager makes a recommendation to the developer for award, typically based on lowest compliant bid but with consideration for trade contractor capacity, schedule alignment, prior relationship, and reference checks.

**Step 6 — Award.** The developer (or the construction manager as the developer's agent, depending on the delivery model) issues a letter of intent or contract to the selected trade contractor, locking in the trade price.

The aggregate result of buying out all trade packages is the **bought-out budget** — the construction budget as supported by signed trade contracts. The bought-out budget is the budget that the construction phase actually operates against.

### The Buyout Variance

The most consequential outcome of buyout is the **buyout variance** — the dollar amount by which the bought-out budget differs from the pre-construction budget that was used to underwrite the construction loan and present the project to investors.

The buyout variance can go either direction. In a soft tender market, with high trade availability and competitive pricing, the buyout variance can be favourable — trades come in below the cost consultant's estimates, and the developer has surplus to either retire to contingency, return to investors, or apply against other budget pressures. In a tight tender market, with capacity constraints and rising material prices, the buyout variance can be adverse — trades come in well above the estimates, and the developer must either fund the gap from contingency, restructure the deal, or value-engineer the design to bring it back within budget.

In Ontario high-rise residential practice, buyout variances of plus or minus 5% on individual trade packages are routine. Buyout variances on the total construction budget of plus or minus 2% are typical in stable markets. Buyout variances exceeding 5% in either direction tend to occur in disrupted markets — periods of supply chain stress, rapid commodity price movement, labour shortages, or shifts in the regulatory environment.

The discipline of buyout, from the developer's perspective, is to manage this variance actively. That means:

- Releasing trade packages on a schedule that allows time for re-tendering if initial pricing is unfavourable
- Maintaining alternate scope options (substitute materials, simplified details) that the construction manager can deploy to bring a high-priced trade back to budget
- Tracking buyout progress against the bought-out budget weekly, not monthly, during the buyout phase
- Reporting buyout variance to investors transparently — buyout variance is the first major financial event of the construction phase and investor communication around it sets the tone for the rest of the project

### The Buyout Window in the Monthly Cycle

It is worth distinguishing between two phases of buyout in the project timeline.

**Pre-construction buyout** occurs after contract award but before construction starts. Most major trades are bought out during this window — the foundation contractor, the structural concrete contractor, the formwork contractor, the structural steel contractor, the building envelope contractor, and the major mechanical and electrical contractors.

**Phased buyout** continues into the construction period. Finishing trades, specialty contractors, and some long-lead-item suppliers are typically bought out closer to the time their work is required on site. This is partly a logistical reality (some trade scopes cannot be defined precisely until coordination drawings have been finalized) and partly a tactical one (the developer may want to wait for market conditions to soften before tendering certain trades).

For the Senior Analyst, the practical implication is that the construction budget is not fully locked at construction commencement. Variance against the original construction budget will continue to evolve through the first six to nine months of the construction phase as phased buyout completes, and the cost report must accurately reflect both bought-out trade contracts and not-yet-bought-out trade allowances.

## 9.4 Contingency — Sizing, Drawdown, and Discipline

<div id="ol-ch9-3"></div>

Contingency is the single most strategically important number in the construction budget. It is the buffer that absorbs the variances, the changes, the unknowns, and the surprises that every construction project produces. A project with too little contingency is one or two trade overruns away from a capital call. A project with too much contingency has tied up equity that could have been deployed elsewhere or returned to investors. Setting contingency correctly, and managing it through construction, is one of the highest-leverage skills in development finance.

### Owner's Contingency vs. Construction Contingency

Most well-run development projects carry two distinct contingencies, held separately in the budget and managed by different parties.

**Construction (or contractor's) contingency** is held within the construction contract. It is owned and controlled by the construction manager or general contractor and is used to fund cost overruns within the contractor's defined scope of responsibility — coordination errors between trades, minor scope omissions in tender packages, unforeseen site conditions that fall within the contractor's risk envelope, and similar. The construction contingency is typically 2–4% of the construction contract value on Ontario residential high-rise projects.

**Owner's contingency** (sometimes called "developer's contingency" or "project contingency") is held within the developer's project budget, separate from the construction contract. It is owned and controlled by the developer and is used to fund cost increases that are outside the contractor's scope — owner-directed scope changes, design changes initiated by the developer or required by regulatory authority, soft cost overruns, market-driven price escalation on phased buyouts, and force majeure events. The owner's contingency is typically 5–8% of the construction budget on standard projects, scaling higher (10–15%) on more complex projects (heritage retention, contaminated sites, complex urban infill, high-rise above existing structures).

The distinction between these two contingencies matters because the rules for accessing them are different. Construction contingency is drawn down through the contractor's internal processes (it does not appear in the developer's PCR as a separate line, though it may appear in the contractor's cost reporting). Owner's contingency is drawn down through the developer's change order process and is reported as a discrete movement on the developer's PCR.

### Sizing Contingency at Project Start

Setting the right contingency at construction start is a function of project complexity, market conditions, and the level of design completeness at construction start.

**Project complexity** is the primary driver. Standard construction on a clean site, with conventional building systems, in a stable regulatory environment, can be supported by a 5% owner's contingency. Projects with complexity factors — heritage facade retention, deep excavation in urban areas, contaminated soil, adjacent structures requiring underpinning, complex curtain wall systems, structural steel in irregular geometries, mass timber construction, district energy connections, transit oriented development with shared infrastructure — generally require contingencies of 7–10% or higher.

**Market conditions** affect contingency sizing in two ways. First, in a tight or escalating market, raw material and labour prices may move against the project during construction, which the contractor's contingency cannot fully absorb. Second, in a tight market, trade contractor failures and walk-aways become more common, and the cost of replacing a failed trade is typically far higher than the bought-out price. Both effects warrant a higher contingency.

**Design completeness at construction start** is the third major driver. A project that begins construction with permit drawings only — no working drawings, no shop drawings, no resolved coordination — will encounter many more changes during construction than a project that begins construction with fully resolved working drawings and a complete shop drawing log. Fast-tracked projects, where construction begins in parallel with later-stage design work, generally require larger contingencies to absorb the design-related changes that occur during construction.

The lender's project monitor will independently assess the contingency at the front end of construction and will report to the lender if it is inadequate. A project with insufficient contingency will sometimes be required to either reduce other budget lines (which is rarely possible at construction start), inject additional equity, or restructure the loan.

### The Contingency Drawdown Curve

Contingency is not drawn down evenly across the construction period. It follows a distinctive curve that experienced developers and project monitors recognize.

In the early months of construction — the foundation and structural phases — contingency drawdown is typically low. The work is repetitive, the trades are typically among the most established, and the changes that arise are usually small. A common pattern is for 10–15% of the total contingency to be drawn down during the first 25% of the construction period.

In the middle period — when mechanical and electrical rough-ins, building envelope, and interior finishes are being executed in parallel — contingency drawdown accelerates. Coordination issues between trades surface, design clarifications proliferate, and owner-directed scope changes (often from the marketing team responding to sales feedback or from the operations team refining the building program) begin to appear in volume. A typical pattern is 50–60% of total contingency being drawn down during the middle 50% of construction.

In the final period — finishes, commissioning, and closeout — contingency drawdown can again be high if the project has experienced delays or if late-stage scope changes are being absorbed. Final-period contingency drawdown is dominated by punch-list items, deficiency rectification costs, late commissioning issues, and closeout claims from trades. It is also the period in which contingency surplus, if any exists, becomes visible — but historically, projects that arrive at the final 25% with surplus contingency very often see that surplus consumed during the final stretch.

The lender's project monitor will track contingency drawdown monthly against the construction percent complete. A project that has consumed 60% of its contingency at 30% construction complete is on a trajectory to run out of contingency before completion. The project monitor will flag this trend to the lender, and the lender will require either a recovery plan, an injection of additional equity, or a restructuring of the budget.

### The Discipline of Contingency Use

Beyond the mechanics of contingency sizing and tracking, the most important aspect of contingency management is the **discipline of when to use it and when not to**.

Contingency exists to absorb unforeseen costs. It does not exist to absorb scope creep, design indecision, or budget pressure that the project team has simply not addressed through proper change order management.

Examples of legitimate contingency use:
- An unforeseen subsurface condition discovered during excavation that requires additional shoring
- A code interpretation change by the building department mid-construction that requires a system upgrade
- A trade contractor failure that requires replacement at higher cost
- A material price escalation between buyout and installation on a long-lead trade
- A delay to a critical-path activity caused by weather, force majeure, or lender-driven delay

Examples of contingency misuse:
- Funding owner-directed scope changes that should be reported as separate budget movements
- Absorbing cost growth on a trade that was knowingly under-priced at tender (the project team should have flagged the under-pricing at buyout)
- Funding professional fees that exceeded their original allowance through scope creep that was never re-contracted
- Funding marketing or sales costs that were not included in the original budget
- Bridging budget timing issues by pulling forward contingency draws to fund non-contingency line items

The cost consultant and the project monitor will both scrutinize each contingency drawdown for whether it represents legitimate use. Contingency drawdowns that look like budget pressure being absorbed silently are flagged to the lender, who will in turn raise the issue with the developer. The developer's investor relations function relies on the integrity of the contingency report — investors will tolerate adverse buyout variance reported transparently far more readily than they will tolerate discovering that contingency was used to mask scope creep.

For the Senior Analyst, the practical discipline is: when reviewing the PCR each month, look at every contingency drawdown line and confirm that the documented reason for the drawdown matches the legitimate-use framework above. When a drawdown does not match — when the explanation is vague, or when the drawdown appears to be funding ordinary scope work rather than a true unforeseen cost — that is a signal to escalate the issue to the development manager before the cost report is finalized.

## 9.5 Variance Analysis — Methodology and Thresholds

<div id="ol-ch9-4"></div>

Variance analysis is the discipline of comparing actual or forecasted costs against the original budget, identifying where significant differences exist, understanding why those differences exist, and determining what action is required.

### The Variance Calculation

At its simplest, variance is calculated as Forecasted Final Cost minus Original Budget, expressed as a dollar amount and as a percentage. A positive variance indicates a cost overrun; a negative variance indicates a cost saving.

The variance can be calculated at several levels of aggregation:

**Project-level variance** is the variance at the total project budget level. It is the headline number in the monthly investor report and the lender summary. A project-level variance within the contingency envelope is generally not a concern. A project-level variance that exceeds the contingency envelope is a flag for capital call discussion.

**Cost category-level variance** is the variance within each major cost category (hard costs, soft costs, financing costs). Category-level variance allows the project team to see whether budget pressure is concentrated (for example, in mechanical and electrical) or distributed across the project.

**Line item-level variance** is the variance on individual line items — specific trade contracts, specific consultant retainers, specific permit fees. Line-item variance is the working level of cost control. It is what the project manager addresses day-to-day and what the cost consultant focuses on in their monthly report.

### Variance Thresholds and Escalation

Most well-run development projects establish variance thresholds that determine the level of escalation required.

A typical threshold structure on a $100M construction budget might be:

| Variance level | Threshold | Escalation |
|---|---|---|
| Routine | < 1% of line item | Project coordinator note in monthly cost report |
| Notable | 1% to 3% of line item | Project manager analysis included in monthly cost report |
| Significant | 3% to 5% of line item | Development manager review and written justification |
| Material | > 5% of line item, or > $250K aggregate | Investor notification, lender notification |
| Critical | Cumulative project variance approaching contingency | Lender discussion, possible budget restructuring |

The thresholds should be calibrated to the size of the project. A 5% variance on a $50,000 consultant fee is $2,500 — barely worth a project coordinator's time. A 5% variance on a $5M structural concrete contract is $250,000 — worth the development manager's full attention.

The lender will typically have its own variance reporting threshold built into the loan agreement. A common formulation is that any single change order exceeding $250,000 or 5% of the original budget for the affected line item must be reported to the lender within five business days of its execution. The Senior Analyst's monthly cost report and draw package serve as the formal channel for this reporting.

### Categorizing Variance Causes

A useful discipline in variance analysis is categorizing the cause of each variance, both for internal use and for lender reporting. A common categorization scheme is:

**Buyout variance** — variance arising from trade tender results coming in different from the cost consultant's pre-tender estimate. Buyout variance is concentrated in the early months of construction.

**Scope change variance** — variance arising from owner-directed or regulator-required changes to the project scope. Scope change variance accumulates throughout construction, with peak periods typically tied to design clarification milestones and to marketing/sales-driven design refinement.

**Site condition variance** — variance arising from unforeseen physical conditions on site (subsurface conditions, existing structure conditions, contaminated soil, archaeological discoveries). Site condition variance is concentrated in the early excavation and foundation phases.

**Market price variance** — variance arising from changes in market prices for materials and labour between budget setting and trade buyout, or between buyout and material delivery on long-lead items. Market price variance can be concentrated in any phase, depending on which trades are exposed to which materials.

**Schedule-related variance** — variance arising from extension of the construction period, primarily through extended general conditions, prolongation costs, escalation on remaining work, and increased financing carrying costs. Schedule-related variance compounds over time.

**Design coordination variance** — variance arising from coordination issues between consultants that surface during construction (typically resolved through RFIs and CCNs that translate into change orders). Design coordination variance is often concentrated in the period when MEP rough-ins are being installed against structural and architectural work.

**Quality / deficiency variance** — variance arising from rectification work on identified deficiencies, premature equipment failures, warranty work prior to substantial performance, or trade rework. Quality variance accumulates through the project but is concentrated in the closeout phase.

Each variance category has different implications for who is responsible, how it should be reported, whether it is a candidate for contingency drawdown or a separate budget movement, and what process improvements (if any) the project team should consider.

### The Trend View

A single month's variance is a snapshot. A trend across months is an indication of the project's underlying financial health.

The most important trend lines to monitor are:

- **Forecasted final cost vs. construction budget over time** — is the project trending upward over consecutive months? An upward trend that is not arrested by mid-construction is a leading indicator of budget failure.
- **Contingency drawdown vs. construction percent complete** — is contingency being consumed faster than the work is being executed?
- **Pending change orders vs. approved change orders over time** — a growing pending change order log indicates a backlog that will eventually convert to approved CO volume.
- **Cost-to-complete vs. remaining unfunded budget** — is the project remaining "in balance" from the lender's perspective?

The monthly investor report and the monthly lender draw package are the formal venues for presenting these trends, but the Senior Analyst should be tracking them continuously and flagging adverse trends to the development manager in real time, not at month-end.

## 9.6 Cost-to-Complete and the In-Balance Test

<div id="ol-ch9-5"></div>

The cost-to-complete (CTC) calculation is the operational heart of construction-phase cost control. It is the number that determines whether the project has enough remaining funding to finish — and it is the number that the lender's project monitor scrutinizes most closely each month.

### Calculating Cost-to-Complete

For each line item in the PCR, cost-to-complete is calculated as:

> **CTC = Forecasted Final Cost − Actual Costs to Date**

For trade contracts, this calculation is normally straightforward: actual costs to date are the amounts billed and certified, and the cost-to-complete is the remainder of the contract value (plus any approved pending change orders not yet billed) that the trade has yet to invoice.

For soft costs, CTC requires more judgment. A consultant fee budget might be expressed as a fixed-fee contract with a defined remaining balance, or as an hourly fee allocation against an open scope, in which case the CTC is the project team's best estimate of remaining hours times the rate.

For contingency, CTC is the unallocated balance — total contingency minus what has been drawn down to date.

The total CTC for the project is the sum of CTCs across all line items. It represents the total dollar amount required to complete the project from today forward.

### The In-Balance Test

The in-balance test compares total CTC to the **remaining unfunded budget** — the portion of the loan and equity capital stack that has not yet been advanced or drawn.

> **Project is in balance when: Remaining Unfunded Budget ≥ Total CTC**

When total CTC exceeds the remaining unfunded budget, the project is **out of balance**. Being out of balance means that, on the project's current trajectory, the available funding will be insufficient to complete the project. Out-of-balance projects must be brought back into balance through one or more interventions:

- Injection of additional equity by the developer or investors
- Increase in the construction loan facility (subject to lender appetite and underwriting)
- Reduction in scope (descoping items that are not strictly required)
- Acceleration of revenue (typically through increased pre-sales velocity if the project is condo)
- Cost reduction through value engineering of remaining scope

Lenders will not advance further draws on an out-of-balance project until it has been brought back into balance. This is one of the most consequential tests in construction-phase budget management — failing the in-balance test stops the project's cash flow.

### How Lenders Conduct the In-Balance Test

The lender's project monitor reviews the in-balance test monthly as part of the draw package review. The project monitor's calculation typically includes:

- **CTC from the developer's PCR**, which the project monitor will review and may adjust if it considers the developer's forecast optimistic
- **An additional allowance for "soft cost overrun risk"** — many project monitors carry a small additional cost-to-complete reserve in their independent calculation to account for unidentified soft cost pressure
- **Holdback considerations** — the project monitor will track whether the holdback account has accumulated correctly and whether the holdback release mechanics align with the schedule

If the project monitor's calculation differs materially from the developer's, the project monitor will prepare a written report identifying the difference and its rationale. The lender will then engage the developer to reconcile the two views before approving the draw.

### When Cost-to-Complete Triggers a Capital Call

A capital call is the formal request from the developer to the project's investors to contribute additional equity to the project. Capital calls are an inevitable feature of certain projects, and they are managed through provisions in the partnership agreement that defines the equity structure.

Capital calls arise from cost-to-complete failures most often at the following inflection points:

- **The buyout reconciliation** — if buyout produces a material adverse variance that exceeds the contingency, the project requires recapitalization at the start of construction.
- **Mid-construction cost reforecast** — if a major trade fails (the typical example is a structural concrete contractor walking off site at 60% completion), the cost-to-complete on that trade can increase by 30–50% versus the bought-out price, often exceeding remaining contingency.
- **Schedule extension events** — a delay of three to six months in the construction schedule can produce extended general conditions, escalation, and prolonged interest costs that together exhaust contingency.
- **Force majeure or external events** — pandemic-related delays, supply chain disruptions, severe weather events, and labour disruptions can produce non-contingency-eligible cost growth.

The Senior Analyst's role in a capital call situation is typically to prepare the supporting analysis — the revised cost-to-complete calculation, the revised forecast of final cost, the revised loan-to-cost analysis, and the proposed call schedule. The development manager and the developer's senior leadership manage the actual investor communication.

## 9.7 The Loan Budget and the Loan-to-Cost (LTC) Reconciliation

<div id="ol-ch9-6"></div>

The construction loan, introduced in detail in Chapter 3, is the primary source of funding for the construction phase. The relationship between the project budget and the loan facility is governed by a set of mechanics that the Senior Analyst must understand precisely to manage the monthly draw cycle correctly.

### The Loan Budget vs. the Project Budget

The **loan budget** is the budget against which the lender has agreed to advance funds. It is part of the construction loan agreement, and it identifies the line-item budget that the lender has underwritten. The loan budget will typically mirror the project's construction budget at the time of loan closing, but it is governed by specific provisions in the loan agreement that may differ from how the developer manages its internal budget.

For example:

- The loan budget may exclude certain soft cost items that the lender does not consider eligible for loan funding (corporate overhead allocations are a common example)
- The loan budget may include a "lender's contingency" that is separate from the developer's contingency and is held back from drawdown for a period
- The loan budget may have line-item caps that prevent overdrawing on any single line, even if other lines are underdrawn
- The loan budget will typically have an interest reserve line that funds construction-period interest until project completion
- The loan budget will have a "rebalancing" provision that requires the lender's approval before reallocating budget between line items

The **project budget** is the budget that the developer manages internally and reports to investors. It typically includes everything in the loan budget plus items the lender does not fund (corporate overhead, certain promotional and marketing expenses, certain pre-development costs that were funded from equity).

The Senior Analyst must reconcile these two budgets monthly. Failing to do so leads to draw rejections at the lender level and, more seriously, can mask budget pressure that is real at the project level but invisible at the loan level (or vice versa).

### Loan-to-Cost (LTC) — Definition and Tracking

Loan-to-Cost is the ratio of the loan amount to the total project cost. It is a critical metric throughout the construction phase because it is one of the lender's primary risk monitoring tools.

**LTC at loan closing** is set by the loan underwriting and is reflected in the loan agreement. A typical Ontario residential high-rise project might have an LTC at closing of 65–75%, with the remainder funded by equity (and, on some projects, by mezzanine debt).

**LTC during construction** is calculated each month as the cumulative loan advances divided by the cumulative project costs. As construction progresses, both numerator and denominator increase, and the LTC ratio should remain at or below the closing LTC ratio. If the project goes over budget, the LTC ratio increases (the loan amount stays roughly constant while the project cost goes up), and the lender's risk position deteriorates.

**LTC at completion** is the final ratio — total loan advanced divided by total project cost at completion. This is the figure that the takeout lender (the long-term lender, if applicable, or the equity buyer if the project is being sold) will assess.

The Senior Analyst tracks LTC each month as part of the draw package and as part of the internal budget management discipline. A rising LTC trend through construction is one of the most important leading indicators of project distress.

### The Equity-First Funding Convention

Most construction loans are structured with an "equity-first" funding convention. Under this convention, the developer is required to fund the project's costs from equity until a defined equity contribution has been made before the lender begins advancing loan funds.

For example, on a $100M project with a 70% LTC loan ($70M loan, $30M equity), the lender may require that the full $30M of equity be funded before the first loan advance is made. This places the equity at the highest-risk position in the capital stack — if the project fails, the equity is consumed first, and the lender's recovery comes from the loan position which is funded later in the project.

A "pari passu" or "ratable" convention, by contrast, allows the developer to draw equity and loan in proportion throughout construction, maintaining the LTC ratio at the closing level. Pari passu funding is less common on construction loans because it places less of the early-stage risk on the equity, but it can be negotiated on stronger sponsor profiles.

The Senior Analyst's monthly draw package will explicitly track the equity contribution against the loan advance and will demonstrate compliance with the funding convention specified in the loan agreement. Misrepresenting this compliance — even unintentionally — can trigger a default under the loan agreement.

### Rebalancing the Loan Budget

Over the course of construction, line-item variances in the project budget will create pressure to reallocate funding between lines in the loan budget. The loan agreement will specify the rebalancing provisions — what is permitted without lender consent, what requires lender consent, and what is prohibited.

A typical provision might allow the developer to reallocate up to 5% of any single line to another line without lender consent, provided that:
- The reallocation does not increase the total loan budget
- The reallocation does not draw down contingency below a defined threshold
- The reallocation does not affect the interest reserve
- The line item being decreased is one that has been demonstrated to be in surplus (typically through trade buyout savings or scope reduction)

Rebalancing requests above the threshold typically require formal approval from the lender's credit committee. Rebalancing into the contingency line, or out of the contingency line into other lines, is typically subject to specific scrutiny — the lender does not want the contingency reduced because that increases the lender's risk on the remainder of the project.

The Senior Analyst's role in rebalancing is to prepare the supporting analysis — what variances are driving the rebalancing request, what the post-rebalancing budget structure looks like, and what the implications are for the in-balance test. The development manager typically manages the conversation with the lender directly.

## 9.8 Interest Reserve Management

<div id="ol-ch9-7"></div>

The interest reserve is one of the most consequential and frequently mismanaged components of the construction budget. It is the line item within the loan budget that funds construction-period interest payments on the construction loan. Its size, drawdown rate, and adequacy through the construction period are central to whether the project will carry itself to completion.

### What the Interest Reserve Funds

During the construction period, the project does not generate income (residential condo projects do not collect occupancy proceeds until first occupancy; rental and commercial projects do not collect rent until lease-up after substantial completion). The construction loan accrues interest, and that interest must be paid monthly. The interest reserve is the budgeted dollar amount, funded as part of the loan facility, that pays this interest until project completion.

The interest reserve is typically calculated at loan closing using the following inputs:

- **Loan facility amount** — the total loan facility, including subsequent advances
- **Drawdown curve** — the projected schedule of monthly loan advances over the construction period (usually an S-curve, with low draws at the start, peak draws in the middle, and tapering at the end)
- **Interest rate** — the loan's current interest rate, with a stress factor for potential rate movement
- **Construction period** — the projected number of months from loan closing to substantial completion
- **Buffer** — an additional buffer for delay risk, typically 2–4 months

The product of these inputs is the projected interest accrual over the construction period, which becomes the interest reserve line in the loan budget.

### Interest Reserve Drawdown Mechanics

Each month, the lender computes the interest accrued on the outstanding loan balance during that month. The accrued interest is paid by drawing the corresponding amount from the interest reserve and applying it to interest expense. The interest reserve balance decreases by that amount.

The interest reserve, like any other loan budget line, has a finite balance. If the interest accrued each month exceeds the rate at which the original interest reserve was sized, the reserve will be exhausted before construction completes — a condition known as "running out of interest reserve."

Three factors most commonly cause interest reserves to run short:

**Construction delay.** The interest reserve was sized for a specific construction period. If construction extends beyond that period, monthly interest continues to accrue, but the reserve does not increase. A six-month delay can consume 5–10% of the original interest reserve depending on the loan balance at that point.

**Loan balance higher than projected.** If draws are advanced earlier than projected, the average outstanding loan balance over the construction period is higher, and accrued interest is correspondingly higher. Front-loaded buyouts, accelerated trade payments, or change orders advanced quickly can all produce this effect.

**Rate movement.** The interest reserve was calculated at a specific rate or rate-plus-stress. If actual rates exceed the stress assumption — for example, in a rapidly rising rate environment — interest accrual will exceed the budget, even with the loan balance and schedule on plan.

In the post-2022 rate environment, interest reserve management has moved from a back-office concern to a front-line risk monitoring function on most projects. The Senior Analyst is typically the person who tracks the reserve balance against the projected drawdown and flags when the reserve is trending below the stress case.

### When the Interest Reserve Runs Short

When the interest reserve runs short of what is required to carry the project to substantial completion, the developer has limited options:

- **Increase the loan facility** — if the lender has appetite, the loan can be increased to fund additional interest. This is the most common solution but is dependent on remaining loan-to-cost capacity and lender willingness.
- **Inject additional equity** — the developer or investors fund the interest shortfall directly. This typically triggers capital call provisions in the partnership agreement.
- **Accelerate revenue** — for condo projects, acceleration of pre-sales or release of additional inventory can fund interest costs (pre-sale deposits cannot generally be drawn for interest payments, but they reduce the project's net funding requirement).
- **Restructure the loan** — extension of the construction loan or conversion to interest-only mini-perm arrangements. This is typically only available for projects close to completion.

The earliest possible identification of an interest reserve shortfall is critical because the remediation options narrow as the project progresses. A shortfall identified at 30% completion has many remediation paths; a shortfall identified at 90% completion may only have a capital call available.

### Interest Reserve in the Monthly Cost Report

The interest reserve appears in the project cost report as a soft cost line item. The Senior Analyst's monthly cost report should track:

- **Original interest reserve balance** — the line item value at loan closing
- **Interest accrued and applied to date** — cumulative monthly interest charges paid from the reserve
- **Remaining reserve balance** — the unfunded amount available
- **Forecasted interest from today to substantial completion** — based on the current loan balance projection, current rate, and current schedule
- **Reserve adequacy** — the difference between remaining reserve and forecasted interest, with the sign indicating whether the project is overfunded or underfunded on interest

A negative reserve adequacy figure is the formal trigger for escalation. Best practice is to flag reserve adequacy that is approaching neutral (within $250K, for example) so that remediation conversations can begin before the reserve is genuinely exhausted.

## 9.9 Cost Reporting Cadence and Outputs

<div id="ol-ch9-8"></div>

Cost reporting on a construction project is not a single document. It is a coordinated set of reports produced on different cadences for different audiences, each derived from the same underlying cost data but framed for different purposes.

### Monthly Cost Report (Cost Consultant)

The cost consultant — typically an external quantity surveyor or independent cost manager — produces the monthly cost report on a fixed cadence aligned with the draw cycle. The cost consultant's report is the formal third-party verified cost statement and serves as the basis for the lender's project monitor review.

The cost consultant's monthly cost report typically includes:

- The PCR (in the column architecture described in Section 9.2)
- A narrative summary of the month's cost movements
- The change order log with status of each change order
- The contingency drawdown log with explanations
- The schedule update with cost implications
- A trend section showing forecasted final cost over the past several months
- The cost-to-complete analysis and in-balance assessment

The cost consultant's report is signed and certified by the cost consultant — they are putting their professional reputation behind the numbers. As a result, the cost consultant's report is treated as the most authoritative cost statement in the project.

### Monthly Draw Package (Senior Analyst / Project Manager)

The monthly draw package is the document that the developer submits to the lender to request the next loan advance. It is built around the cost consultant's report but adds operational documentation that the lender requires to release the draw — described in detail in Chapter 8.

The Senior Analyst is typically the person who assembles the draw package. The Senior Analyst's discipline is to ensure that:

- The architect's certificate for payment matches the cost consultant's certified amount
- The schedule of values billing is internally consistent
- All change orders included in the draw have been formally approved
- The statutory declarations are current and complete
- The insurance certificates are current and the insured amount is adequate
- All soft cost invoices included in the draw are properly approved
- The draw amount calculated reconciles to the cost consultant's certified amount, less holdback, less any prior draw adjustments

A draw package with internal inconsistencies is rejected at the project monitor stage and must be reissued. This delays the draw advance, delays trade payments, and can produce knock-on effects in the schedule. Discipline at the draw package assembly stage is the most concrete way the Senior Analyst protects project liquidity.

### Monthly Investor Report (Development Manager / Senior Analyst)

The monthly investor report communicates the project's status — including financial status — to the equity investors. It is generally produced by the development manager in conjunction with the Senior Analyst, drawing on the same underlying cost data but framed for an investor audience.

The investor report's cost section typically includes:

- A summary of the current forecasted final cost vs. the underwritten budget
- A summary of approved and pending change orders for the month
- A contingency status summary
- A schedule status summary with cost implications
- A summary of any material variances from prior forecasts and the explanation
- A reaffirmation or revision of the project's projected returns to investors (only when the cost movement is material enough to warrant a return revision)

Investor reports require careful framing — they must be transparent about cost variances and challenges without being unnecessarily alarming. The Senior Analyst's role here is to ensure the underlying numbers are accurate; the development manager's role is to set the tone and framing for the investor audience.

### Quarterly Lender Reporting (Senior Analyst / Development Manager)

In addition to the monthly draw cycle, lenders typically require quarterly reporting that goes beyond the standard draw package. Quarterly reports cover:

- A formal forecast of final project cost
- A reaffirmation of the in-balance position
- A detailed schedule analysis including critical path status
- A market update that affects the project's revenue assumptions (presale velocity, pricing, lease-up status)
- A summary of any partner or trade contractor distress events
- A cash flow forecast through substantial completion

The quarterly lender report is the formal forum in which the developer revises the project's underwritten parameters with the lender. Material adverse variances reported at quarterly review may trigger covenant testing under the loan agreement.

### Annual or Project-End Reconciliation

At the end of the construction phase, when the project has reached substantial performance and the major contracts have been closed out, the developer produces a final cost reconciliation that closes out the construction-phase budget management discipline.

The final reconciliation includes:

- The final actual cost on each line item
- The final variance against the original budget
- The final contingency status (typically a small surplus held as project-end reserve)
- The final loan balance against the final project cost (final LTC)
- The handover to the asset management or sales/leasing team

The final reconciliation is the basis for the developer's after-project review — the institutional learning that informs the next project's budget setting. For the Senior Analyst, the final reconciliation is also the basis for performance compensation calculations: GP promote, development manager bonus, and any contractual bonus or penalty provisions tied to budget performance.

## 9.10 Common Sources of Budget Failure (and How to Prevent Them)

<div id="ol-ch9-9"></div>

Most projects that experience budget failure trace the failure to a small set of recurring causes. Understanding these causes — and the preventive disciplines that address them — is one of the most valuable things a development professional can carry from project to project.

**1. Insufficient contingency at the start.** A project that starts construction with 4% contingency in a market environment that historically produces 7% contingency consumption is set up for failure. Preventive discipline: benchmark contingency against historical project performance for the specific project type and market, and reject pressure to "trim contingency" at deal underwriting.

**2. Soft tender market discipline collapse.** When trades come in well below budget at buyout, the project team often loses budget discipline — additional scope is added, design upgrades are approved, and the buyout savings are spent rather than retired to contingency. Preventive discipline: at the buyout reconciliation, formally allocate buyout savings to contingency or to investor distribution before any new spending decisions are made.

**3. Phased buyout deferral.** A project that defers buyout of finishing trades and specialty contractors hoping for market softening can find itself buying out into a market that has tightened, with adverse variance on those late-stage trades. Preventive discipline: do not defer buyout speculatively; buy out on the schedule that was planned at construction start unless there is a specific, documented reason to deviate.

**4. Change order backlog.** A growing pending change order log, where changes are being directed and executed but not formally priced and approved, masks budget pressure. The pending changes will eventually convert to approved cost, often at higher rates than they would have if priced contemporaneously. Preventive discipline: enforce a maximum pending CO age (typically 30 days) and report aging at the monthly cost meeting.

**5. Schedule extension without reforecast.** A schedule that has slipped by three months without a corresponding reforecast of extended general conditions, prolonged interest costs, and escalation on remaining work creates a hidden cost overrun that surfaces at closeout. Preventive discipline: every schedule update that extends substantial completion by more than 30 days triggers a mandatory reforecast of associated soft costs and financing costs.

**6. Trade contractor failure ignored.** When a trade contractor is showing distress signs — slow billing, payment disputes with their subs, deteriorating quality — the project team often hopes the situation will resolve itself rather than initiating early replacement. By the time replacement becomes unavoidable, the cost of replacement has grown substantially. Preventive discipline: monitor trade contractor health on a defined set of indicators monthly, and have replacement options pre-identified for major trades.

**7. Soft cost neglect.** Soft costs (consultants, permits, financing, insurance, marketing) often receive less attention than hard costs because they appear smaller individually. But cumulative soft cost overruns of 10–15% are common on projects that lose discipline, and they can be material to the project's bottom line. Preventive discipline: review soft costs with the same monthly variance discipline as hard costs, and require the same change order process for any soft cost commitment that exceeds its budget allowance.

**8. Failure to track loan-budget vs. project-budget separately.** A project that manages only the project budget but loses track of how the loan budget reconciles can produce draw rejections and, in extreme cases, inadvertent default events. Preventive discipline: monthly explicit reconciliation between the project budget and the loan budget, with documentation of any line items that differ.

**9. Optimistic forecasting.** Forecasted final cost figures that consistently track below the cost consultant's view, or that lag the obvious trend, are a sign that someone in the project team is not surfacing pressure honestly. Preventive discipline: cultivate and protect the discipline of "bad news travels fast" within the project team — celebrate the project manager who flags problems early, even when the news is unwelcome.

**10. Disconnect between site and cost report.** The most insidious failure is when the cost report does not reflect what is actually happening on site. The PCR shows status quo, but trades are accumulating extras that haven't been reported, or the schedule is slipping but the cost team is showing baseline. Preventive discipline: the Senior Analyst should walk the site at least monthly with the project manager and the cost consultant, and the monthly cost meeting should include the site superintendent or construction manager, not just the financial team.

## 9.11 Key Metrics and Terms — Reference Table

<div id="ol-ch9-10"></div>

The following table summarizes the metrics, ratios, and terminology introduced in this chapter for reference during quizzes, applied exercises, and on-the-job application.

| Metric / Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Project Cost Report (PCR)** | The monthly cost report, organized by line item and column architecture, that is the central operating document of construction-phase budget management. |
| **Original Budget** | The construction budget at the start of the construction phase. Does not change for the life of the project. |
| **Approved Change Orders to Date** | Cumulative dollar value of change orders formally executed and approved through the change order process. |
| **Pending Change Orders** | Change orders that have been issued and priced but not yet formally approved. |
| **Forecasted Final Cost (EAC)** | The project team's current best estimate of what each line item will cost at final close-out. |

| **MasterFormat** | Construction industry's 50-division taxonomy for organizing construction trade contracts and specifications. |
| **UniFormat** | Alternative taxonomy organized by building element rather than trade discipline. |
| **Buyout** | The process of converting the pre-construction budget into a budget grounded in signed trade contracts. |
| **Buyout Variance** | The dollar amount by which the bought-out budget differs from the pre-construction budget. |
| **Owner's Contingency** | Contingency held in the developer's project budget, controlled by the developer, used to fund cost increases outside the contractor's scope. |
| **Construction Contingency** | Contingency held within the construction contract, controlled by the contractor, used for in-scope cost overruns. |
| **In-Balance Test** | Test that compares total Cost to Complete to remaining unfunded budget. The project is "in balance" when remaining unfunded budget is greater than or equal to total CTC. |

| **Loan Budget Rebalancing** | Reallocation of funding between line items in the loan budget, typically subject to lender approval thresholds. |
| **Interest Reserve** | Loan budget line that funds construction-period interest payments. |
| **Reserve Adequacy** | Remaining interest reserve minus forecasted interest from today through substantial completion. |
| **Capital Call** | Formal request to investors for additional equity contribution to address project funding shortfall. |

## AE-9.1 — Reading the Cost Report

### Scenario

You are reviewing the cost consultant's monthly PCR for a 32-storey residential condominium project in Etobicoke. Construction is at month 14 of a projected 30-month schedule. The summary line for the structural concrete trade reads:

| Original Budget | Approved COs | Pending COs | Forecasted Final Cost | Actual to Date | Cost to Complete | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $18,400,000 | $720,000 | $310,000 | $19,540,000 | $11,200,000 | $8,340,000 | +$1,140,000 |

The structural concrete trade is approximately 60% complete based on the trade's billing. The project's owner's contingency at construction start was $5,000,000 (5% of the $100M construction budget). To date, $1,800,000 has been drawn. Remaining contingency is $3,200,000.

### Questions

1. What does the +$1,140,000 variance represent, and is it within the typical buyout variance range or a sign of mid-construction drift?
2. Including this trade's variance and its pending COs, is the remaining contingency adequate to support remaining construction risk through the back half of the schedule?
3. What follow-up actions would you take based on this line review before finalizing this month's cost report?

---

## AE-9.1 — Solution

**Question 1 — Variance interpretation**

The +$1,140,000 variance represents the aggregate movement on the structural concrete line from the original budget ($18.4M) to the forecasted final cost ($19.54M). This is a **6.2% positive variance against the original budget** — outside the typical 2–5% buyout variance range and into the territory that warrants investigation. The variance is composed of $720,000 in approved change orders plus $310,000 in pending change orders plus an additional $110,000 of reforecasted cost from other adjustments.

Because we are at month 14 and the trade is 60% complete, this is **mid-construction drift rather than buyout variance** — buyout completed in months 1–6 of the project. The pattern (multiple change orders accumulating through the structural phase, pending CO log of $310,000 still open) suggests that change order discipline on this trade may be loose, and the trade may continue to accumulate cost through its remaining 40% of work.

**Question 2 — Contingency adequacy**

The contingency status: $5,000,000 starting balance, $1,800,000 drawn to date, $3,200,000 available. The pending COs on structural concrete alone would consume an additional $310,000 if approved, leaving roughly $2,890,000.

The construction phase is at month 14 of 30, with significant remaining trades still to fully bill (mechanical, electrical, finishes, commissioning) and significant remaining variance risk. As a rough check against the contingency drawdown curve described in Section 9.4: contingency drawdown of 36% at construction percent complete of approximately 47% is broadly tracking the curve, but the trajectory of the structural concrete trade combined with the broader pattern of pending changes likely puts the project on a path to exhaust contingency before completion. The reserve adequacy needs a full project-level recalculation, not just this trade in isolation.

**Question 3 — Follow-up actions**

Before finalizing this month's cost report I would: (a) request from the project manager and cost consultant a full breakdown of the $720,000 in approved COs and the $310,000 in pending COs by category — what was scope change, what was site condition, what was design coordination; (b) review the trade's billing curve against its progress to see whether the trade is over-billing (which can mask future cost-to-complete issues); (c) review the change order ageing log for this trade to see how many of the pending COs are over 30 days old; (d) ask the cost consultant whether their forecasted final cost reflects only known issues or includes a forward-looking allowance for residual risk on the remaining 40% of the trade's work; (e) walk the site with the project manager to confirm the trade's progress is genuinely at 60% and not over-billed.

## AE-9.2 — Buyout Reconciliation

### Scenario

A 24-storey residential project is at construction commencement. The pre-construction budget for hard costs was $87,000,000, including a $4,300,000 contractor's contingency. Buyout has now completed for all major trades. The aggregate result:

| Trade | Pre-Construction Budget | Bought-Out Cost | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation and shoring | $7,200,000 | $7,650,000 | +$450,000 |
| Structural concrete and formwork | $19,500,000 | $20,800,000 | +$1,300,000 |
| Curtain wall | $13,200,000 | $14,950,000 | +$1,750,000 |
| Mechanical | $9,800,000 | $9,300,000 | −$500,000 |
| Electrical | $6,400,000 | $6,150,000 | −$250,000 |
| Other major trades (combined) | $26,600,000 | $25,950,000 | −$650,000 |
| **Total** | **$82,700,000** | **$84,800,000** | **+$2,100,000** |

The development manager has proposed that the under-budget mechanical and electrical buyouts (a combined $750,000 surplus) be redirected to upgrade the lobby finishes and the rooftop amenity, which would consume the $750,000 entirely.

### Questions

1. What is the aggregate buyout variance for these trades, in dollars and as a percentage of the budgeted total for these trades?
2. Three trades came in materially over budget, two came in materially under budget, and the residual category came in slightly under. What questions would you raise with the cost consultant before reporting this buyout result to the lender?
3. From a budget management perspective, what is your assessment of the development manager's proposal to redirect the M&E surplus to design upgrades?

---

## AE-9.2 — Solution

**Question 1 — Aggregate buyout variance**

Total budgeted: $82,700,000 (excluding the contractor's contingency). Total bought out: $84,800,000. Aggregate buyout variance: **+$2,100,000, which is +2.5% of the budgeted total**. This is within the typical 2% range but at the upper end, indicating a moderately tight tender market.

**Question 2 — Questions for the cost consultant**

Several questions before reporting to the lender:

- Why did curtain wall come in 13.3% over budget — is this a market price issue specific to glazing, a scope issue (was the budget basis incomplete?), or a design issue (did the bidders carry different assumptions about complexity)?
- Why did structural concrete come in 6.7% over — was the cost consultant's pre-tender estimate based on a market rate that has since moved?
- Are the under-budget mechanical and electrical buyouts genuinely complete buyouts, or are they bought out with scope gaps that will surface as change orders later?
- Have the bidders' financial health and capacity been verified — is there a risk that one of these aggressive bids will fail mid-construction?
- What is the contractor's contingency status now that buyout is complete — can the contractor's contingency cover the variance, or will the owner's contingency be required?

**Question 3 — Assessment of the redirect proposal**

The proposal to redirect $750,000 of buyout savings to design upgrades is a textbook example of the **"soft tender market discipline collapse"** failure mode described in Section 9.10. The aggregate buyout variance is +$2,100,000 — the project as a whole is over-budget at construction commencement, even with the under-budget mechanical and electrical results. The under-budget surpluses on M&E should be retired to contingency to absorb the over-budget pressure on structure and curtain wall, not redirected to additional scope.

From a budget management perspective, the appropriate response to the development manager's proposal is: (a) the buyout should be reported to the lender and investors at the aggregate level — over-budget by $2.1M; (b) the M&E surplus should be allocated to absorb the structural and curtain wall overrun; (c) any discussion of design upgrades should be deferred until at least month 6 of construction, when the cost trajectory is clearer.

## AE-9.3 — Interest Reserve and In-Balance Analysis

### Scenario

You are preparing the month 18 cost report for a project at approximately 65% construction complete. The original schedule called for substantial completion at month 28; the current schedule projects substantial completion at month 32 — a four-month delay against the original.

The relevant loan and budget figures are:

- Construction loan facility: $145,000,000
- Construction loan advanced to date: $98,000,000
- Original interest reserve: $11,500,000
- Interest reserve drawn to date: $7,800,000
- Remaining interest reserve: $3,700,000
- Average loan balance projected for months 19–32: $122,000,000
- Current loan rate (variable, set at Prime + 0.75%): 7.20% per annum
- Total project cost forecast at completion: $215,000,000
- Total project cost actually expended to date: $147,000,000

### Questions

1. Calculate the forecasted interest accrual from month 19 through projected substantial completion at month 32. Compare this to the remaining interest reserve. Is the reserve adequate?

## AE-9.3 — Solution

**Question 1 — Forecasted interest vs. remaining reserve**

Forecasted interest from month 19 through month 32 (14 months remaining):

> Average loan balance $122M × 7.20% × 14/12 = **$10,248,000**

Remaining interest reserve: $3,700,000. **Interest shortfall: approximately $6,548,000.**

The reserve is materially inadequate. The four-month schedule extension is the primary cause — the original $11.5M reserve was sized for a 28-month construction period; the current 32-month period requires materially more reserve. At current loan balance and rate, the reserve will be exhausted around month 23–24, well before substantial completion.

**Question 2 — LTC position**

Current LTC (advanced to date): $98M / $147M = **66.7%**. This is roughly aligned with a 70% closing LTC — actual costs have grown faster than loan advances, putting the current LTC slightly below the closing position.

Projected LTC at completion: assuming the full $145M loan is advanced and project cost lands at $215M, LTC at completion would be **67.4%**. This is within the typical 65–75% range, indicating that the loan facility is sized appropriately for the project total cost — but this does not address the interest reserve issue, which sits within the $145M loan facility.

**Question 3 — Remediation options and recommended priority**

Realistic remediation options:

- **Increase the loan facility** — likely difficult since it is already at 67% LTC and increasing it further deteriorates the lender's risk position.
- **Inject additional equity to fund the interest shortfall** — this would trigger a capital call, consume $6.5M of additional equity, and require partnership agreement provisions to be activated.
- **Accelerate revenue** — for a condo project, releasing additional units or accelerating closings could fund the gap, but requires market velocity that may or may not exist.
- **Restructure the loan** into an interest-only or extended-term arrangement — typically only available very near completion.

The recommended priority for the draw package narrative: **surface the issue immediately at month 18**, frame it as a forecasted future shortfall rather than a current crisis, and request a formal lender meeting to discuss remediation. Do not let the draw cycle proceed for another two or three months hoping the situation improves — by month 21 or 22, remediation options narrow significantly. The Senior Analyst's role is to ensure this is escalated to the development manager and to the lender at the earliest possible moment.

## Chapter Summary

Budget management and cost control are the daily operational discipline of the construction phase and the most direct application of the Senior Analyst's role on a development project. This chapter has examined that discipline at a level of detail that goes beyond the cost report introduction in Chapter 8, building out the architecture of the project cost report, the mechanics of buyout, the discipline of contingency management, the methodology of variance analysis, the cost-to-complete and in-balance tests that the lender's project monitor relies on, the loan-to-cost reconciliation that connects the project budget to the loan budget, the management of the interest reserve as a finite and fragile resource, the cadence and outputs of cost reporting across the project's audiences, and the recurring causes of budget failure on real projects.

The unifying theme across all of these topics is the same: cost is not controlled by the cost report — cost is controlled by the discipline behind the cost report. A PCR that is mechanically accurate but unaccompanied by genuine variance analysis, real conversation with the cost consultant, walked-on-site verification of progress, honest forecasting of remaining risk, and timely escalation of pressure to the development manager and the lender will not protect the project from cost failure. The Senior Analyst who builds these disciplines on the first project they manage will carry them through their career and across every project they touch.

The next chapter — **Chapter 10: Quality Control & Inspections** — moves from the financial discipline of the construction phase to the technical discipline. Quality control determines whether what is being built meets the specifications, the building code, and the conditions of warranty and insurance. As with cost control, quality control is a shared responsibility across the project team, with the Senior Analyst providing the financial overlay through deficiency cost tracking and warranty cost reserves.
