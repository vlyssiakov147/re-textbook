# Chapter 15: Disposition & Exit

**Phase:** Asset Stabilization through Portfolio Reallocation
**Prerequisite Chapters:** 3 (Financing & Capital Stack), 12 (Marketing, Sales & Leasing), 14 (Project Completion, Accounting Close & Investor Reporting)

---

> **In Practice — Senior Analyst Perspective**
> Disposition is not a single event — it is a decision framework that begins well before the final unit closes and extends through the complete wind-down of any retained or unsold interests in the project. For a developer whose business model involves building and selling condominium units, the disposition question appears to be settled the moment the last unit transfers title. But even in that case, the developer retains interests that require active management: commercial units held on the ground floor, retained parking inventory, any rental replacement units committed to the municipality as a condition of approval, and — on mixed-use projects — a purpose-built rental tower or retail podium that was always intended to be held or sold as a going concern. On purpose-built rental and mixed-use projects, the disposition decision is the central strategic question of the project's final phase: sell outright to an institutional buyer, refinance and hold for cash flow and long-term appreciation, recapitalize with a new equity partner, or some combination of these paths. Each path has materially different implications for the developer's balance sheet, its tax position, its equity investors' returns, and its ongoing relationship with its lenders. The Senior Analyst's role in the disposition phase is to model each path quantitatively, to identify the conditions under which each path is superior, and to support the execution of whichever path the developer's principal selects with accurate, defensible financial analysis. Getting the disposition decision right — and executing it precisely — is the difference between a project that meets its return targets and one that quietly underperforms in its final chapter.

## 15.1 The Disposition Decision Framework

<div id="ol-ch15-0"></div>

The disposition decision for a real estate development project is not a binary choice between selling and holding. It is a structured analysis of the developer's options for converting its remaining interest in the completed or near-completed project into the combination of liquidity, return, and balance sheet flexibility that best serves its strategic objectives. The framework for that analysis must account for the asset type, the capital structure of the project, the terms of the joint venture agreement, the developer's tax position, and the current state of the investment sales market.

### Defining the Residual Interest

Before any disposition analysis can begin, the Senior Analyst must precisely define what interest is available for disposition. On a straightforward residential condominium project, the answer is usually simple: once all units are closed, the developer's interest in the project entity winds down to the holdback reserves, the warranty reserve, and any unsold parking or storage inventory. There is no going-concern interest to sell.

On projects that include retained components — a rental tower, commercial retail units (CRUs) on the ground floor, a podium office component, or a community facility committed to the municipality — the residual interest is a productive, income-generating asset that can be valued and sold on the investment market. The analysis for these retained components is fundamentally different from a condominium unit sale: the asset is being sold to an investor-buyer based on its capitalized income stream, not to an end-user at a per-unit price.

Mixed-use projects that combine a condominium tower with a purpose-built rental (PBR) component present a particularly common disposition scenario in the Ontario high-rise development market. The PBR component — whether structured as a separate building in a phased development or as a podium base — is often the subject of a disposition analysis that runs in parallel with the condominium sales program. Developers who secured development financing for the entire project, including the PBR component, on the strength of the condominium pre-sales may find themselves needing to either refinance the PBR into permanent mortgage financing or sell the asset at stabilization to repay the construction lender and redeploy equity into the next project.

### The Four Disposition Paths

**Path 1 — Outright Sale at Stabilization**: The developer sells the income-producing asset (rental building, commercial component) to a third-party buyer at or near the point of stabilization — typically defined as 95% occupancy or higher, sustained over a minimum holding period that satisfies the buyer's underwriting requirements. The sale price is determined by applying a capitalization rate to the stabilized net operating income, or by applying a price-per-suite metric derived from comparable transactions. This path provides immediate liquidity, crystallizes the investor return, and eliminates ongoing operational exposure.

**Path 2 — Refinance and Hold**: The developer replaces the construction loan with long-term permanent mortgage financing (typically an insured CMHC mortgage on eligible rental properties, or a conventional mortgage on commercial assets) and holds the asset for ongoing cash flow and potential future appreciation. This path defers the realization of profit — the asset's value is not crystallized until a future sale — but generates recurring cash flow, preserves optionality to sell in a more favourable market, and may qualify for tax-deferred treatment on the deferred gain. The refinancing proceeds are used to repay the construction lender and return equity capital to investors.

**Path 3 — Recapitalization with a New Equity Partner**: The developer sells a partial interest in the stabilized asset to a new equity partner — typically an institutional investor or pension fund — at a valuation that reflects the stabilized income stream. This path provides partial liquidity (the developer receives proceeds from the partial sale), brings in a long-term capital partner for the ongoing hold, and reduces the developer's ongoing equity exposure. The developer typically retains a minority co-investment interest and an asset management mandate, generating ongoing fees while reducing the asset's drag on the developer's balance sheet.

**Path 4 — Phased or Staged Disposition**: On large mixed-use projects with multiple components, the developer may execute different disposition strategies for different asset classes simultaneously — selling the commercial retail units to individual investors or an institutional buyer while refinancing and holding the rental tower, or selling the rental tower to a REIT while retaining the commercial podium. The phased approach requires careful structuring to ensure that the sale of one component does not inadvertently impair the value or financing of a component being retained.

### Strategic Factors in Path Selection

The developer's selection among these paths is driven by several factors that the Senior Analyst must quantify or evaluate: the investor waterfall's impact on retained-asset distributions (a preferred return that is accruing on capital invested in the rental component creates mounting pressure to crystallize returns sooner rather than later); the quality and depth of the current investment sales market for the asset class (cap rate compression in a seller's market favours Path 1; cap rate expansion in a buyer's market may favour holding through Path 2); the developer's own balance sheet capacity (a developer carrying multiple projects simultaneously may need the liquidity of Path 1 to fund the next land acquisition); and the tax implications of each path, which can differ materially depending on the project structure and the developer's own corporate tax position.

## 15.2 Valuation Methods for Disposition Pricing

<div id="ol-ch15-1"></div>

Establishing a credible disposition value for an income-producing real estate asset requires the application of multiple valuation methodologies, each of which captures a different dimension of value. The Senior Analyst is responsible for preparing the valuation analysis that underlies the developer's pricing decision, whether that decision manifests as an asking price in a formal sales process or as a minimum acceptable price in a negotiated transaction.

### Income Capitalization — Direct Cap Rate Method

The direct capitalization method — the most widely used valuation approach for stabilized income-producing real estate — converts the asset's stabilized net operating income into a capital value by dividing by an appropriate capitalization rate.

**Net Operating Income (NOI)** is the property's stabilized gross potential revenue less vacancy and credit loss, less operating expenses, but before debt service, capital expenditures, and income taxes. For a purpose-built rental building in Ontario, NOI is calculated on a stabilized basis — using a market vacancy assumption (typically 3% to 5% for well-located Toronto-area rental, or higher for suburban markets) and normalized operating expenses that reflect efficient management of the building type. Actual first-year NOI, which may reflect a lease-up period with elevated vacancy, is discounted in favour of a stabilized year NOI for direct cap purposes.

The **capitalization rate** is the ratio of NOI to value that a buyer requires to justify acquiring the asset at a given price. Capitalization rates are a function of asset quality, location, tenancy profile, financing conditions, and the prevailing investment market. In the Toronto-area purpose-built rental market, cap rates for high-quality, recently constructed buildings have ranged from approximately 3.5% to 5.5% depending on location, vintage, and suite mix, with significant compression during low-interest-rate periods and widening pressure when borrowing costs rise. The Senior Analyst sources current cap rate benchmarks from investment broker research reports, recent comparable sales, and conversations with the developer's capital markets advisor.

The direct cap calculation is straightforward: Value = Stabilized NOI ÷ Cap Rate. A building with a stabilized NOI of $4,200,000 valued at a 4.25% cap rate has an implied value of $98,823,529 — approximately $98.8 million.

### Income Capitalization — Discounted Cash Flow Method

The discounted cash flow (DCF) method values the asset by projecting its cash flows over a defined investment horizon — typically 10 years for institutional analysis — and discounting those projected cash flows back to a present value at the investor's required discount rate (also called the internal rate of return or target yield). The terminal value at the end of the projection period is calculated by applying a terminal capitalization rate to the projected Year 11 NOI, and this terminal value is also discounted to present value.

The DCF method is more analytically robust than the direct cap method for assets with rental growth potential, near-term lease expiries or rent resets, or capital expenditure requirements — because it captures the timing and variability of cash flows rather than reducing them to a single stabilized year. For purpose-built rental buildings under Ontario's Residential Tenancies Act, the DCF analysis must reflect the distinction between in-place rents (which are subject to annual guideline increases for existing tenants) and market rents (which apply to vacant suites turned over to new tenants), as the gap between in-place and market rents is a significant driver of projected revenue growth.

The Senior Analyst's DCF model for a disposition analysis typically presents three scenarios — base case (market consensus assumptions), upside case (stronger-than-expected rent growth and lower vacancy), and downside case (softer market conditions) — to give the developer and its advisors a sense of the range of value outcomes and the sensitivity of the indicated value to key assumptions.

### Price-Per-Suite Comparables

In addition to the income-based valuation methods, the Senior Analyst tracks and applies price-per-suite metrics derived from recent transactions in the comparable asset class and geography. Price per suite is a simpler metric than the cap rate — it expresses the total transaction price divided by the number of suites — and is widely used as a cross-check on the income-based valuation and as the primary communication tool in broker-to-buyer marketing materials.

Price-per-suite benchmarks vary significantly by suite size, building quality, location, and vintage. The Senior Analyst applies per-suite benchmarks from genuinely comparable transactions — same asset class, similar geography, similar vintage — rather than using blended metropolitan averages that may not reflect the specific asset's characteristics.

### Gross Revenue Multiplier

The gross revenue multiplier (GRM) — the ratio of the purchase price to gross potential revenue — is a simpler, less analytically precise metric than the cap rate or DCF, but is commonly used as a preliminary filter in disposition analysis and as a cross-check. A GRM of 18x to 22x is typical for high-quality Toronto-area rental assets in normal market conditions, implying that investors are willing to pay 18 to 22 times the building's annual gross revenue. Like all rule-of-thumb metrics, the GRM has significant limitations and should not be the primary basis for a disposition pricing decision.

## 15.3 The Investment Sales Process

<div id="ol-ch15-2"></div>

When the developer decides to pursue an outright sale (Path 1) or a partial recapitalization (Path 3), it typically engages an investment sales broker — also called a capital markets advisor — to manage the sale process. The investment sales process for a large institutional-quality real estate asset follows a structured sequence designed to maximize competitive tension among prospective buyers and ensure that the developer achieves the best available market price.

### Engaging the Capital Markets Advisor

The capital markets advisor is engaged under a listing agreement that defines the scope of the engagement (exclusive or non-exclusive listing), the advisory fee (typically expressed as a percentage of the gross sale price, ranging from 0.5% to 1.25% for large institutional transactions), the listing period, and any minimum acceptable price or approval threshold. The developer selects its capital markets advisor on the basis of the advisor's track record in the relevant asset class and market, its relationships with prospective buyers, and its ability to run a competitive process that generates multiple offers.

The Senior Analyst prepares the financial model and supporting analysis that forms the core of the offering memorandum — the primary marketing document distributed to prospective buyers during the sale process.

### The Offering Memorandum

The offering memorandum (OM) is the comprehensive marketing and due diligence document that the capital markets advisor distributes to prospective buyers after executing a confidentiality agreement. The OM presents the asset in its best light while complying with the requirement for accurate and complete disclosure. It typically contains the following sections: an executive summary of the investment opportunity; a property description (physical description, building systems, amenities, suite mix); a market overview (submarket demographics, employment, rental market dynamics); a tenancy summary (current occupancy, in-place rents versus market rents, lease terms for any commercial tenants); a financial summary (historical and projected NOI, stabilized NOI, operating expense breakdown); a proposed terms section (sale format, bid deadline, conditions); and the Senior Analyst's financial model, typically provided as a locked Excel workbook or presented in summary tables within the OM.

The Senior Analyst's financial model within the OM must be accurate, internally consistent, and defensible under buyer due diligence scrutiny. A model that cannot withstand detailed questioning by a sophisticated buyer's analyst will erode confidence in the asset and the developer, and may prompt requests for price adjustments during the due diligence period.

### Bid Process and Negotiation

Investment sale processes for large assets are typically run as structured bid processes: the capital markets advisor distributes the OM to a targeted list of prospective buyers, sets a deadline for non-binding offers (the initial bid), reviews the initial bids with the developer, selects a short list of buyers for further due diligence access and refined offers (the best and final offer round), and then negotiates a purchase and sale agreement with the selected buyer.

The non-binding initial bid establishes the buyer's headline price and key terms — conditions, deposit requirements, due diligence period, and closing timeline. The Senior Analyst tracks all initial bids in a bid comparison schedule, comparing each buyer's price, cap rate implied by the price, conditions of closing, proposed due diligence timeline, and any unusual terms. This comparison is the primary analytical input to the developer's decision on which buyers to advance to the best and final offer round.

The purchase and sale agreement — negotiated by the developer's real estate solicitor — will include representations and warranties from the seller, a due diligence period during which the buyer has the right to review all property documentation and financial records, a condition removal process, a deposit structure, and a closing date. The Senior Analyst supports the due diligence process by maintaining a virtual data room (VDR) — an organized, secure online repository of all property and financial documents — and responding to buyer due diligence queries.

### Vendor Take-Back Financing

On transactions where the buyer's financing is a constraining factor — for example, where conventional mortgage proceeds are insufficient to fund the full purchase price — the developer may provide vendor take-back (VTB) financing: a loan from the developer to the buyer that supplements the buyer's first mortgage and bridges the gap between the lender's proceeds and the purchase price. VTB financing is uncommon in large institutional transactions but appears occasionally in transactions with private buyers or in markets where construction loan lenders are unwilling to provide bridge financing for stabilized assets. VTB financing introduces credit risk to the developer — the developer is now a lender to the buyer — and its terms (rate, term, security position) must be reviewed carefully by the developer's legal and financial advisors.

## 15.4 Purpose-Built Rental: The CMHC MLI Select Program

<div id="ol-ch15-3"></div>

For developers who elect to hold their purpose-built rental (PBR) component rather than sell it (Path 2 — Refinance and Hold), the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation's MLI Select program is typically the most economically attractive permanent financing vehicle available. Understanding the MLI Select program's structure, eligibility criteria, and financial mechanics is essential for the Senior Analyst modeling the hold-versus-sell analysis.

### MLI Select Program Overview

MLI Select (Multi-Unit Mortgage Loan Insurance Select) is CMHC's flagship mortgage loan insurance program for multi-unit residential rental properties, offering insured mortgage financing at loan-to-value ratios, amortization periods, and interest rates that are materially superior to conventional (non-insured) alternatives. The program replaced the former CMHC rental insurance suite (Rental Construction Financing Initiative, Flex 1 and Flex 2) with a points-based framework that rewards borrowers for achieving defined levels of affordability, energy efficiency, and accessibility in their rental projects.

Under MLI Select, borrowers earn points by committing to specific performance metrics in three categories: affordability (units priced below a defined percentage of area median household income), energy efficiency (achieving specified reductions in energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions versus a baseline), and accessibility (incorporating universal design features at the suite and building level). A minimum point threshold is required to access the program; higher point scores unlock progressively better financing terms — higher LTV ratios, longer amortization periods, and lower insurance premiums.

### MLI Select Financing Terms

At the highest tier of the MLI Select program (50 or more points), borrowers can access: loan-to-value ratios of up to 95%; amortization periods of up to 50 years; and a mortgage insurance premium that is lower than the standard CMHC multi-unit premium. The 50-year amortization feature — not available on any other CMHC product — produces significantly lower annual debt service than a conventional 25- or 30-year amortization, which is the primary driver of the program's economic attractiveness for developers holding PBR assets.

The combination of high LTV (maximizing loan proceeds relative to the asset's value) and long amortization (minimizing annual debt service) produces a refinancing structure that can return most or all of the developer's original equity investment in the asset — sometimes described as a "cash-out refinance" or "equity crystallization" — while generating positive after-debt-service cash flow from the asset's operations. When the loan proceeds from the MLI Select mortgage are sufficient to repay the construction loan and return the equity investors' capital contributions, the developer effectively owns the stabilized rental asset with little or no remaining equity basis, and any future sale proceeds represent near-pure profit.

### Calculating the MLI Select Loan Proceeds

The MLI Select loan amount is determined by the lesser of: (a) the LTV ratio applied to the CMHC-appraised value of the property; (b) the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) constraint — the loan amount at which annual debt service consumes no more than a specified percentage of NOI; and (c) the loan-to-cost ratio limit for new construction properties.

The DSCR constraint is typically the binding constraint on the MLI Select loan amount for high-LTV applications: CMHC requires that the property's NOI (using CMHC's conservative underwriting of vacancy and operating expenses) be sufficient to cover the annual debt service at the program's minimum DSCR, typically 1.10x to 1.20x. A property that fails the DSCR test cannot access the full LTV-implied loan amount regardless of its appraised value.

The Senior Analyst's MLI Select loan sizing model proceeds as follows: (1) project the stabilized NOI on CMHC's underwriting assumptions; (2) calculate the maximum annual debt service at the minimum DSCR; (3) derive the maximum loan amount at the program's interest rate and amortization period using the annual debt service payment as the constraint; (4) cross-check against the LTV limit applied to the appraised value; and (5) identify which constraint is binding and size the loan accordingly.

## 15.5 Tax Considerations in Disposition

<div id="ol-ch15-4"></div>

The tax consequences of a disposition decision are among its most material financial variables, and the path that maximizes pre-tax returns may not maximize after-tax returns once the developer's tax position is properly modelled. The Senior Analyst must understand the tax framework applicable to each disposition path — not to provide tax advice (which is the domain of the developer's tax advisor) but to ensure that the financial model captures the correct after-tax cash flows and that the tax implications of each path are presented accurately to the developer's principal and investors.

### Recaptured CCA and Terminal Loss

When a developer sells a depreciable capital property — a rental building, for example — the difference between the proceeds of disposition and the property's undepreciated capital cost (UCC) at the time of sale is either recaptured depreciation (fully taxable as income) or a terminal loss (fully deductible). Recaptured CCA (Capital Cost Allowance) arises when the proceeds exceed the UCC; terminal loss arises when the UCC exceeds the proceeds.

For a developer who has owned and operated a rental building for several years and claimed CCA throughout the holding period, the UCC at the time of sale may be significantly lower than the original cost, and the recaptured CCA on a sale at a high market price may be substantial. The recaptured CCA is included in the developer's income for the year of sale and taxed at the full corporate rate — unlike capital gains, there is no inclusion rate reduction for recaptured CCA. This is a material consideration in the decision between selling the asset outright versus continuing to hold it.

### Capital Gains Treatment

The portion of the sale proceeds that exceeds the original capital cost of the asset (not the UCC — the original cost, before CCA claims) is a capital gain. For a corporation, the capital gains inclusion rate determines what portion of the capital gain is includable in income and subject to tax. The inclusion rate has been subject to legislative change in Canada — the federal 2024 Budget proposed increasing the inclusion rate from one-half to two-thirds for corporations and trusts — and the Senior Analyst must apply the rate in effect at the time of the analysis (or model sensitivity to rate changes where there is legislative uncertainty) and confirm the applicable rate with the developer's tax advisor.

### Section 97 Rollover — Transfer to a Partnership

Where the developer's corporate structure allows, it may be possible to transfer the rental asset to a limited partnership at cost (without triggering an immediate taxable gain) under the Income Tax Act section 97(2) rollover provisions. This deferral mechanism is available where both the transferor and the partnership are Canadian residents and where the partnership issues partnership units as consideration for the transferred property. The section 97 rollover defers the tax on any accrued gain until the transferee partnership disposes of the property — it does not eliminate the tax, but it creates flexibility for the developer to bring in new equity partners (via the LP structure) without immediately crystallizing a taxable gain. The use of a section 97 rollover requires careful legal and tax structuring and must be reviewed by the developer's tax advisor before implementation.

### HST on Commercial Asset Sales

The sale of a commercial income-producing property — a CRU, an office unit, or a ground-floor retail podium — is generally a taxable supply for HST purposes, and the seller is required to collect and remit HST on the sale proceeds. However, many commercial real estate transactions between HST-registered parties are structured as joint elections to avoid the requirement for the seller to collect and the buyer to fund HST at closing: the seller and buyer jointly elect under section 167 of the Excise Tax Act to treat the sale as a business sale to which HST does not apply (on the basis that the buyer is acquiring a business — the rental enterprise — rather than real property per se). The Senior Analyst confirms the HST treatment of any commercial disposition with the developer's HST advisor before preparing the net proceeds model.

## 15.6 Retained Commercial Units — Strategy and Disposition

<div id="ol-ch15-5"></div>

On many Toronto and urban Ontario condominium developments, the ground floor of the building is designed to accommodate commercial retail uses (CRUs) — ground-floor units leased to retail, restaurant, or service tenants that activate the street edge and satisfy the municipality's urban design objectives for commercial main streets and pedestrian-priority zones. These CRUs are typically registered as separate condominium units in the commercial component of a mixed-use condominium and are either sold to individual investors at registration or retained by the developer for ongoing rental income.

### Individual Strata Sale versus Institutional Bulk Sale

The developer's primary disposition decision for a commercial retail portfolio is whether to sell the units individually (strata sale) to owner-occupiers or small investors, or to hold them assembled and sell as a portfolio to an institutional buyer, or to retain them for the developer's own account as a long-term income asset.

Individual strata sale — selling each CRU separately — maximizes the per-unit price because owner-occupier and small investor buyers typically pay a premium over the capitalized investment value of the unit. A 1,200-square-foot restaurant unit that would be valued at approximately $800,000 based on its rental income might be worth $1,100,000 or more to an owner-occupier who attributes value to the right to occupy and operate in that specific location. The trade-off is the administrative complexity of managing multiple buyer transactions and the risk that individual units do not sell quickly, leaving the developer with a lingering unsold inventory that the condominium corporation must manage.

Portfolio sale to an institutional buyer — selling all CRUs as a bundled asset — achieves a lower per-unit price than the strata option but offers certainty of execution, speed, and the elimination of ongoing strata management complexity. Institutional buyers of retail strata portfolios typically apply a capitalization rate that reflects the portfolio's in-place lease terms, tenant mix, and the risks associated with mixed-use condominium retail (strata commercial tends to trade at a higher cap rate than freehold commercial retail because of the shared governance of the condominium corporation and the restrictions imposed by the condominium declaration on permitted uses).

### Lease-Up Strategy Before Disposition

For CRUs that are being retained or assembled for a portfolio sale, the developer's primary value-creating activity in the disposition period is executing leases with quality tenants at market rents. A vacant CRU has minimal investment value — it generates no income and cannot be valued by a cap rate approach. A leased CRU with a creditworthy tenant on a long-term lease is a fully investible asset. The Senior Analyst models the impact of lease-up on the CRU portfolio's value: a unit leased at $60,000 per annum on a 10-year net lease, valued at a 5.5% cap rate, has an indicated value of $1,090,909. The same unit vacant has an indicated value determined by residual land value analysis, which will typically be materially lower.

The leasing strategy for CRUs in a new condominium building requires careful management of the permitted use provisions in the condominium declaration, any tenant mix objectives embedded in the site plan agreement with the municipality, and the building's occupancy restrictions that may prevent certain food and beverage uses until the building's ventilation and grease trap infrastructure is complete.

## 15.7 Exit from the Joint Venture Structure

<div id="ol-ch15-6"></div>

The disposition of the underlying real estate asset is only one dimension of the developer's exit from a project. The other dimension — equally consequential and sometimes more complex — is the dissolution of the joint venture or limited partnership structure through which the project was developed and financed. Chapter 14 addressed the mechanics of the waterfall distribution and the wind-down of the project entity in the context of a completed condominium project. This section addresses the additional complexity that arises when the project retains an income-producing component beyond the condominium closing program.

### Transferring the Retained Asset to a New Entity

When the project entity (the construction-phase LP or JV) has fulfilled its purpose — building the condominium and closing the units — the retained income-producing asset (the PBR tower, the commercial portfolio) must be transferred from the project entity to a new holding entity that will either own the asset for the long term or serve as the vehicle for the institutional investor recap or sale process. This transfer requires careful legal and tax structuring to avoid triggering immediate tax consequences (see Section 15.5 — section 97 rollover) and to ensure that the new entity's capital structure is appropriate for the intended holding or sale strategy.

The joint venture agreement governing the project entity will typically include provisions that address the treatment of retained assets at project completion — whether the LP investors have the right to receive their share of the retained asset in kind (a distribution of ownership interests in the new holding entity), whether the developer has the right to buy out the LP investors at an agreed or appraised price, or whether the retained asset must be sold within a defined period. The Senior Analyst reviews the joint venture agreement's exit provisions as a first step in structuring the retained asset strategy, because the agreement's terms may limit the developer's flexibility to pursue the economically optimal disposition path.

### Buy-Sell (Shotgun) Provisions

Many joint venture agreements include a buy-sell provision — sometimes called a shotgun clause — that gives either party the right to initiate a compelled transaction at any time after a defined trigger event. Under a typical shotgun provision, the initiating party names a price per unit interest in the joint venture; the other party must then either buy the initiating party's interest at that price or sell its own interest to the initiating party at that price. The shotgun mechanism creates a strong incentive for the named price to be fair — because the naming party cannot know in advance whether it will be the buyer or the seller.

Shotgun clauses are most commonly triggered in joint ventures where the partners have different investment horizons or disposition preferences: an institutional LP investor that has reached the end of its investment mandate for the project may trigger the shotgun to force a liquidation; a developer who believes the asset's market value has increased since the LP's entry point may trigger the shotgun as a way to buy out the LP at the original underwritten value rather than at current market. The Senior Analyst models the financial implications of both sides of a potential shotgun scenario — what it would cost the developer to buy out the LP at the named price, and what the developer would receive if the LP exercises its right to buy the developer's interest at the same price.

### Right of First Offer and Right of First Refusal

An alternative to the shotgun mechanism — more common in partnerships with longer-term investment horizons — is a right of first offer (ROFO) or right of first refusal (ROFR) on the interest of the other party. Under a ROFO, if one party wishes to sell its interest in the joint venture, it must first offer that interest to the other party at a price it names; the other party has a defined period to accept or decline. Under a ROFR, if one party receives a bona fide third-party offer to purchase its interest, it must present that offer to the other party, who has the right to purchase on the same terms. ROFOs and ROFRs are less immediately coercive than the shotgun but achieve a similar objective of ensuring that a partner change requires the consent or at least the informed decision of the continuing partner.

## 15.8 Portfolio-Level Disposition Strategy

<div id="ol-ch15-7"></div>

A developer who has completed multiple projects and has built a portfolio of income-producing assets — whether retained PBR buildings, ground-floor commercial units, or mixed-use components — faces a disposition question at the portfolio level that is distinct from the individual asset analysis. Portfolio-level disposition strategy involves decisions about which assets to sell, in what sequence, and through what vehicle to optimize the developer's overall risk-adjusted return and strategic positioning.

### Holding Period Optimization

The optimal holding period for an income-producing asset is the point at which the marginal return from continued ownership falls below the developer's cost of capital or target return threshold. Factors that accelerate the optimal exit include: cap rate compression that has driven the asset's current market value above what the developer's internal model would support on a long-term hold basis (a good time to sell); the approach of significant capital expenditure requirements (a building that will require substantial reinvestment in the near term may be better sold before those capital needs are fully reflected in the asset's operating cost structure); and portfolio rebalancing needs (if the developer's next project requires equity, selling a stabilized asset to fund the new land acquisition may be the most efficient capital allocation decision).

Factors that argue for extending the holding period include: in-place rents that are materially below market (an asset whose rents have been held below market by guideline increases is sitting on unrealized revenue potential that a sale at current income would not fully capture); anticipated market rent growth driven by near-term supply shortfall; a refinancing opportunity in the near term (CMHC renewal or a conventional mortgage coming due) that will return equity at the asset's appreciated value without triggering a sale; and tax deferral benefits from continuing to hold rather than crystallizing the gain.

### REIT and Institutional Buyer Disposition

For developers with portfolios of sufficient scale, the most efficient large-format disposition vehicle is a sale to a REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust) or a large institutional investor (pension fund, insurance company life co) that is able to acquire an entire portfolio in a single transaction. Portfolio sales to institutional buyers typically command a modest discount to the sum of the individual asset values — the "portfolio discount" — but this discount is often outweighed by the transaction efficiency, speed, and certainty of a single-buyer deal.

The Senior Analyst's portfolio-level analysis for an institutional sale includes: a consolidated portfolio NOI schedule (aggregating the stabilized NOI of all assets in the portfolio); a blended cap rate analysis (applying individual asset cap rates to produce a blended portfolio value); and a comparison of the implied portfolio sale value against the sum-of-the-parts individual asset values, to quantify the portfolio discount and support the developer's decision on whether a bulk sale or individual asset sales are the superior strategy.

### Developer Exit via REIT IPO

For the largest developers with fully stabilized, diversified portfolios, the ultimate exit vehicle is a real estate investment trust initial public offering — contributing the portfolio to a newly formed REIT and selling REIT units to public market investors. The REIT IPO vehicle provides the developer with liquidity (through the sale of REIT units in the public offering), a continuing economic interest (through retained REIT units), an asset management mandate (the developer's operating platform is typically contracted as the REIT's asset manager), and access to the public capital markets for future growth. The REIT IPO is not a realistic exit path for most developers — the minimum asset scale required to attract institutional REIT investors is typically $400 million to $800 million of stabilized property value — but it represents the apex of the exit spectrum and is increasingly relevant for Ontario developers who have assembled portfolios of purpose-built rental assets through successive development cycles.

## 15.9 Common Failure Modes in Disposition

<div id="ol-ch15-8"></div>

Disposition failures — outcomes that leave value on the table, trigger tax exposures not anticipated in the pro forma, or damage investor relationships — are not random. They arise from predictable analytical and process failures that can be avoided with disciplined preparation.

**1. Failing to model the disposition path from project inception.** Developers who treat the disposition decision as a post-construction afterthought rather than a front-end pro forma assumption often discover that the capital structure, lease-up strategy, and financing arrangements they chose for the development phase are not optimized for the intended exit. A rental building developed with institutional co-investment from an LP investor whose mandate expires in five years will face disposition pressure at the five-year mark regardless of market conditions — that pressure should be modelled at underwriting, not discovered at year four.

**2. Over-relying on a single valuation method.** Using only the direct cap rate method — without cross-checking against DCF, price-per-suite comparables, or GRM — produces a value estimate that may be materially inaccurate if the assumed cap rate is wrong. In a market where cap rates are moving quickly (either compressing or expanding), a single-metric valuation can miss the current market by a wide margin. The Senior Analyst always presents multiple valuation approaches and a range of value estimates, not a single point value.

**3. Proceeding to market without a stabilized tenancy.** Developers who launch a formal sale process before the asset has achieved stabilized occupancy — pressured by construction loan maturity or LP investor timeline demands — consistently achieve lower prices than those who wait for stabilization. A buyer purchasing a building at 70% occupancy must underwrite the lease-up risk and price accordingly; a buyer purchasing at 97% occupancy pays for the fully operational asset. Unless the development timeline is forcing a pre-stabilization sale, patience through the lease-up period is almost always the value-maximizing strategy.

**4. Inadequate data room preparation.** An investment sales process in which the vendor cannot promptly produce leases, environmental reports, as-built drawings, title documentation, or operating cost records forces buyers to extend their due diligence timelines, increases their perception of risk, and frequently results in price adjustments or transaction failures. The Senior Analyst's role in preparing and maintaining the virtual data room is as consequential as the financial model preparation.

**5. Overlooking joint venture agreement exit provisions.** Developers who have not carefully reviewed the disposition provisions of their joint venture agreement before executing a sale may find that they have violated the LP's ROFR, triggered an unanticipated shotgun mechanism, or failed to obtain required approval thresholds for the transaction. Any of these oversights can result in the transaction being challenged, delayed, or voided. The disposition process must begin with a complete review of the joint venture agreement by the developer's legal counsel.

**6. Ignoring tax on recaptured CCA in the hold-versus-sell analysis.** A hold-versus-sell analysis that compares the gross sale proceeds against the present value of projected future cash flows — without deducting the tax on recaptured CCA from the sale proceeds — will systematically overstate the attractiveness of the sell option relative to the hold option. On an asset with significant accumulated CCA, the tax on recapture can represent 20% or more of the gross gain, and its exclusion from the model produces a materially misleading comparison.

**7. Mismanaging the disposition timeline relative to the construction loan maturity.** A construction lender whose loan is maturing will not extend the maturity indefinitely to accommodate an optimal market timing decision by the developer. Developers who allow the disposition timeline to drift beyond the construction loan's extended maturity expose themselves to default remedies, forced sales, and the loss of negotiating leverage with buyers who understand the developer's constrained position. Disposition planning must be calibrated against the construction loan maturity, with sufficient time buffer to complete a structured sale process before the lender's patience expires.

## 15.10 Key Metrics and Terms — Reference Table

<div id="ol-ch15-9"></div>

The following table summarizes the metrics, instruments, and terminology introduced in this chapter.

| Metric / Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Net Operating Income (NOI)** | Stabilized gross potential revenue less vacancy and credit loss, less operating expenses; the income stream used as the basis for income-capitalization valuation; calculated before debt service, capital expenditures, and income taxes. |
| **Capitalization Rate (Cap Rate)** | The ratio of a property's NOI to its market value; expresses the initial yield an investor receives on the purchase price; lower cap rates indicate higher valuations relative to income, typically reflecting higher-quality assets or stronger market conditions. |
| **Direct Capitalization Method** | The income-capitalization valuation approach that converts a single stabilized NOI figure into a capital value by dividing by an applicable cap rate; the most widely used valuation method for stabilized income-producing real estate. |
| **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Method** | The income-capitalization valuation approach that projects property cash flows over a defined investment horizon, discounts them to present value at the investor's required return rate, and adds the present value of a terminal sale; captures timing and variability of cash flows more robustly than the direct cap method. |
| **Terminal Capitalization Rate** | The cap rate applied to the projected Year 11 (or final year plus one) NOI in a DCF analysis to derive the terminal value of the asset at the end of the projection period; typically set at a modest premium to the going-in cap rate to reflect aging and uncertainty. |
| **Price-Per-Suite** | A transaction metric expressing the total purchase price of a multi-unit residential property divided by the number of suites; used as a cross-check on income-based valuation and as a primary communication metric in investment marketing. |
| **Gross Revenue Multiplier (GRM)** | The ratio of a property's purchase price to its gross potential revenue; a simple preliminary filter used to assess value relative to income at a high level, without the precision of income-based methods. |
| **MLI Select** | CMHC's Multi-Unit Mortgage Loan Insurance Select program; the primary insured mortgage product for purpose-built rental properties, offering high LTV ratios, extended amortization periods, and competitive insurance premiums in exchange for borrower commitments on affordability, energy efficiency, and accessibility. |
| **Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)** | The ratio of NOI to annual debt service (principal and interest payments); the primary loan sizing constraint in CMHC MLI Select underwriting; a minimum DSCR is required to access the program's maximum loan amount. |
| **Vendor Take-Back (VTB) Financing** | A loan provided by the seller of a property to the buyer to supplement the buyer's first mortgage financing; introduces credit risk to the developer-as-seller and is most common in private market transactions where institutional mortgage proceeds are insufficient. |
| **Offering Memorandum (OM)** | The primary marketing and due diligence document distributed to prospective buyers in an investment sale process; prepared by the capital markets advisor with financial inputs from the developer's Senior Analyst; contains the property description, market overview, financial summary, and proposed sale terms. |
| **Virtual Data Room (VDR)** | A secure online document repository maintained by the vendor to support buyer due diligence during an investment sale process; contains all material property documents including leases, environmental reports, title documentation, as-built drawings, and financial records. |
| **Buy-Sell (Shotgun) Provision** | A joint venture agreement mechanism that allows either party to name a price for the entire joint venture interest and compel the other party to either buy the naming party's interest or sell their own interest at that same price; ensures fair pricing through mutual uncertainty about which role each party will occupy. |
| **Right of First Offer (ROFO)** | A joint venture right requiring a party wishing to sell its interest to first offer that interest to the other party at a price it names before approaching third parties; the other party may accept or decline within a defined period. |
| **Right of First Refusal (ROFR)** | A joint venture right requiring a party who has received a bona fide third-party offer to first present that offer to the other party, who may purchase the interest on the same terms within a defined period. |
| **Section 97(2) Rollover** | An Income Tax Act provision that allows a taxpayer to transfer property to a partnership without triggering an immediate taxable gain, provided both parties are Canadian residents and the partnership issues units as consideration; defers rather than eliminates the accrued tax liability. |
| **Recaptured CCA** | The taxable income arising when the proceeds of disposition of a depreciable capital property exceed its undepreciated capital cost (UCC); fully included in income and taxed at the corporate rate, without the benefit of the capital gains inclusion rate. |
| **Undepreciated Capital Cost (UCC)** | The remaining tax cost of a depreciable capital property after deducting all Capital Cost Allowance (CCA) claimed in prior years; the tax base against which proceeds of disposition are compared to determine recaptured CCA or terminal loss. |
| **Strata Sale** | The individual unit-by-unit sale of commercial retail units (CRUs) or other condominium components to separate buyers; typically achieves a higher per-unit price than a bulk portfolio sale but involves greater transaction complexity and execution risk. |
| **Portfolio Discount** | The modest reduction in aggregate value that typically arises when a portfolio of assets is sold to a single buyer relative to the sum of the individual asset values; reflects the buyer's requirement for a premium to compensate for the complexity and concentration of a large single-transaction acquisition. |
| **Holding Period Optimization** | The analytical process of identifying the point at which the marginal return from continued ownership of an income-producing asset falls below the developer's cost of capital, signaling that disposition will produce a higher risk-adjusted return than continued holding. |

## AE-15.1 — Hold versus Sell Analysis for a Purpose-Built Rental Component

### Scenario

You are the Senior Analyst for a Toronto-area developer that has completed a mixed-use condominium and purpose-built rental project. The condominium tower (212 units) has fully closed. The PBR tower (186 suites) reached stabilized occupancy of 96% fourteen months ago and is now generating stabilized annual NOI of $4,725,000. The current outstanding balance on the construction loan attributable to the PBR component is $58,000,000, maturing in 11 months. Total equity invested in the PBR component by the developer (GP) and LP investors is $18,500,000, with a preferred return of 7.5% per annum, simple interest, that has been accruing for 4.0 years. The LP contributed 80% of the equity ($14,800,000) and the GP contributed 20% ($3,700,000).

**Investment sales market context:** A comparable purpose-built rental building of similar vintage and location in the same submarket sold three months ago at a 4.40% cap rate, and your capital markets advisor has confirmed that the current market for your asset is likely 4.35% to 4.55%.

**MLI Select context:** Your CMHC advisor has confirmed that the PBR tower qualifies for the MLI Select program at 50+ points (95% LTV). The CMHC appraiser is expected to value the building at $108,000,000. The maximum DSCR-constrained loan amount, calculated using CMHC's underwriting assumptions (NOI of $4,600,000, minimum DSCR 1.10x, 50-year amortization at a blended insured rate of 4.35%), is $92,800,000. CMHC insurance premium at this LTV: 2.75% of the loan amount (capitalized into the loan).

### Questions

1. Calculate the indicated value range for the PBR tower using the direct capitalization method, and identify the implied price-per-suite range.
2. Calculate the net proceeds to the joint venture under the sell option (using the midpoint cap rate of 4.45%), assuming a standard 1.0% capital markets advisory fee and no other transaction costs.
3. Calculate the net proceeds to the joint venture under the MLI Select refinancing option, and compare the equity returned to investors under both paths.
4. Calculate the accrued preferred return for the LP and GP, and determine whether either disposition path generates sufficient distributable proceeds to fully fund the preferred return and any promote.

---

## AE-15.1 — Solution

**Question 1 — Direct cap rate valuation and price-per-suite**

Stabilized NOI: $4,725,000

At a 4.35% cap rate: $4,725,000 ÷ 0.0435 = **$108,620,689** (approximately $108.6 million)
At a 4.55% cap rate: $4,725,000 ÷ 0.0455 = **$103,846,154** (approximately $103.8 million)

**Indicated value range: $103.8 million to $108.6 million**

Price per suite (186 suites):
- At $108.6 million: $108,620,689 ÷ 186 = **$583,981 per suite** (approximately $584,000 per suite)
- At $103.8 million: $103,846,154 ÷ 186 = **$558,312 per suite** (approximately $558,000 per suite)

**Indicated price-per-suite range: approximately $558,000 to $584,000 per suite**

These benchmarks are consistent with what a capital markets advisor would present for a high-quality, recently stabilized purpose-built rental building in the Toronto submarket under current market conditions.

**Question 2 — Net proceeds under the sell option (midpoint cap rate 4.45%)**

Gross sale price at 4.45% cap rate: $4,725,000 ÷ 0.0445 = **$106,179,775** (approximately $106.2 million)

Less capital markets advisory fee at 1.0%: $106,179,775 × 0.010 = $1,061,798

Net sale proceeds before construction loan repayment: $106,179,775 − $1,061,798 = **$105,117,977**

Less outstanding construction loan balance: $105,117,977 − $58,000,000 = **$47,117,977**

Net distributable proceeds to the joint venture under the sell option: **$47,117,977** (approximately $47.1 million)

**Question 3 — Net proceeds under the MLI Select refinancing option**

The DSCR-constrained maximum loan amount is $92,800,000. This is less than 95% of the CMHC appraised value ($108,000,000 × 95% = $102,600,000), confirming that the DSCR constraint is the binding constraint.

Gross MLI Select loan proceeds: $92,800,000
Less CMHC insurance premium (2.75%, capitalized into the loan): $92,800,000 × 0.0275 = $2,552,000
Net mortgage proceeds received by the borrower: $92,800,000 − $2,552,000 = **$90,248,000**

Note: The insurance premium is capitalized into the loan rather than paid upfront, so the borrower receives net proceeds of $90,248,000 and the loan balance outstanding (for debt service purposes) is $92,800,000. For the purposes of calculating equity returned, we use the net proceeds received.

Less outstanding construction loan repayment: $90,248,000 − $58,000,000 = **$32,248,000**

Net equity returned to the joint venture under the MLI Select refinancing option: **$32,248,000** (approximately $32.2 million)

**Comparison — equity returned under each path:**

| Path | Net Distributable Proceeds | Equity Invested | Capital Returned | Residual in Asset |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sell at 4.45% cap | $47,117,977 | $18,500,000 | All $18,500,000 plus profit | None — asset sold |
| MLI Select refi | $32,248,000 | $18,500,000 | $18,500,000 (partial) | Developer retains asset |

Under the sell option, the JV distributes $47.1 million — fully returning the $18.5 million in equity with $28.6 million of profit. Under the MLI Select refinancing, the JV distributes $32.2 million — returning only $32.2 million of the $18.5 million equity (the equity is fully returned since $32.2 million > $18.5 million), with $13.7 million of profit distributed, and the developer retaining the asset with a new $92.8 million mortgage and an ongoing cash flow stream.

**Question 4 — Preferred return calculation and waterfall sufficiency**

Accrued preferred return at 7.5% per annum, simple interest, over 4.0 years:

**LP ($14,800,000 × 7.5% × 4.0 years):** $14,800,000 × 0.075 × 4.0 = **$4,440,000**
**GP ($3,700,000 × 7.5% × 4.0 years):** $3,700,000 × 0.075 × 4.0 = **$1,110,000**
**Total accrued preferred return: $5,550,000**

Total required to satisfy Tier 1 (return of capital) and Tier 2 (preferred return): $18,500,000 + $5,550,000 = **$24,050,000**

**Under the sell option ($47,117,977 distributable):**
- Tier 1: Full return of capital ($18,500,000) — fully funded. ✓
- Tier 2: Full preferred return ($5,550,000) — fully funded. ✓
- Remaining for Tier 4 promote: $47,117,977 − $18,500,000 − $5,550,000 = **$23,067,977**

The sell option generates substantial promoted returns. Assuming a standard 70/30 promote (LP/GP), the promote distributions would be $16,147,584 to the LP and $6,920,393 to the GP.

**Under the MLI Select refinancing ($32,248,000 distributable):**
- Tier 1: Full return of capital ($18,500,000) — fully funded. ✓
- Tier 2: Preferred return — available after capital return: $32,248,000 − $18,500,000 = $13,748,000; accrued preferred return of $5,550,000 — **fully funded**. ✓
- Remaining for Tier 4 promote: $13,748,000 − $5,550,000 = **$8,198,000**

Even under the MLI Select refinancing, the preferred return is fully satisfied and a promote of $8,198,000 ($5,738,600 LP, $2,459,400 GP) is distributable — with the developer retaining the stabilized rental asset for ongoing cash flow. The sell option generates a larger immediate distribute, but the MLI Select path preserves the asset's future appreciation and cash flow while still fully satisfying investor return obligations. The optimal path is a function of the developer's capital needs, the JV agreement's holding period provisions, and the investor's exit timeline.

A Toronto midrise condominium development contains 8 CRU units on the ground floor with the following characteristics:

**Question 3 — Strategy comparison and non-financial factors**

**Strategy B (individual strata sales) produces higher gross proceeds: $9,538,000 versus $8,350,650 — a difference of $1,187,350.** This premium is driven primarily by the owner-occupier premium on the vacant units ($600/sf versus $450/sf residual value) and the absence of a portfolio discount.

Non-financial factors that might lead the developer to choose Strategy A (the lower-proceeds portfolio sale) despite the $1.19 million proceeds gap:

**Speed and certainty of execution.** A portfolio sale to a single institutional buyer closes in one transaction, typically within 60 to 90 days of an accepted offer. Selling 8 CRUs individually — particularly in a commercial strata market where buyer pools for leased CRUs and vacant CRUs may be limited — could take 12 to 24 months or longer, during which the developer must continue to manage the CRU portfolio, field strata maintenance obligations, and carry the assets on its balance sheet.

**Condominium corporation management complexity.** As long as the developer retains unsold CRUs, it is a unit owner in the condominium corporation and bears the associated common element fees, status certificate obligations, and governance participation requirements. Removing the entire CRU portfolio from the developer's balance sheet in a single transaction eliminates this ongoing administrative burden.

**Vacancy risk on the individual strata path.** CRU-4 and CRU-7 are currently vacant. An owner-occupier buyer sale for a vacant CRU requires identifying a specific end-user; there is no guarantee these units will sell at $600/sf or within any defined timeline. If the units remain vacant for 12 or more months, the $600/sf assumption becomes increasingly speculative, and the developer may be forced to accept $450/sf or lower in any case — eroding the Strategy B premium.

**Lender pressure.** If the construction loan maturity is approaching and the lender is requiring full repayment of the CRU component's allocated loan balance, the developer may not have the runway to execute individual strata sales. A single bulk sale — even at a discount — delivers the loan repayment proceeds more quickly and more certainly.

## AE-15.3 — Joint Venture Exit and Shotgun Clause Analysis

### Scenario

A developer (GP) and an institutional LP investor formed a joint venture to develop a 22-storey, 195-suite purpose-built rental building in the City of Mississauga. The building was completed 18 months ago and has now reached stabilized occupancy of 97%. The joint venture agreement includes a shotgun provision that may be triggered by either party after the building reaches stabilization. The LP's investment mandate requires a disposition or recapitalization within 24 months of stabilization.

The stabilized NOI is $3,780,000. The GP's internal valuation model, using a 4.60% cap rate (reflecting the Mississauga submarket's modest discount to Toronto), indicates a value of $82,174,000. The LP's advisor has independently valued the building at $84,500,000 (using a 4.47% cap rate). The joint venture's outstanding construction loan balance is $51,000,000. Total equity invested is $16,000,000: LP $12,800,000 (80%), GP $3,200,000 (20%). The preferred return of 8.0% per annum, simple interest, has accrued over 3.75 years. The waterfall after preferred return is: 75% LP / 25% GP promote.

The GP believes that if the building were sold on the open market through a full competitive process, it would likely achieve between $83,000,000 and $85,000,000. The GP also believes that its best use of capital at this moment is to buy out the LP and own the building outright, as it intends to add the building to a portfolio it plans to contribute to a REIT in approximately four years.

### Questions

1. Calculate the accrued preferred return for the LP and GP, and the total distributable proceeds under a hypothetical sale at the midpoint of the competitive market estimate ($84,000,000), net of a 0.85% advisory fee and the construction loan repayment.
2. The GP is considering triggering the shotgun at $79,500,000 for the entire JV interest. Calculate what each party receives if the LP exercises its right to sell (i.e., the GP buys the LP's 80% interest) versus what each party would receive under the open market sale at $84,000,000. Explain the GP's strategic rationale.
3. If the LP decides to trigger the shotgun at $86,000,000 instead, what are the GP's options and what is the financial consequence of each?

---

## AE-15.3 — Solution

**Question 1 — Preferred return and waterfall at $84,000,000**

**Preferred return calculation (8.0% simple interest, 3.75 years):**
- LP: $12,800,000 × 0.080 × 3.75 = **$3,840,000**
- GP: $3,200,000 × 0.080 × 3.75 = **$960,000**
- Total preferred return: **$4,800,000**

**Net distributable proceeds at $84,000,000 gross sale price:**
Less advisory fee (0.85%): $84,000,000 × 0.0085 = $714,000
Net sale proceeds: $84,000,000 − $714,000 = $83,286,000
Less construction loan repayment: $83,286,000 − $51,000,000 = **$32,286,000** distributable to the JV

**Waterfall at $32,286,000 distributable:**
- Tier 1 — Return of capital ($16,000,000): Fully funded. ✓ Residual: $32,286,000 − $16,000,000 = $16,286,000
- Tier 2 — Preferred return ($4,800,000): Fully funded. ✓ Residual: $16,286,000 − $4,800,000 = $11,486,000
- Tier 3 — Promote at 75/25: LP receives $11,486,000 × 75% = $8,614,500; GP receives $11,486,000 × 25% = $2,871,500

**Total distributions at $84,000,000:**

| Party | Capital | Preferred Return | Promote | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LP (80%) | $12,800,000 | $3,840,000 | $8,614,500 | **$25,254,500** |
| GP (20%) | $3,200,000 | $960,000 | $2,871,500 | **$7,031,500** |
| **Total** | **$16,000,000** | **$4,800,000** | **$11,486,000** | **$32,286,000** |

**Question 2 — GP triggers shotgun at $79,500,000; LP elects to sell**

Under the shotgun at $79,500,000, the LP elects to sell its 80% JV interest to the GP. The LP receives 80% of the net JV equity value implied by the $79,500,000 shotgun price.

Net JV equity at the shotgun price:
Gross price: $79,500,000
Less advisory fee (not applicable — shotgun is a private party transaction, no advisor): $0
Less construction loan: $51,000,000
Net JV equity: $79,500,000 − $51,000,000 = $28,500,000

Under the shotgun mechanism, the $28,500,000 of net JV equity is distributed per the waterfall:
- Return of capital: $16,000,000 → LP: $12,800,000; GP: $3,200,000. Residual: $12,500,000
- Preferred return ($4,800,000): Fully funded. Residual: $7,700,000
- Promote (75/25): LP: $5,775,000; GP: $1,925,000

**LP total at shotgun price: $12,800,000 + $3,840,000 + $5,775,000 = $22,415,000**
**LP total at $84,000,000 open market: $25,254,500**

**LP shortfall from accepting shotgun: $25,254,500 − $22,415,000 = $2,839,500**

The LP receives $2.84 million less under the shotgun than it would under an open market sale at $84,000,000. This is the GP's strategic gain: by naming a shotgun price below what an open market process would achieve, the GP can acquire the LP's interest at a discount to market while the LP, facing its 24-month mandate timeline, may prefer the certainty and speed of the shotgun over the risk and timeline of a competitive process.

The GP's calculation: it acquires the LP's 80% interest for $22,415,000 (paid from its own balance sheet or new financing), then owns a building worth approximately $83–85 million outright. The GP's total basis in the building is the $22,415,000 paid to the LP plus its own $7,031,500 waterfall entitlement (which it retains as equity): total GP basis = approximately $29,446,500 for an asset worth $84,000,000 — implied acquisition premium of $54,553,500, or a going-in equity yield significantly above market. The REIT contribution plan makes the timing advantageous: four more years of NOI growth before the REIT IPO would increase the asset's contribution value further.

**Question 3 — LP triggers shotgun at $86,000,000; GP's options**

If the LP triggers the shotgun at $86,000,000, the GP must either: (a) accept the shotgun price and sell its 20% JV interest to the LP at the LP's named price; or (b) exercise its right to buy the LP's 80% JV interest at the same $86,000,000 price.

Net JV equity at $86,000,000: $86,000,000 − $51,000,000 = $35,000,000
Waterfall:
- Capital return: $16,000,000. Residual: $19,000,000
- Preferred return ($4,800,000): Fully funded. Residual: $14,200,000
- Promote: LP: $10,650,000; GP: $3,550,000

**If GP sells (LP buys GP's 20%):** GP receives $3,200,000 + $960,000 + $3,550,000 = **$7,710,000**
**If GP buys (GP acquires LP's 80%):** GP pays LP $12,800,000 + $3,840,000 + $10,650,000 = **$27,290,000**

The GP must decide whether it can fund the $27,290,000 required to buy out the LP at the $86,000,000 shotgun price. If it can, it achieves its REIT portfolio objective — owning the building outright at a total all-in basis of approximately $30.5 million (GP's $27.29 million LP buyout plus its own $3.21 million retained equity). If it cannot fund the buyout, it must accept the sell side — receiving $7,710,000 for its 20% JV interest and losing its ownership position in an asset it intended to contribute to its REIT. The LP has used the shotgun strategically to force either a clean exit at an aggressive valuation or a buyout that returns maximum capital — the LP's 24-month mandate is satisfied by either outcome at this price.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the full arc of the disposition and exit phase — the final strategic decision in a development project's lifecycle that converts the developer's remaining productive interests into liquidity, realized returns, and the platform for the next project. Disposition is not a single event but a framework of choices: sell outright at stabilization, refinance and hold for ongoing cash flow, recapitalize with a new equity partner, or execute a phased combination of paths depending on asset class and investor timeline.

Establishing a credible disposition value requires the application of multiple valuation methodologies — the direct capitalization method, the discounted cash flow model, price-per-suite comparables, and gross revenue multipliers — each capturing a different dimension of the asset's worth. The direct cap rate is the workhorse of stabilized asset valuation; the DCF is essential for assets with significant in-place versus market rent gaps or near-term capital requirements.

For developers choosing to hold purpose-built rental assets, CMHC's MLI Select program provides the most economically attractive permanent financing available — combining high LTV ratios, extended 50-year amortization, and competitive insured mortgage rates in a structure that can return most or all of the developer's original equity while preserving the asset for future appreciation and REIT contribution.

The investment sales process — engaging a capital markets advisor, preparing the offering memorandum, running a structured bid process, and managing due diligence through to closing — demands the same analytical precision as the project's original underwriting. The virtual data room, the financial model embedded in the OM, and the bid comparison schedule are the Senior Analyst's primary contributions to a disposition process that maximizes competitive tension and achieves the best available market price.

Commercial retail unit disposition requires a deliberate choice between individual strata sales — which maximize per-unit price through the owner-occupier premium — and portfolio sales to institutional buyers — which sacrifice per-unit price for speed, certainty, and administrative simplicity. The optimal strategy depends on the depth of the buyer market, the lease-up status of individual units, and the developer's timeline constraints relative to construction loan maturity.

Exit from the joint venture structure — including the dissolution of the project entity and the management of buy-sell mechanisms, ROFOs, and ROFRs — is a legal and financial exercise that must be driven by a thorough reading of the joint venture agreement and a precise financial model of each party's outcomes under every plausible scenario. Shotgun clauses are powerful instruments that reward the party with the stronger balance sheet and the clearer strategic vision; managing a shotgun scenario requires the Senior Analyst to model both sides of the transaction with equal care.

The tax framework of disposition — recaptured CCA, capital gains inclusion rates, section 97 rollovers, and HST on commercial dispositions — is the domain of the developer's tax advisor, but the Senior Analyst's financial model must reflect the correct after-tax cash flows for each disposition path or the analysis will produce misleading comparisons. A hold-versus-sell decision made on pre-tax figures that ignores a material recaptured CCA tax liability is not a real estate analysis — it is an accounting error with strategic consequences.

The developer who manages the disposition phase with the same analytical discipline applied to the acquisition and development phases will close out each project with investor relationships that are satisfied, balance sheet capacity that is restored, and a recorded financial outcome that demonstrates the value creation the team delivered. That track record — built project by project, disposition by disposition — is the foundation on which the next land acquisition is underwritten, the next investor relationship is formed, and the next project is launched.
