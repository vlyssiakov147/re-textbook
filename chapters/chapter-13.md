# Chapter 13: Condominium Registration, Closing & Occupancy

**Phase:** Construction through Post-Occupancy
**Prerequisite Chapters:** 4 (Pre-Development & Entitlements), 9 (Budget Management & Cost Control), 11 (Risk Management & Insurance), 12 (Marketing, Sales & Leasing)

---

> **In Practice — Senior Analyst Perspective**
> Condominium registration and the closing period are the events that convert years of capital deployment and construction activity into actual revenue. Everything the project has been building toward — the pre-sale program, the construction schedule, the deposit trust balance, the lender's interest reserve — comes to a head in a compressed window of legal, operational, and financial activity that must be coordinated precisely. As a Senior Analyst, you will not be the person filing the declaration with the Land Registry Office or managing the purchaser walkthrough schedule. You will be the person who knows whether the construction deliverables required for registration are on track, whether the closing schedule maps against the construction loan's maturity date, whether any closing defaults are materializing and at what financial cost, whether the final cost report reflects the true project position as closings occur, and whether the loan paydown schedule is proceeding as modelled. The registration and closing period is operationally dense and financially consequential. Delays here are not abstract schedule slippages — they are daily interest charges on a nine-figure construction loan, potential purchaser rescission events, and, in extreme cases, threats to the project's solvency. The Senior Analyst's job in this phase is to maintain the financial picture with precision and to surface risks early enough that the management team can respond before they become crises.

## 13.1 The Path from Substantial Performance to Condominium Registration

<div id="ol-ch13-0"></div>

The journey from the construction team's declaration of substantial performance to the legal event of condominium registration is not a single step — it is a sequential process involving multiple regulatory bodies, legal filings, third-party professionals, and municipal confirmations. Each step must be completed before the next can begin, and any delay at any stage extends the period during which the developer is carrying the building under construction financing without receiving closing proceeds. Understanding this sequence — and the conditions precedent at each stage — is essential for the Senior Analyst modelling the project's cash flow and loan repayment schedule.

### Substantial Performance

Under Ontario's *Construction Act* (formerly the *Construction Lien Act*), substantial performance is a defined legal milestone: the point at which the project is ready for use or is being used for its intended purpose, or when construction is sufficiently complete that the only remaining work consists of remedying minor deficiencies. The certificate of substantial performance is published in the Ontario Construction News, which begins the lien-period clock and triggers the holdback release process under the Act.

From a condominium development perspective, substantial performance is the milestone that starts the clock running toward occupancy and registration. It is the point at which the developer's insurance obligations transition from a builder's risk policy to a building owner's policy (addressed in Chapter 11), and it is the milestone against which the construction loan's stabilization provisions are often measured. For the Senior Analyst, the substantial performance date is the anchor from which several downstream financial events are timed: the construction lien holdback period, the beginning of the interim occupancy period for the first floors, and the outer boundary of the construction loan's term.

### Construction Lien Holdback Period

Under the *Construction Act*, the owner is required to retain a mandatory holdback of 10% of the value of work done and materials supplied throughout the construction period. This holdback cannot be released until the expiry of the lien period following publication of the substantial performance certificate. The standard lien period runs 60 days from the date of publication. During this period, any subcontractor or supplier with an unpaid claim may register a construction lien against the property.

For a high-rise condominium project, the holdback represents a significant dollar amount — on a $100 million hard cost project, the holdback is $10 million. This amount sits in the construction lender's holdback account and is not available to fund ongoing work or to be disbursed until the lien period expires without registered liens, or until all registered liens are vacated or bonded over.

The Senior Analyst tracks the holdback balance, the publication date of the substantial performance certificate, and the lien period expiry date as a cash flow item. The holdback release is a known future cash flow that should be modelled into the project's post-construction financial position. Any liens registered during the period require legal review and are flagged immediately to the development manager.

### Tarion Enrollments and Warranty Obligations

Before units can be occupied, the developer must confirm that the project's Tarion enrollment is current and that the enrollment covers all units in the building. Tarion administers the Ontario New Home Warranties Plan Act warranty program, which provides new condominium purchasers with statutory warranty coverage for defects in work and materials (one year), distribution systems (two years), and major structural defects (seven years).

The developer's Tarion enrollment obligations do not end at construction completion — they continue through the warranty period. However, from an occupancy-readiness standpoint, the relevant pre-occupancy Tarion obligation is the completion of all required pre-occupancy inspections and the processing of the PDI (Pre-Delivery Inspection) for each unit, which was addressed in Chapter 12. The Senior Analyst should confirm that the Tarion enrollment is in good standing and that no warranty registration issues are outstanding that could delay the issuance of occupancy permits.

### Occupancy Permits

The municipality's issuance of an occupancy permit (or, in some jurisdictions, a conditional occupancy permit pending minor outstanding items) is a prerequisite for any unit to be occupied. Occupancy permits are issued on a floor-by-floor or unit-group basis as construction is completed, beginning with the lower floors and progressing upward as the building is finished. The construction manager coordinates the occupancy permit applications with the building department, and the permits are typically issued following a building inspector's review confirming that the relevant floor or unit group meets the applicable Ontario Building Code requirements and is fit for occupancy.

The Senior Analyst tracks the occupancy permit schedule by floor against the projected interim occupancy schedule. Delays in obtaining occupancy permits — typically caused by outstanding construction deficiencies, outstanding inspection items, or backlog at the municipality's building department — directly delay the interim occupancy schedule, which in turn delays the condominium registration date and the closing schedule. Any floor or unit group where occupancy permits are lagging more than two weeks behind the projected schedule should be flagged as a potential registration delay.

### Site Plan and Development Agreement Clearance

Before the municipality will confirm that all conditions of the site plan approval and development agreement have been satisfied — a confirmation required as part of the condominium registration package — the developer must have completed all required public works obligations. These typically include the construction and acceptance of any required road improvements, streetscaping, municipal services connections, and on-site public realm features stipulated in the site plan agreement. The developer must also have paid all outstanding development charges, education levies, and any other municipal fees that were deferred to the occupancy or registration stage.

The Senior Analyst tracks the outstanding site plan obligations and their completion status as part of the overall project cost tracking. Development charges and education levies that were deferred to occupancy represent a known cash outflow at registration that must be modelled into the closing period cash flow. Failure to obtain site plan clearance is one of the most common causes of condominium registration delays, particularly on projects where some municipal works were left for completion concurrent with the final construction phase.

## 13.2 The Condominium Registration Process

<div id="ol-ch13-1"></div>

Condominium registration is the legal process through which the building's title is converted from a single fee simple property owned by the developer into a registered condominium with individually titled units. After registration, the developer no longer holds title to the units — each unit has its own title, which transfers to the purchaser at closing. The condominium corporation holds title to the common elements on behalf of all unit owners.

### The Registration Documents

The registration package filed with the Land Registry Office consists of several documents that must be prepared, reviewed, and approved before they can be filed:

**The Declaration** is the foundational constitutional document of the condominium corporation. It describes the property, establishes the unit boundaries, allocates the unit factors (the proportionate shares of common expenses and ownership of common elements), sets out the conditions of the corporation's governance, and includes any provisions relating to the developer's obligations to the corporation following registration. The declaration is prepared by the developer's solicitor and must comply with the *Condominium Act, 1998* in all material respects.

**The Description** is the survey-based document that establishes the legal boundaries of each unit and the common elements. The description is prepared by an Ontario Land Surveyor and includes floor plans showing unit boundaries measured from the as-built construction. The description must reflect the building as actually constructed — any material discrepancy between the as-built condition and the description will require correction before filing. The preparation of the description is one of the longest-lead items in the registration process; the surveyor cannot begin the as-built measurement work until the building is sufficiently complete, and the description preparation typically takes four to eight weeks after the measurement work is done.

**The Bylaws** govern the day-to-day operation of the condominium corporation. At the time of registration, the developer typically adopts initial bylaws that address the governance structure, the procedures for unit owner meetings, and the financial management of the corporation. The initial bylaws are prepared by the developer's solicitor and are subject to amendment by the unit owners at the first owner-controlled meeting of the corporation.

**The Budget Certificate** is a document prepared in connection with the condominium's initial budget that certifies the adequacy of the common expense contributions projected for the first year of the corporation's operation. Under the *Condominium Act, 1998*, the developer is required to contribute to the reserve fund for the first two years of the corporation's operation on terms set out in the Act, and the budget certificate reflects these obligations.

### The Land Registry Office Filing

Once the registration package is complete, the developer's solicitor files the declaration, description, and bylaws with the appropriate Land Registry Office. In Ontario, land registration is maintained through the Teranet electronic land registration system, and the filing is done electronically. The Land Registrar reviews the package and, if it meets all statutory requirements, registers the condominium, assigning a condominium plan number and individual PIN numbers for each unit.

The time from filing to registration can range from a few days to several weeks depending on the Land Registry Office's workload. The developer's solicitor should be in communication with the Land Registry Office throughout the pre-filing period to understand current processing times and to expedite the filing if possible. From the developer's perspective, every day between the filing date and the registration date is a day of additional construction loan interest without any closing proceeds being received.

### Post-Registration: Notices of Closing

Within a defined period after registration (governed by the terms of the purchase agreements), the developer is required to issue notices of closing to all purchasers. The notice of closing specifies the purchaser's closing date — the date on which the unit's title will be transferred and the purchase price balance (net of deposits already in trust) must be paid. Closing dates are typically scheduled on a staggered basis over a period of three to eight weeks following registration, with the developer's solicitor coordinating the closing schedule to manage the legal and administrative workload.

The notice period between the registration date and the scheduled closing dates varies by project but is typically 10 to 21 days for early closings, allowing purchasers' solicitors sufficient time to arrange the closing funds, confirm mortgage instructions from the purchasers' lenders, and complete the required title searches and title insurance.

## 13.3 The Interim Occupancy Period in Detail

<div id="ol-ch13-2"></div>

Chapter 12 introduced the interim occupancy period as the phase between a purchaser's occupancy of their unit and the closing that occurs after condominium registration. This chapter examines the mechanics of the interim occupancy period from the developer's operational and financial management perspective, including the interim occupancy fee calculation, the management of the building during interim occupancy, and the legal framework governing the developer's obligations to occupying purchasers.

### Interim Occupancy Fee Calculation

The interim occupancy fee is calculated under the prescribed formula set out in the *Condominium Act, 1998*:

> **Interim Occupancy Fee = Monthly Interest on Unpaid Purchase Price + Monthly Common Expense Contribution + Monthly Property Tax Instalment**

The **monthly interest on the unpaid purchase price** is calculated by applying the lower of (a) the interest rate stated in the purchase agreement for this purpose or (b) the prescribed rate (set from time to time under the Act's regulations, and currently based on the Bank of Canada's conventional mortgage rate) to the balance of the purchase price remaining unpaid — meaning the total purchase price less the deposits paid to date. Because deposits accumulate over the pre-construction period, the unpaid balance, and therefore the interest component, declines as deposits are paid.

The **monthly common expense contribution** is one-twelfth of the annual common expense budget for that unit as projected in the condominium's first-year budget. This is the amount the purchaser would pay as a condo fee after registration; during interim occupancy, it is collected by the developer as a component of the interim occupancy fee.

The **monthly property tax instalment** is the developer's estimate of the unit's monthly share of the property tax assessed on the condominium once registered. Prior to assessment, the developer uses an estimate based on comparable properties or the municipality's pre-registration advisory assessment.

The Senior Analyst prepares and maintains the interim occupancy fee schedule for each unit, updated monthly as deposit tranches are received and as common expense budget estimates are revised. The schedule is a line-item financial document tracking fee amounts, collection status, and any arrears for each occupying unit.

### Building Operations During Interim Occupancy

During the interim occupancy period, the developer is still the owner of the building and is responsible for its operation. The common elements must be maintained, elevators must be operational, utilities must be connected and paid, and security must be provided. The developer typically engages the designated property management company for the condominium to begin operating the building's common elements and shared systems from the date of first interim occupancy.

The cost of operating the building's common elements during the interim occupancy period is funded partly by the interim occupancy fees (the common expense component) and partly by the developer, to the extent that the common expense contribution is insufficient to cover actual costs in the early period before full occupancy. Thin interim occupancy fee collections — because only a few floors are initially occupied — may require the developer to fund operating shortfalls from the construction loan's operating reserve or from equity. The Senior Analyst tracks the operating budget versus actual for the building's common areas during the interim occupancy period and monitors the sufficiency of the operating reserve.

### Purchaser Obligations and Developer Rights During Interim Occupancy

The *Condominium Act, 1998* establishes specific rules governing both the developer's and the purchaser's obligations during interim occupancy. The developer must maintain the unit in a habitable condition, address deficiencies identified in the PDI on a timely basis, and ensure that building systems are operational. The purchaser is responsible for the care of the interior of the unit, must pay the prescribed interim occupancy fee on time, and is subject to the building's rules and regulations as if the condominium had already been registered.

Importantly, the developer's right to collect interim occupancy fees is not a tenancy relationship — the *Residential Tenancies Act, 2006* does not apply to interim occupancy arrangements, and the developer cannot be characterized as a landlord in this context. This distinction matters because it means that the developer's enforcement rights against a purchaser who fails to pay interim occupancy fees are governed by the purchase agreement and the *Condominium Act*, not by the eviction provisions of the RTA. The purchase agreement typically provides that default in interim occupancy fee payments is an event that entitles the developer to treat the purchaser as being in default of the purchase agreement, potentially triggering the deposit forfeiture provisions.

### Interim Occupancy Period Length and Extension Risk

The length of the interim occupancy period — the time between first occupancy and condominium registration — is one of the most variable elements of a high-rise condominium project's schedule. Registrations can occur as quickly as three to four months after first occupancy on a well-organized project with an experienced solicitor and a straightforward registration package, or can extend to twelve months or more on complex projects, projects with outstanding site plan obligations, or projects where the description preparation was delayed.

The financial cost of an extended interim occupancy period is real and material. Each additional month of interim occupancy is a month during which the construction loan is outstanding (at full or near-full balance, because no closing proceeds have yet been received), the developer is managing building operations, and the purchasers are in possession but no revenue has been recognized. An interim occupancy period that runs three months longer than projected adds three months of construction loan interest — potentially several million dollars on a large project — to the total project cost. The Senior Analyst models the financial sensitivity of the project's returns to interim occupancy period length and maintains a monthly forecast of the expected registration date as the registration process progresses.

## 13.4 The Closing Process

<div id="ol-ch13-3"></div>

The closing of individual condominium units — the transactions through which each purchaser takes title and pays the balance of the purchase price — is the financial engine of the project's revenue realization. All of the deposit trust accumulation, the pre-sale activity, and the construction period investment culminate in the closing schedule, and the Senior Analyst's financial modelling, tracking, and reporting duties are at their most intensive during this period.

### The Mechanics of a Condominium Closing

A condominium unit closing in Ontario follows the same basic structure as any residential real estate transaction, with some features specific to the condominium context:

**The Statement of Adjustments** is the financial accounting document prepared by the developer's solicitor that calculates the net amount payable by the purchaser at closing. The statement starts with the purchase price, deducts the deposits held in trust (credited to the purchaser), and adds any amounts owing by the purchaser — adjustments for prepaid property taxes, any outstanding interim occupancy fee arrears, the purchaser's pro-rated contribution to the condominium's reserve fund as required by the Act, and any upgrade charges. The balance is the amount the purchaser must bring to closing, typically funded by a mortgage advance and/or personal funds.

**Mortgage Instructions and Mortgage Advances**: The majority of purchasers close with a mortgage. The purchaser's lender will have issued a commitment letter, and at closing, the lender's solicitor (often the same as the purchaser's solicitor) will advance the mortgage funds on the condition that title is transferred, the charge is registered on title, and all conditions of the mortgage commitment are satisfied. The developer's solicitor and the purchaser's solicitor coordinate the flow of mortgage funds, deposit release from trust, and any additional funds required to close.

**Title Transfer and Registration**: The transfer of title is registered electronically through the Teranet system simultaneously with the mortgage charge. The developer transfers title to the purchaser, the purchaser's mortgage is registered as a charge on title, and the dealing is completed. From this moment, the developer has no further interest in that unit — it belongs to the purchaser (subject to the mortgage).

**Construction Loan Repayment**: The closing proceeds flow to the construction lender as partial repayment of the outstanding construction loan balance. The construction lender will have provided the developer's solicitor with partial discharge conditions — the specific amount to be paid on the closing of each unit, which represents that unit's allocated share of the loan. This partial discharge amount is deducted from the purchaser's net closing funds before the balance is released to the developer.

### The Closing Schedule and the Construction Loan Maturity Date

The construction loan has a maturity date — the date by which the full outstanding balance must be repaid. In most Ontario construction loan structures, the maturity date is a defined period after substantial performance or after the first closing, and the loan is expected to be retired through the closing proceeds as units close. A well-structured closing schedule closes enough units quickly enough to retire the construction loan before or at its maturity date.

The Senior Analyst maintains a detailed closing schedule model that maps each unit's expected closing date, the expected closing proceeds (net purchase price less deposits already in trust), the construction loan's partial discharge amount for each unit, and the projected outstanding loan balance after each closing day. The model produces a cash flow forecast showing the trajectory of loan repayment and the projected date of full loan retirement, which is compared against the loan's maturity date.

If the model shows that the loan will not be retired by its maturity date — because closings are slower than projected, defaults are higher than expected, or the registration date was delayed — the developer must either negotiate an extension with the construction lender (which typically comes with an extension fee and a rate premium) or accelerate the closing schedule to close units more quickly. The Senior Analyst surfaces this risk well in advance of the maturity date — ideally with 60 to 90 days of runway — so that the development manager can engage the lender before the issue becomes a default.

### Adjustments, Deficiencies, and Holdbacks at Closing

Purchasers who have identified unit deficiencies — items not completed or not conforming to the specifications in the purchase agreement — may seek to hold back a portion of the closing funds pending the developer's completion of the outstanding work. The purchase agreement's terms regarding closing holdbacks vary; many developer-friendly agreements contain broad "as is" closing clauses that limit the purchaser's right to withhold funds. However, in practice, some purchasers will refuse to close on units with material outstanding deficiencies, creating the risk of a closing delay.

The developer's construction and customer service teams should be proactively managing the deficiency list for each unit in advance of the scheduled closing, using the PDI deficiency list as the baseline. Units with outstanding deficiencies that cannot be cleared before the scheduled closing date present a closing risk that the Senior Analyst should flag. The cost of addressing deficiencies — whether through construction work, cash payments to purchasers in lieu of repairs, or post-closing trust holdbacks agreed with purchasers' solicitors — is a project cost that must be tracked against the contingency reserve.

### Upgrade Revenue Collection at Closing

Purchasers who selected finish upgrades above the base package are obligated to pay the upgrade cost at closing, as an additional adjustment on the statement of adjustments. Upgrade revenue is typically a meaningful incremental revenue line — on a large project, it can represent several million dollars in additional gross revenue above the base unit prices. The Senior Analyst tracks the upgrade charge schedule by unit, confirms that upgrade agreements are fully executed, and ensures that the closing proceeds include the upgrade amounts where applicable. Unexecuted upgrade agreements or disputes about upgrade charges are flagged before the unit's closing date so that the developer's solicitor can address them in the statement of adjustments preparation.

## 13.5 The Condominium Corporation: Turnover and First Year Operations

<div id="ol-ch13-4"></div>

Upon registration, the condominium corporation comes into existence — but the developer, as the initial owner of all units, is initially the sole member of the corporation. As closings occur and purchasers take title, they become unit owners and members of the corporation. The *Condominium Act, 1998* prescribes a process by which control of the corporation transitions from the developer to the unit owners, and the developer has specific obligations to the corporation and its members in the period following registration.

### Developer Control and the Turnover Meeting

During the period immediately following registration, the developer (or its nominee) acts as the initial board of directors of the condominium corporation, as permitted by the Act. This is a temporary arrangement — the Act requires the developer to call the turnover meeting within a specified period after the corporation achieves a certain threshold of unit owner membership.

Under the *Condominium Act, 1998*, the developer must call the **turnover meeting** (formally, the first meeting of owners) within 21 days of the date on which 20% of the units (by unit factor) have been transferred to purchasers. At the turnover meeting, the unit owners elect their own board of directors, and control of the corporation passes from the developer to the owners. The developer is required to deliver to the new board a package of documents and materials — the "turn-over documents" — that enables the owners to govern the corporation.

### Turn-Over Documents and Developer Obligations

The package of documents the developer must deliver at or following the turnover meeting includes: the original executed copies of the declaration, description, and bylaws; all contracts affecting the common elements; all insurance policies; all financial records of the corporation from the registration date; all warranty and service contracts for building equipment and systems; all as-built drawings and specifications; all operating and maintenance manuals; and the corporation's reserve fund study (if one has been completed).

The as-built drawings are of particular importance and are a common source of disputes. Purchasers, property managers, and the corporation's property management company all rely on the as-built drawings to understand the building's systems, maintain them, and manage capital repairs. Incomplete or inaccurate as-built drawings generate ongoing problems for the corporation and can expose the developer to warranty claims. The Senior Analyst should confirm that the construction manager's as-built documentation obligation is captured in the construction contract and that its delivery is tracked as a project close-out requirement.

### The Reserve Fund Study

The *Condominium Act, 1998* requires that a reserve fund study be conducted within specified timescales following registration. The reserve fund study is an engineering assessment of the building's major common elements and systems, their estimated remaining useful lives, and the amount of capital contributions required over a 30-year horizon to fund major repairs and replacements without special assessments to unit owners.

The developer has an obligation under the Act to fund the reserve fund during the initial two-year period following registration, at a level that is either consistent with the reserve fund study or at the statutory minimum prescribed by the Act. The reserve fund contribution obligation is a post-registration cash flow obligation for the developer that continues until the corporation has sufficient owner-controlled operations to fund the reserve independently. This obligation must be modelled in the project's post-registration cost tracking and not overlooked as a project close-out item.

### The Section 43 Disclosure and Initial Budget

Under Section 43 of the *Condominium Act, 1998*, the developer is required to prepare and deliver to purchasers (at or before occupancy) an audited first-year operating budget for the condominium corporation. This budget sets the common expense contributions for the first year and is the basis for purchasers' pro-rated contributions at closing.

A common source of dispute between developers and condominium corporations is the adequacy of the initial budget — specifically, whether the common expense contributions set at registration are sufficient to fund actual operating costs. Where the initial budget understates operating costs, the corporation faces a shortfall in the first year, which may require either a special assessment to unit owners or a supplement from the developer. Developers who set unrealistically low initial budgets to make the project more attractive to purchasers often face legal action from the condominium corporation for the resulting shortfall. The Senior Analyst should review the initial budget for reasonableness against comparable operating cost benchmarks and flag any budget categories that appear materially understated.

## 13.6 Financial Management of the Closing Period

<div id="ol-ch13-5"></div>

The closing period — the weeks and months during which individual units close following condominium registration — is the most financially concentrated period of the project's lifecycle. The Senior Analyst's closing period responsibilities span cash flow forecasting, loan paydown tracking, cost report finalization, and project completion accounting.

### The Closing Cash Flow Model

The closing cash flow model is the primary financial tool for managing the closing period. It is built at the unit level — each of the project's units is a row in the model, with its scheduled closing date, purchase price, deposit amount (the total deposits paid into trust), the partial discharge amount for the construction loan, and the net proceeds flowing to the developer after loan repayment. The model is aggregated to produce a weekly and monthly cash flow forecast showing the expected trajectory of loan repayment and the projected release of equity from the project.

The model is updated weekly throughout the closing period as actual closings are confirmed, defaults materialize, extensions are granted, and re-sales are initiated for defaulted units. A static model built at registration and not updated as the closing period progresses is a liability — it will diverge from reality quickly and provide misleading information to the development manager and lender.

Key metrics tracked in the closing cash flow model include:

**Cumulative closings to date** versus the schedule: how many units have closed against the projected number at this point in the closing schedule.

**Loan outstanding balance** after each closing day versus the model's projected balance: is the loan being paid down on pace?

**Default count and estimated re-sale timeline**: for each defaulted unit, the estimated date of re-sale and the projected net proceeds (purchase price of re-sale less forfeited deposit less re-sale marketing costs), which determines when those units will contribute to the loan paydown.

**Projected loan retirement date** versus maturity date: the key risk metric. If the loan retirement date is tracking later than maturity, escalation is required.

### Final Cost Report and Project Completion Accounting

The closing period is also when the project's final cost report is prepared and the project's overall financial performance is calculated against the original pro forma. As the last costs are incurred and the last closings occur, the Senior Analyst produces the final cost report — a comprehensive reconciliation of every budget line item from pre-development through project close-out, including all hard costs, soft costs, financing costs, marketing and sales costs, and carrying costs during the interim occupancy and closing periods.

The final cost report is compared against the original pro forma and against the most recent budget update to identify variances. Major variance categories typically include: construction cost overruns or savings versus the GMP contract, financing cost variances due to project schedule changes (the most common source of overrun on large projects), marketing and sales cost variances, and carrying cost variances from extended interim occupancy or closing periods.

The project's gross margin — total closing proceeds less total project costs — is calculated and compared against the pro forma gross margin. The project's return on equity (ROE) and internal rate of return (IRR) are calculated for comparison against the original underwriting targets and are included in the project completion report delivered to the developer's principal and equity investors.

### Warranty Reserves and Post-Closing Contingencies

Even after the final closing, the developer retains financial obligations arising from the Tarion warranty program. Warranty claims from unit owners and the condominium corporation — particularly for major structural defects (covered for seven years under the statutory warranty) — can produce material costs years after the project is complete. Prudent developers maintain a warranty reserve — a post-closing contingency set aside from the project's closing proceeds — to fund anticipated warranty costs without requiring additional equity contributions.

The Senior Analyst's role in warranty reserve management is to assist in sizing the reserve based on the project's construction quality record (deficiency history, PDI outcomes, post-occupancy complaint volumes) and comparable warranty cost experience on the developer's prior projects. A warranty reserve that is too thin will require equity contributions when claims materialize; a reserve that is too large ties up capital unnecessarily. A range of 0.5% to 1.5% of total construction cost is commonly encountered as a starting point for warranty reserve sizing, calibrated upward for projects with known construction quality issues.

## 13.7 Purchaser Default Management in the Closing Period

<div id="ol-ch13-6"></div>

Chapter 12 introduced the concept of purchaser default at closing and the developer's remedies. This chapter examines the operational and financial management of defaults as they occur in a closing program, including the deposit forfeiture process, the re-sale strategy, and the financial modelling of default scenarios.

### Identifying and Declaring Default

A purchaser is in default when they fail to tender the required closing funds (or to appear through their solicitor for closing) on the scheduled closing date. The process of formally declaring default typically involves the developer's solicitor making a formal tender of the transfer document on the closing date and the purchaser's solicitor failing to deliver the balance of the purchase price. The developer's solicitor then documents the tender and the failed closing for the purpose of supporting the developer's claim to the forfeited deposit.

Not every closing that does not occur on the scheduled date is a true default — some "soft" missed closings occur because of minor documentation issues, delayed mortgage advances, or administrative errors that are resolved within one to three business days. The Senior Analyst distinguishes between soft misses (which resolve quickly and without financial consequence) and hard defaults (where the purchaser is not closing) by tracking the status of each delayed closing in detail and communicating daily with the developer's solicitor during the closing period.

### Deposit Forfeiture

Upon a confirmed default, the developer is entitled to forfeit the purchaser's deposit. The developer's solicitor sends a direction to the trust solicitor (the solicitor holding the deposits in trust) directing release of the deposit to the developer. The deposit is released from trust and recorded as a receipt — it is not revenue from a unit sale but rather a recovery that offsets the carrying cost of the defaulted unit pending re-sale.

The accounting treatment of forfeited deposits requires care. The forfeited deposit is not recognized as unit sale revenue — it is a partial recovery that must be netted against the cost of the default (additional carrying costs, re-sale marketing costs, and any price discount on re-sale). The Senior Analyst tracks each defaulted unit as a separate line item in the project's closing tracking, recording the deposit forfeiture, the estimated re-sale timeline, and the projected re-sale price, and netting these to arrive at the unit's projected net contribution to the project's total revenue.

### Re-Sale Strategy and Pricing

Once a unit has been defaulted and the deposit forfeited, the developer must re-sell the unit as quickly as practicable to minimize the carrying cost of the vacant unsold inventory. Re-selling a completed unit (a registered condominium unit with a clear title that can be transferred immediately) is a different commercial exercise from selling a pre-construction unit — the buyer is getting an immediate product, not a future promise, and the market of potential buyers includes both end-users looking to move in quickly and investors looking for an income-producing property from day one.

The re-sale strategy should be determined at the time the default is confirmed, not after weeks of inaction. The key decisions are: pricing (should the re-sale price match the original purchase price, or should it be adjusted to current market conditions?), marketing channel (should the unit be listed through the co-operating broker network, offered first to a curated investor group, or offered with an inducement to adjacent purchasers who might want a larger combined space?), and timing (immediate re-listing versus a short strategic delay to assess whether the default is an isolated event or part of a broader pattern).

The Senior Analyst supports the re-sale decision by modelling the financial outcome under different pricing and timing scenarios. The model should compare: (a) the forfeited deposit amount retained plus the expected re-sale proceeds, versus (b) the original purchase price, netted against the additional carrying costs incurred from the default date to the re-sale closing date. In a flat or declining market, the net recovery on a defaulted unit can be meaningfully below the original purchase price even after the deposit is forfeited; in a rising market, the combination of forfeited deposit plus higher re-sale proceeds can produce a net outcome for the developer that is equal to or better than the original purchase.

### Default Rate Benchmarking

The Senior Analyst should track the project's default rate against the range assumed in the project's risk scenario analysis (addressed in Chapter 11 and Chapter 12) and against the developer's historical default experience on comparable projects. Default rates that are tracking above the modelled range — particularly if they are concentrated in specific suite types (such as investor-heavy smaller units) or specific floors (which may indicate a product or building quality issue) — should trigger immediate analysis and a revised financial model update. A default rate that is material relative to the project's construction loan paydown schedule may require the developer to engage the construction lender proactively with a recovery plan.

## 13.8 Post-Registration Obligations to the Condominium Corporation

<div id="ol-ch13-7"></div>

The developer's obligations do not end when the last unit closes. For several years following registration, the developer remains exposed to the condominium corporation and its unit owners through warranty obligations, reserve fund contributions, and potential litigation arising from construction defects or budget disputes. The Senior Analyst's role in post-registration matters is primarily financial — tracking outstanding obligations, maintaining reserves, and quantifying the developer's residual exposure.

### Tarion Warranty Claims Management

Post-registration warranty claims arise when unit owners identify defects in their units or in the common elements that they believe are covered under the Tarion statutory warranty. Claims are submitted through the Tarion online portal and processed according to Tarion's claims adjudication process. The developer is notified of each claim and has an opportunity to remedy the defect before Tarion steps in.

The Senior Analyst tracks all outstanding warranty claims, their estimated remediation cost, and whether the estimated cost falls within the warranty reserve. A pattern of claims in a specific building system — mechanical, roofing, envelope, or structural — is a signal that a systemic deficiency may exist, which could produce a large number of claims and a warranty cost significantly exceeding the reserve. In that scenario, the developer must either fund the warranty costs from the project's residual equity or, if the warranty reserve is held in a separate account, draw on the reserve.

### Reserve Fund Obligations

As noted in Section 13.5, the developer's obligation to contribute to the condominium corporation's reserve fund continues for the initial two-year period following registration. The reserve fund contribution is calculated based on the reserve fund study or the statutory minimum, whichever is greater. The Senior Analyst tracks the reserve fund contribution schedule and confirms that payments are being made on time, as failure to make required contributions can give the condominium corporation a legal claim against the developer.

### Section 21 Deficiency Contributions

Under the *Condominium Act, 1998*, if the developer's initial first-year budget for the condominium corporation is found to have understated operating costs, the developer may be required to contribute to the corporation's operating fund to make up the shortfall — a provision referred to in practice as a Section 21 contribution. The mechanism and conditions for this obligation are technical and the subject of ongoing legal interpretation, but the Senior Analyst should be aware that the initial budget's accuracy has financial consequences beyond the first year and that a Section 21 contribution obligation is a real post-registration cost risk on projects where the initial budget was set conservatively low.

## 13.9 Common Failure Modes in the Registration and Closing Period

<div id="ol-ch13-8"></div>

The registration and closing period concentrates a large number of interdependent tasks into a compressed timeframe, and failures at any point can create cascading financial and legal consequences. The following patterns of failure recur across projects and market cycles.

**1. Underestimating the registration timeline.** Developers who plan their construction loan maturity date based on an optimistic registration timeline — assuming registration will occur within 60 to 90 days of substantial performance — frequently find themselves in extension negotiations with their construction lender when registration takes five to eight months. The registration timeline is not fully within the developer's control: it depends on the description preparation, the site plan clearance, and the Land Registry Office's processing time. Preventive discipline: build a conservative registration timeline into the construction loan term (allowing at least six months from substantial performance to registration), maintain weekly status meetings with the registration solicitor, and begin the description preparation as early as construction completeness permits.

**2. Missing the construction lien holdback release.** Failing to track the publication of the substantial performance certificate and the resulting lien period expiry — or failing to confirm that no liens have been registered before directing the holdback release — can produce a holdback release into a liened property, which creates a serious legal and financial problem. Preventive discipline: the Senior Analyst should have the lien period expiry date calendared from the date of substantial performance publication, should coordinate with the construction solicitor on lien search procedures at expiry, and should never authorize holdback release without written confirmation from the construction solicitor that the title is clear.

**3. Inadequate pre-closing unit preparation.** Units that have outstanding deficiencies on their closing date — because the construction punch-list was not completed on schedule — create closing day crises that can delay closings, generate purchaser holdback requests, and damage the developer's reputation with purchasers and their solicitors. Preventive discipline: the construction manager should be tracking unit-by-unit completion status against the closing schedule at least 30 days before each unit's closing date, and deficiencies that cannot be resolved before closing should be identified early so that the developer's solicitor can structure appropriate holdback arrangements.

**4. Closing schedule misalignment with the loan maturity date.** A closing schedule that is back-loaded — with most closings in the final weeks of the scheduled period — creates a dangerous dependency on the late closings and leaves no buffer for defaults, extensions, or administrative delays. If even a handful of late-scheduled closings fail, the loan may not be retired by maturity. Preventive discipline: the closing schedule should be front-loaded to the extent possible, with the developer's solicitor working to close the largest units (producing the largest partial discharges) earlier in the schedule.

**5. Failing to coordinate the statement of adjustments with upgrade revenue.** Purchasers who selected upgrades but whose upgrade agreements were not fully documented and signed before closing will dispute upgrade charges at the closing statement of adjustments. Closings delayed or disputed because of upgrade documentation failures are preventable with basic file management. Preventive discipline: the development manager should confirm, at least 30 days before each unit's closing date, that all upgrade agreements are fully executed and that the upgrade amounts are confirmed with the designer or the construction manager.

**6. Section 43 budget understatement.** Setting the initial condominium budget unrealistically low — to make common expense projections look attractive to purchasers — creates a reserve fund shortfall and potential Section 21 liability. Preventive discipline: prepare the initial budget with the project's property management company using realistic operating cost benchmarks, and resist the temptation to understate costs for marketing purposes.

**7. Delayed as-built documentation delivery.** Condominium corporations that do not receive complete as-built drawings and system manuals at turnover face operational difficulties and are more likely to bring warranty claims and legal actions against the developer to compel delivery. Preventive discipline: make as-built documentation delivery a contract obligation of the construction manager, with a holdback tied to its timely and complete delivery.

**8. Reserve fund sizing without an independent study.** Developers who size the initial reserve fund contribution based on their own judgment rather than on an independent reserve fund study — or who use a study prepared by a consultant with a history of underestimating long-term capital needs — expose themselves to reserve fund shortfall claims from the corporation. Preventive discipline: engage a qualified independent reserve fund study provider early enough that the study is complete before the first-year budget is finalized.

**9. Ignoring the turnover meeting timeline.** Developers who delay calling the turnover meeting past the statutory deadline create legal exposure to the corporation's new board, which may have grounds for legal action. The turnover meeting also triggers the deadline for delivering the turn-over document package; an unprepared developer who has not assembled the package in advance will face an embarrassing and legally risky shortfall at turnover. Preventive discipline: calendar the turnover meeting obligation from the date of the 20% unit transfer threshold and begin assembling the turn-over document package at registration.

**10. Insufficient closing day legal capacity.** A closing schedule that attempts to close dozens of units on the same day overwhelms the developer's solicitor's office capacity and the Land Registry Office's processing capacity, creating administrative delays that can push closings into the next business day and trigger interest charges or purchaser complaints. Preventive discipline: the closing schedule should be structured to close no more than the developer's solicitor's office can process in a single day (typically 20 to 30 closings for a well-staffed office), spread over the scheduled closing period.

## 13.10 Key Metrics and Terms — Reference Table

<div id="ol-ch13-9"></div>

The following table summarizes the metrics, instruments, and terminology introduced in this chapter.

| Metric / Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Substantial Performance** | The statutory milestone under Ontario's *Construction Act* at which the project is sufficiently complete that remaining work consists only of minor deficiencies; triggers the lien period clock and holdback release process. |
| **Construction Lien Holdback** | The mandatory 10% retention on all construction payments required under the *Construction Act*; held until expiry of the lien period following the publication of the substantial performance certificate. |
| **Lien Period** | The 60-day period following publication of the substantial performance certificate during which subcontractors and suppliers may register construction liens against the property. |
| **Tarion** | Ontario's new home warranty regulator and insurer; administers the statutory warranty program covering new condominium units for defects in work and materials (one year), distribution systems (two years), and major structural defects (seven years). |
| **Occupancy Permit** | The municipal permit confirming that a floor or unit group meets the applicable building code requirements and is fit for occupancy; issued on a floor-by-floor basis as construction is completed. |
| **Site Plan Clearance** | Municipal confirmation that all obligations under the site plan approval and development agreement — public works, municipal fees, development charges — have been fulfilled; required as part of the condominium registration package. |
| **Declaration** | The foundational constitutional document of the condominium corporation, establishing unit boundaries, unit factors, and governance conditions; prepared by the developer's solicitor and filed at Land Registry as part of the registration package. |
| **Description** | The survey-based document prepared by an Ontario Land Surveyor establishing the as-built legal boundaries of each unit and common element; filed at Land Registry as part of the registration package. |
| **Condominium Registration** | The filing of the declaration, description, and bylaws with the Land Registry Office that creates the condominium corporation and individual unit titles, enabling closings to occur. |
| **Notice of Closing** | The formal notice issued by the developer to each purchaser following registration, specifying the purchaser's scheduled closing date. |
| **Statement of Adjustments** | The financial accounting document prepared for each unit closing, reconciling the purchase price against deposits in trust, property tax adjustments, reserve fund contributions, upgrade charges, and any interim occupancy fee arrears to produce the net amount payable by the purchaser at closing. |
| **Partial Discharge** | The specific repayment amount allocated to each unit from the construction loan outstanding balance; paid at each unit's closing to reduce the construction loan. |
| **Turnover Meeting** | The first meeting of condominium unit owners, required to be called by the developer within 21 days of 20% of units (by unit factor) being transferred to purchasers; the meeting at which owner-elected directors take control of the corporation. |
| **Turn-Over Documents** | The package of documents — declaration, description, bylaws, contracts, insurance policies, financial records, as-built drawings, and warranty materials — delivered by the developer to the new board of directors at or following the turnover meeting. |
| **Reserve Fund Study** | The engineering assessment of the condominium's major common elements and systems, their estimated useful lives, and the capital contributions required over a 30-year horizon to fund major repairs and replacements; required under the *Condominium Act, 1998* within specified timescales after registration. |
| **Section 43 Budget** | The audited first-year operating budget for the condominium corporation that the developer is required to prepare and deliver to purchasers; sets the initial common expense contribution levels. |
| **Section 21 Contribution** | A developer's obligation under the *Condominium Act, 1998* to contribute to the corporation's operating fund where the initial first-year budget understated actual operating costs. |
| **Interim Occupancy Fee** | The prescribed carrying cost fee paid by a purchaser during the interim occupancy period, calculated as the sum of interest on the unpaid purchase price balance, monthly common expense contribution, and monthly property tax instalment. |
| **Warranty Reserve** | A post-closing financial reserve maintained by the developer to fund anticipated warranty claims under the Tarion statutory warranty program; typically sized at 0.5% to 1.5% of total construction cost. |
| **Soft Miss** | A delayed unit closing caused by minor administrative or documentation issues that resolves within one to three business days without deposit forfeiture or legal process. |
| **Hard Default** | A confirmed purchaser default — the failure to close on the scheduled date — that triggers the developer's remedies under the purchase agreement, including deposit forfeiture and the right to re-sell the unit. |
| **Deposit Forfeiture** | The retention by the developer of the purchaser's trust deposit upon a confirmed closing default; a partial recovery against the cost of the default, netted against re-sale costs and any price differential on the re-sold unit. |
| **Closing Cash Flow Model** | The unit-level financial model tracking each unit's scheduled closing date, expected proceeds, partial discharge amount, and projected construction loan paydown; the primary financial tool for managing the closing period. |

## AE-13.1 — Registration Delay and Construction Loan Extension Analysis

### Scenario

You are the Senior Analyst on a 31-storey, 344-unit condominium project in the City of Toronto. The project achieved substantial performance 14 weeks ago. The construction loan has a current outstanding balance of $142,500,000, bearing interest at a floating rate of prime plus 1.50% (currently 7.25% per annum). The loan's maturity date is 52 weeks from the date of substantial performance.

The following registration status has been compiled by the development manager and the developer's solicitor:

| Task | Status |
|---|---|
| Substantial performance certificate published | Complete — 14 weeks ago |
| Construction lien period expiry | Complete — 5 weeks ago (no liens registered) |
| Holdback release from construction lender | Pending — solicitor confirmation received; release expected within 5 business days |
| Occupancy permits (floors 3–31) | Floors 3–22 issued; Floors 23–31 pending (outstanding mechanical inspection on floors 27–31) |
| Site plan clearance from City | Pending — outstanding item: streetscape plantings on east frontage not yet accepted by City |
| Ontario Land Surveyor — description preparation | Surveyor completed field measurements; description drafting in progress; estimated completion 6 weeks |
| Solicitor — declaration and bylaws draft | Draft complete; under developer review |
| Tarion enrollment status | Current; no outstanding issues |
| Estimated Land Registry filing date | Solicitor estimates 9 to 11 weeks from today |
| Closing schedule (post-registration) | Developer's solicitor projects a 7-week closing schedule once registration is achieved |

The developer had originally projected registration at week 18 from substantial performance (4 weeks from today). The pro forma modelled the construction loan being retired through closings by week 46 from substantial performance — 6 weeks before the loan's maturity date at week 52.

### Questions

1. Based on the current registration status, what is the revised projected registration date, and how does it compare to the original projection?
2. Re-model the loan retirement timeline under the revised registration estimate and assess whether the construction loan can be retired before its maturity date.
3. What are the most critical path items, and what actions should the development manager take to accelerate the registration timeline?

---

## AE-13.1 — Solution

**Question 1 — Revised registration date**

The solicitor's estimate of 9 to 11 weeks to Land Registry filing means registration will occur at approximately week 23 to 25 from substantial performance. Using the midpoint, the revised projected registration date is approximately **week 24 from substantial performance** — which is 10 weeks from today (given that today is week 14).

The original projected registration date was week 18 (4 weeks from today). The current estimate represents a **6-week delay** from the original projection. The outstanding critical path items — floors 23–31 occupancy permits (mechanical inspection), site plan clearance, and description preparation — all need to resolve before filing can occur. The 9-to-11-week estimate from the solicitor presumably reflects an assessment that these items will be resolved within that window; if any of them takes longer, the registration timeline extends further.

**Question 2 — Revised loan retirement timeline**

Original pro forma:
- Registration: week 18
- Closing schedule: 7 weeks (weeks 19–25)
- Loan retirement: approximately week 24–25
- Loan maturity: week 52
- Buffer: approximately 27 weeks

Revised projection:
- Registration: week 24 (midpoint of 23–25 range)
- Closing schedule: 7 weeks (weeks 25–31)
- Loan retirement: approximately week 30–31
- Loan maturity: week 52
- Buffer: approximately 21–22 weeks

Under the revised registration timeline, the loan is still projected to be retired approximately 21 to 22 weeks before maturity — a substantial buffer remains. The 6-week registration delay is financially meaningful in terms of interest cost but does not create a maturity risk.

**Financial cost of the 6-week delay:**

Daily interest on $142,500,000 at 7.25%: $142,500,000 × 0.0725 ÷ 365 = **$28,277 per day**

6-week delay × 42 days = **$1,187,634 in additional construction loan interest**

This is a real project cost impact — over $1.1 million in additional interest — that should be flagged in the cost report as an adverse variance to the financing cost budget line. The contingency reserve should be reviewed to confirm that it can absorb this overrun without requiring a budget amendment.

Note: As closings begin, the loan balance will decline rapidly, so the interest cost of the delay is front-loaded in the delay period (while the full balance is outstanding) and diminishes as closings occur.

**Question 3 — Critical path items and recommended actions**

The three items on the critical path are:

**Mechanical inspection, floors 27–31**: This is a construction manager responsibility. The development manager should request an immediate status meeting with the construction manager to understand what deficiencies are outstanding, whether the mechanical contractor has been given a remediation deadline, and whether an expedited re-inspection with the building department can be scheduled within the next two to three weeks. Any outstanding deficiencies should have a completion deadline set in writing.

**Site plan clearance — streetscape plantings**: The delay on east frontage plantings is typically a landscape contractor issue. The development manager should contact the landscape contractor to confirm the planting completion date and coordinate with the City's Site Plan Inspector to schedule an acceptance walk once planting is complete. If the planting season or weather is a factor (relevant if the project is in a late autumn or winter phase), the developer may need to discuss with the City whether a cash deposit in lieu of the planting can be accepted as a condition of clearance, with the planting deferred to the next appropriate season.

**Description preparation**: The Ontario Land Surveyor is in drafting; estimated completion is 6 weeks. The development manager should confirm with the surveyor that the 6-week estimate is firm and identify whether any additional resources (a second surveyor's review, faster turnaround services) can shorten the timeline. The developer's solicitor should be ready to review the description immediately upon receipt and to prepare the full filing package in parallel with the description finalization, so that the filing is ready to submit the day the description is completed.

The site plan clearance and mechanical inspection items are likely to resolve within the next three to four weeks if properly managed; the description is the binding constraint on the 9-to-11-week solicitor estimate. Accelerating the description — even by two weeks — would be worth prioritizing.

## AE-13.2 — Closing Period Default and Re-Sale Analysis

### Scenario

A 26-storey, 278-unit condominium project registered its condominium 3 weeks ago and has been in active closings. The construction loan outstanding at the start of closings was $96,400,000. The loan bears interest at 6.90% per annum. The closing schedule runs for 8 weeks; the loan's maturity is at week 10 (2 weeks after the end of the scheduled closing period, as a buffer).

At the end of week 3 of closings, 118 units have closed. The average purchase price across all 278 units is $712,000; deposits average 20% of purchase price per unit ($142,400 average deposit per unit). The construction loan partial discharge per unit is $346,000 (the loan balance divided by total units at the start of closings).

The following default and at-risk status has been compiled:

| Category | Units |
|---|---|
| Closed (title transferred, proceeds received) | 118 |
| Scheduled week 4–8, no issues identified | 138 |
| Confirmed defaults — purchaser notified, deposit forfeiture process initiated | 11 |
| Extension requested — 45 days beyond scheduled closing date | 7 |
| Non-responsive — no communication from purchaser or solicitor in 5+ business days | 4 |
| **Total** | **278** |

Re-sale analysis for the 11 confirmed default units: current comparable sales in the immediate submarket indicate that similar units are trading at approximately 4% below the original purchase prices of the defaulted units.

### Questions

1. What is the construction loan balance after week 3 closings, and does the projected base-case closing pace retire the loan before maturity?
2. For the 11 confirmed defaults, calculate the developer's estimated net financial impact (deposit retained less loss on re-sale, excluding carrying costs for simplicity).
3. What is the risk to the loan retirement date if the 7 extension units and 4 non-responsive units do not close within the scheduled 8-week closing period?

---

## AE-13.2 — Solution

**Question 1 — Loan balance after week 3 and base-case projection**

Loan balance at start: $96,400,000
Partial discharge per unit: $346,000
Units closed in weeks 1–3: 118

Loan repaid through closings: 118 × $346,000 = $40,828,000
**Remaining loan balance after week 3: $96,400,000 − $40,828,000 = $55,572,000**

Base case for weeks 4–8: 138 units scheduled with no issues identified, assumed to close as scheduled.

Loan repayment from remaining scheduled closings: 138 × $346,000 = $47,748,000

Remaining loan balance after all scheduled base-case closings: $55,572,000 − $47,748,000 = **$7,824,000**

This residual balance reflects the 11 confirmed defaults + 7 extensions + 4 non-responsive units (22 units total) whose partial discharges have not been received: 22 × $346,000 = $7,612,000 (approximate, noting the small rounding difference relates to whether all 278 units' discharge amounts sum exactly to the opening balance — confirming: 278 × $346,000 = $96,188,000, which is close to but not exactly $96,400,000 due to rounding in the per-unit discharge amount; treat as approximate).

The **base-case loan balance at the end of week 8 is approximately $7.8 million**, not zero. The loan is not fully retired at the end of the scheduled closing period under base-case assumptions. The maturity date is week 10 — 2 weeks after week 8 — meaning the developer has 2 additional weeks to resolve the outstanding units before a loan maturity breach.

**Question 2 — Net financial impact of confirmed defaults**

For each confirmed default unit:
- Average purchase price: $712,000
- Deposit forfeited (20%): $142,400 retained by developer
- Re-sale price at −4% discount: $712,000 × 0.96 = $683,520
- Re-sale net proceeds assume: no re-sale commission for simplicity (question specifies excluding carrying costs)

Net recovery per defaulted unit: $683,520 (re-sale) + $142,400 (forfeited deposit) = $825,920
Original purchase price: $712,000
**Net surplus per unit (before carrying costs): $825,920 − $712,000 = $113,920**

This result — positive net position — occurs because the forfeited deposit ($142,400) more than offsets the 4% price discount ($28,480). The developer retains the deposit and re-sells at a lower price, but the total recovery exceeds the original purchase price by the margin between the deposit amount and the price discount.

For 11 defaulted units: $113,920 × 11 = **$1,253,120 net surplus before carrying costs**

However, if carrying costs (construction loan interest on the un-discharged loan balance for these units during the re-sale period) are added back, the surplus will be reduced. At $346,000 per unit partial discharge, the total undischarged loan balance for 11 units is $3,806,000. At 6.90% per annum, the daily interest cost on this balance is approximately $720. If re-sales take an average of 90 days, the carrying cost is approximately $64,800 — materially reducing but not eliminating the net surplus.

**Question 3 — Risk from extension and non-responsive units**

7 extension units (45-day extension beyond scheduled closing) + 4 non-responsive units = 11 additional at-risk units.

If none of these 11 units close within the 8-week scheduled period, the residual loan balance at end of week 8 becomes approximately:

Units not closed beyond the 11 confirmed defaults: 11 additional × $346,000 = $3,806,000

Total loan balance at end of week 8: $7,824,000 (base) + $3,806,000 (additional at-risk) = **~$11,630,000**

The 7 extension units are requesting 45 additional days — they would close approximately at week 14 to 15 from the start of closings. The loan's maturity is at week 10. If the extensions are honoured and these 7 units close at week 14–15, the loan balance of roughly $2,422,000 attributable to those units will still be outstanding at the maturity date (week 10).

The developer must therefore address the maturity risk by week 10. Options:
- Negotiate a short-term loan extension with the construction lender for the outstanding balance, demonstrating that closings are imminent. The lender will likely agree given the small remaining balance relative to the original loan amount and the documented closing timeline.
- Fund the residual balance from equity or a bridge credit facility to retire the construction loan at maturity, then recover from the incoming closing proceeds.
- Accelerate the 4 non-responsive units through formal default and deposit forfeiture, eliminating their contribution to the residual balance and accepting the re-sale timeline risk on those units instead.

The quantitative analysis should be presented to the development manager with the week 10 maturity as the deadline and the ~$11.6M residual balance as the size of the potential maturity breach. A construction loan extension for 4 to 6 weeks on a $11.6M balance represents a modest financial cost (daily interest of approximately $2,200) relative to the cost of a formal loan default. The recommendation is to engage the construction lender proactively in week 6 or 7 of the closing period with the closing status report and the projected residual balance.

## AE-13.3 — Condominium Corporation Turnover and Initial Budget Analysis

### Scenario

A 19-storey, 186-unit condominium project registered its condominium 8 weeks ago. Unit factors are equal across all units (each unit holds 1/186 of the common interest). As of today, 52 units have transferred to purchasers. The developer's solicitor has advised that the turnover meeting obligation is approaching.

The developer's initial first-year operating budget for the condominium corporation was prepared 18 months ago during the pre-sales period. The budget projects annual common expenses of $1,020,000 — equivalent to a common expense contribution of $5,484 per unit per year ($457 per unit per month).

An independent property management company engaged to operate the building has now prepared its own first-year operating estimate. Their estimate is $1,294,000 annually, broken down as follows:

| Expense Category | Developer's Budget | Property Manager's Estimate | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Utilities (hydro, gas, water) | $182,000 | $241,000 | +$59,000 |
| Property Insurance | $94,000 | $118,000 | +$24,000 |
| Concierge and Security | $168,000 | $221,000 | +$53,000 |
| Cleaning and Janitorial | $74,000 | $89,000 | +$15,000 |
| Repairs and Maintenance | $98,000 | $136,000 | +$38,000 |
| Management Fees | $64,000 | $79,000 | +$15,000 |
| Landscaping and Snow Removal | $48,000 | $61,000 | +$13,000 |
| Reserve Fund Contribution | $160,000 | $182,000 | +$22,000 |
| Miscellaneous | $132,000 | $167,000 | +$35,000 |
| **Total** | **$1,020,000** | **$1,294,000** | **+$274,000** |

### Questions

1. Has the developer's statutory obligation to call the turnover meeting been triggered? Show your calculation.
2. Calculate the financial shortfall to the condominium corporation if the developer's initial budget is used rather than the property manager's estimate, and explain the developer's potential financial exposure.
3. What recommendation would you make to the development manager regarding the initial budget, and what are the practical steps to manage the exposure?

---

## AE-13.3 — Solution

**Question 1 — Turnover meeting obligation**

Under the *Condominium Act, 1998*, the developer must call the turnover meeting within **21 days** of the date on which 20% of the units (by unit factor) have been transferred to purchasers.

In this project, unit factors are equal, so 20% of the units by unit factor = 20% of 186 units = **37.2 units**, rounded to **38 units**.

Units transferred to purchasers as of today: **52 units** — which exceeds 38.

The 20% threshold was therefore crossed when the 38th unit transferred, which occurred before today. Depending on when the 38th closing occurred (which the Senior Analyst can determine from the closing schedule), the 21-day clock may already be running or may have already expired. **The turnover meeting obligation has been triggered.** The developer's solicitor must be contacted immediately to confirm when the 38th closing occurred, confirm the 21-day deadline, and if the deadline has not already passed, issue the turnover meeting notice without further delay.

If the 38th closing occurred more than 21 days ago, the developer may already be in technical breach of the statutory obligation to call the meeting, which is a serious compliance matter requiring immediate legal advice.

**Question 2 — Financial shortfall and developer exposure**

Annual shortfall: $1,294,000 (property manager's estimate) − $1,020,000 (developer's budget) = **$274,000 per year**

Monthly shortfall per unit: $274,000 ÷ 186 units ÷ 12 months = **$122.89 per unit per month**

If the corporation operates on the developer's budget and actual costs track the property manager's estimate, the corporation will have a first-year operating deficit of approximately $274,000. This deficit must be funded from somewhere — either from a special assessment to unit owners, from the reserve fund (which depletes the reserve), or from a contribution by the developer.

Under the *Condominium Act, 1998*, the developer's obligation to contribute to the operating fund shortfall (the Section 21 mechanism) is triggered if the corporation's first-year operating costs exceed the initial budget amounts within specific categories. The legal conditions and thresholds are technical and have been the subject of litigation; the developer's legal counsel should be consulted. However, the directional risk is clear: a $274,000 operating shortfall — approximately 26.9% above the budgeted amount — is a material discrepancy that is unlikely to be defensible if contested by the corporation.

The largest variances are in utilities (+$59,000), concierge/security (+$53,000), repairs and maintenance (+$38,000), and miscellaneous (+$35,000). The utilities variance may reflect higher actual energy prices than were assumed at the time the budget was prepared 18 months ago. The concierge and security variance may reflect actual staffing costs versus the developer's estimate.

**Question 3 — Recommendation and practical steps**

The recommendation to the development manager is to revise the initial budget to align with the property manager's estimate before the turnover meeting and before the first-year budget is finalized and distributed to purchasers. The primary reason is that using the understated budget creates a near-certain first-year shortfall that will produce unit owner complaints, potential Section 21 exposure, and damage to the developer's reputation with the corporation's new board.

The practical steps are:

First, request that the development manager and the developer's solicitor review the property manager's estimates against actual contract costs and current market benchmarks for each category. Some of the variance may be the property manager's conservatism rather than the developer's understatement. Reconcile the two estimates category by category.

Second, if the revised analysis confirms that the property manager's estimates are materially more accurate, prepare a revised Section 43 budget reflecting the higher costs and consult legal counsel on the process for updating the budget before or at registration, or at the turnover meeting. Purchasers should be notified of any material increase in projected common expenses.

Third, model the financial impact on the developer of the revised budget. Higher common expenses mean higher interim occupancy fees (the common expense component of the interim occupancy fee), which increases the monthly cost to occupying purchasers still in interim occupancy. This may generate purchaser complaints but is legally required — the developer cannot set a lower interim occupancy fee than the actual common expense contribution.

Fourth, ensure the reserve fund study is completed on the revised timeline and that the reserve fund contribution line in the revised budget reflects the study's recommendation rather than an estimate. The reserve fund shortfall in the developer's original budget ($22,000 below the property manager's estimate) is the category most likely to produce long-term financial harm to the corporation if understated.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the legal, operational, and financial mechanics of the final phase of an Ontario condominium development project — the period from substantial performance through condominium registration, unit closings, and post-registration obligations. The registration process involves a sequence of interdependent prerequisites — construction lien holdback release, occupancy permits, site plan clearance, description preparation, and solicitor filing — that must be managed concurrently to minimize the delay between substantial performance and the registration date that enables closings to begin. Every week of delay in that sequence is a week of construction loan interest on a nine-figure outstanding balance.

The interim occupancy period — the distinctive Ontario mechanism during which purchasers occupy their units before title transfers — creates a sustained period of building operation under the developer's ownership, with prescribed interim occupancy fees that partially offset carrying costs and a legal framework governing the developer's obligations to occupying purchasers. The closing period converts the project's deposit trust balances and purchase agreements into actual cash proceeds, retiring the construction loan and releasing the project's equity. The closing cash flow model — tracking each unit's scheduled closing, partial discharge amount, and actual closing status — is the primary financial management tool of this period.

The condominium corporation's establishment following registration, the turnover meeting obligation, the delivery of turn-over documents, and the developer's ongoing reserve fund contribution and warranty obligations extend the developer's financial exposure well beyond the last closing. The initial operating budget, the reserve fund study, and the Tarion warranty program each carry financial risk that must be actively managed and tracked against the project's post-closing cost accounting.

The Senior Analyst's role throughout this phase is to maintain the accuracy of the financial picture under a high volume of concurrent transactions, flag deviations from the loan retirement schedule with adequate lead time, quantify the financial cost of registration delays and closing defaults, and ensure that the project's completion accounting accurately reflects the final cost-versus-revenue outcome compared to the original pro forma. The discipline of this phase — methodical, detail-oriented, and deadline-driven — is the difference between a project that closes cleanly and one that becomes a protracted workout.

The next chapter — **Chapter 14: Project Completion, Accounting Close & Investor Reporting** — examines the post-closing activities that formally conclude the project's financial lifecycle: the final cost report, the project completion audit, the distribution of returns to equity investors, the reporting obligations under the project's investor agreements, and the developer's institutional knowledge capture and post-mortem review process.
