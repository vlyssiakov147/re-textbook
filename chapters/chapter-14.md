# Chapter 14: Project Completion, Accounting Close & Investor Reporting

**Phase:** Post-Closing through Wind-Down
**Prerequisite Chapters:** 4 (Pre-Development & Entitlements), 9 (Budget Management & Cost Control), 12 (Marketing, Sales & Leasing), 13 (Condominium Registration, Closing & Occupancy)

---

> **In Practice — Senior Analyst Perspective**
> The last closing on a condominium project is not the end of the Senior Analyst's work — it is the beginning of a distinct and analytically demanding phase that converts an active project file into a definitive financial record. Every assumption made in the original pro forma, every budget amendment approved during construction, every variance absorbed by the contingency reserve, and every scheduling delay that added to the financing cost line must now be reconciled against actual outcomes and presented in a form that is accurate, auditable, and useful to the principal, the equity investors, and the lender. This is the accounting close. In parallel, the developer's obligations to its equity partners — capital return, preferred return calculation, promote distribution, and formal reporting — must be discharged precisely in accordance with the joint venture or limited partnership agreement. Errors at this stage are not theoretical: a miscalculated preferred return shortchanges investors; an incomplete cost capture overstates the project's profit; a poorly documented project post-mortem forfeits the institutional knowledge that makes the next project cheaper and better. The Senior Analyst's role in this phase is to produce work that is defensible under audit, correct to the cent in investor distributions, and useful as a management tool for future underwriting. The reward for getting this phase right is a developer whose investors are informed and confident, whose lender relationship is clean, and whose own internal records are a reliable foundation for the next project.

## 14.1 The Post-Closing Financial Position

<div id="ol-ch14-0"></div>

With the last unit closed and the construction loan retired, the developer's attention shifts from managing active cash flows to accounting for completed ones. The project transitions from a live financial model — updated weekly with closing actuals, default resolutions, and loan balance tracking — to a closed financial record that must be reconciled, verified, and formally reported. This transition requires a methodical process of cost capture, revenue confirmation, and residual obligation accounting before any final distributions can be made.

### Confirming the Revenue Final Position

The first step in closing the project's financial records is confirming the total revenue received. On a condominium project, revenue sources typically include unit sale proceeds (the base purchase price), upgrade revenue collected at closing through the statement of adjustments, and net deposit forfeitures on defaulted units (forfeited deposits less re-sale marketing costs and any re-sale price adjustments). Parking and storage locker sales, if sold separately from units, are a fourth revenue line on many projects.

The Senior Analyst reconciles total revenue by tracing every unit's closing proceeds back to the statement of adjustments prepared for that closing, confirming that the proceeds received match the amounts recorded in the closing cash flow model. Any discrepancy — a missing upgrade charge, a closing adjustment not reflected in the model, or a deposit forfeiture that was recorded but not received — must be identified and resolved before the revenue position is finalized. The revenue reconciliation is a unit-level exercise; summary-level reconciliation is insufficient because it will not reveal unit-specific discrepancies.

HST (Harmonized Sales Tax) treatment requires particular attention in the revenue reconciliation. New residential condominium units in Ontario are subject to HST, but the majority of purchasers who occupy their units as a primary residence are entitled to claim the HST New Housing Rebate, which the developer typically advances on their behalf at closing in exchange for an assignment of the rebate. The developer's net revenue on each unit is the purchase price inclusive of HST less the assigned rebate amount. Units sold to investors who do not qualify for the rebate (or who assign to the developer a commercial rebate only) require a different HST treatment. The Senior Analyst confirms, for each unit, that the HST obligation and any rebate assignment are correctly reflected in the statement of adjustments and in the project's HST filing position.

### Confirming the Cost Final Position

Cost capture at project close requires a disciplined review of every cost category to confirm that all costs have been accrued and that no invoices, holdback releases, warranty reserves, or post-closing obligations have been omitted. The most common sources of incomplete cost capture at project close are: construction holdbacks not yet released, outstanding consultant invoices for post-construction deliverables (as-built drawings, occupancy-related inspections), deferred development charges or education levies, condominium corporation reserve fund contribution obligations, Tarion warranty reserve contributions, and professional fees for post-closing legal, accounting, and tax work.

The Senior Analyst prepares a post-closing cost accruals schedule — a line-item list of every outstanding cost obligation, the estimated amount, and the expected payment date — and confirms that each item is accrued in the project's cost records before the accounting close is finalized. Any item that cannot be estimated with reasonable certainty (for example, warranty claims that have not yet been quantified) should be noted as a contingent liability with the developer's best estimate, with a reserve funded from the project's residual proceeds before final distribution.

### The Final Cost Report

The final cost report is the comprehensive reconciliation of every budget line item from pre-development through project close-out. It is the definitive version of the budget tracking document that has been maintained throughout the project's lifecycle and updated at each project stage. The final cost report presents, for every budget category: the original pro forma budget, the most recent approved budget (reflecting all amendments), the actual cost incurred, and the variance between the approved budget and the actual cost. For each material variance, the report includes a brief explanatory note.

The final cost report is not simply an accounting document — it is a management analysis. Variances must be explained, not merely stated. A construction cost overrun of $2.3 million is not self-explanatory; it requires attribution (which trade, which scope change, which design revision, which unforeseen condition) so that the developer can understand whether the overrun was preventable and how to avoid a similar outcome on the next project. A financing cost saving of $800,000 because the project closed three weeks ahead of the revised schedule is equally meaningful — it tells the developer and its investors that the construction management team's acceleration effort at the end of the construction phase had a quantifiable financial payoff.

The Senior Analyst authors the final cost report with input from the construction manager (hard cost final accounts), the project's consultants (soft cost invoicing), the lender's loan administration (financing cost confirmation), and the developer's legal and tax advisors (closing and post-closing professional fees). The report is reviewed by the development manager and the developer's principal before it is distributed to investors.

## 14.2 Project Return Calculations

<div id="ol-ch14-1"></div>

With total revenue and total cost confirmed, the project's financial performance can be calculated against the original pro forma and the most recent underwriting. These calculations are the core of the project completion report delivered to the developer's equity investors and are the benchmark against which the development team's performance is evaluated.

### Gross Margin and Net Margin

**Gross margin** is total revenue less total project costs (hard costs, soft costs, financing costs, marketing and sales costs, and carrying costs through the closing period). It is the project's top-line profitability measure before accounting for the distribution of returns between the developer and its equity investors or the deduction of any income taxes.

**Net margin** is gross margin less any management fees, development fees, or advisory fees paid to the developer entity by the project entity (fees that are a return to the developer in its management capacity rather than its investor capacity), and less any accrued income tax obligations at the project entity level. Net margin represents the after-fee, after-tax profit available for distribution to the project entity's investors and the developer's equity interest.

Both margins are expressed in absolute dollar terms and as a percentage of total project cost and as a percentage of total revenue, and are compared against the pro forma targets. A project that achieves a 22% gross margin versus a pro forma target of 24% has underperformed — and the final cost report must explain the 200-basis-point shortfall in terms of specific cost overruns or revenue shortfalls.

### Return on Equity (ROE)

Return on equity is calculated as the project's net profit (gross margin less development fees and taxes) divided by the total equity invested in the project from inception through close-out. Equity invested includes all hard equity contributed by the developer and its limited partners, any mezzanine financing treated as equity in the capital stack, and any development fees deferred and re-invested as equity.

The timing of equity investment matters in ROE calculation. Equity invested at the beginning of a five-year project is not the same as equity invested in the project's final year — the former has been at risk longer and the return on it must compensate for that duration. ROE as a simple ratio does not capture this timing dimension; IRR does.

### Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

The internal rate of return is the discount rate at which the net present value of all project cash flows (both invested and returned) equals zero. It is the most comprehensive measure of project-level financial performance because it captures both the magnitude of returns and their timing. An IRR of 18% on a project that ran for four years is fundamentally different from an IRR of 18% on a project that ran for seven years, even if the simple dollar return is identical — the four-year project has returned capital to investors much more quickly, enabling re-deployment.

The IRR is calculated on the project's equity cash flows: negative cash flows (equity invested) and positive cash flows (equity returned through construction loan draws repaid by equity, or through distributions of closing proceeds). The calculation uses the actual dates of each cash flow, not period-end approximations, to produce an accurate time-weighted return.

The Senior Analyst presents the IRR in three versions: the levered IRR (on equity cash flows net of debt service, reflecting the actual financing structure employed), the unlevered IRR (on total project cash flows as if the project had been funded entirely with equity, a measure of the project's inherent real estate performance independent of leverage), and the LP IRR (the internal rate of return earned specifically by the limited partners, after the promote structure has been applied — discussed in Section 14.3).

### Equity Multiple

The equity multiple (also called the return multiple or investment multiple) expresses the total capital returned to investors divided by the total capital invested. An equity multiple of 1.65x means investors received $1.65 for every $1.00 invested. Like the IRR, the equity multiple is calculated separately for the levered position and for each investor class under the waterfall structure. The equity multiple is intuitive for investors and is typically presented alongside the IRR in investor reporting.

## 14.3 The Investor Distribution Waterfall

<div id="ol-ch14-2"></div>

On projects with joint venture equity partners or limited partnership investors, the project's net proceeds are not simply divided pro rata among all investors. They are distributed according to a waterfall structure — a sequential priority of cash flow distribution — that is defined in the joint venture agreement or limited partnership agreement negotiated at the project's inception. Calculating the correct distribution of proceeds under the waterfall is one of the most consequential and technically demanding tasks of the project close-out period.

### Waterfall Structure Fundamentals

A typical condominium development waterfall for a deal between a developer (the general partner or managing partner) and institutional or high-net-worth equity investors (the limited partners) follows a sequential structure:

**Tier 1 — Return of Capital**: All invested capital is returned to investors in proportion to their respective capital contributions before any profit is distributed. This tier has no preferred rate of return — it simply ensures that investors get their principal back first. On a project where the developer has contributed 10% of the equity and the LP investors have contributed 90%, the return-of-capital tier returns those amounts in those proportions.

**Tier 2 — Preferred Return**: After capital is returned, investors receive a preferred return — a cumulative, compounding (or simple, depending on the agreement) return on their invested capital calculated from the date of investment to the date of distribution. The preferred return rate is negotiated at the time of the joint venture formation and is typically in the range of 6% to 10% per annum for condominium development deals, reflecting the investors' required hurdle rate for deploying capital into development risk. The preferred return is cumulative — if the project takes longer than projected and the preferred return accrues over a longer period, the total preferred return obligation increases accordingly. This is one of the reasons that schedule overruns on development projects are financially punitive to the developer: they increase the preferred return obligation before the developer sees any promoted profit.

**Tier 3 — Catch-Up (if applicable)**: Some waterfall structures include a catch-up provision that allows the developer to receive a higher share of distributions above the preferred return threshold until the developer has "caught up" to its target split of the profit above the preferred return. Catch-up provisions are more common in fund structures than in single-project joint ventures, and their presence and mechanics must be reviewed carefully in the joint venture agreement.

**Tier 4 — Promoted Interest (the Promote)**: After investors have received their return of capital and preferred return (and after any catch-up), remaining proceeds are split between the developer and the LP investors in a ratio that gives the developer a disproportionate share of the upside. This disproportionate allocation — the promote or carried interest — is the developer's compensation for having sourced, managed, and executed the project. A typical promote structure gives the developer 20% to 30% of remaining cash flow after the preferred return is satisfied, with LP investors receiving the balance. Some agreements have tiered promote structures with higher developer shares at higher IRR levels (incentive promote tiers).

### Calculating the Waterfall Distribution

The waterfall calculation requires the following inputs for each investor: the amount of capital contributed, the dates of each capital contribution, the preferred return rate, and any catch-up provisions. These inputs are applied to the project's net distributable proceeds — the cash remaining after all project costs have been paid, all debt retired, and all post-closing reserves funded — to produce the distribution amount for each investor class.

The calculation is performed sequentially through the tiers: the total distributable proceeds are first allocated to return of capital across all investors; the residual (if any) is then allocated to satisfy the accrued preferred return; any further residual flows through the promote calculation. If the distributable proceeds are insufficient to fund a tier in full, the shortfall is noted and the distribution is made at the tier's capacity — there is no further distribution and the investors accept the shortfall.

The Senior Analyst prepares the waterfall calculation as a formal, auditable schedule. Each input — capital contribution date, amount, preferred return rate — is sourced directly from the joint venture agreement and the project's capital call records. The calculation is performed in a spreadsheet with each tier's distribution shown separately, and the schedule is reviewed by the developer's legal counsel and the LP investors' representative before distributions are made. Errors in the waterfall calculation — whether overstating or understating the developer's promote — expose the developer to legal liability and reputational damage with its investor relationships.

### Development Fee and Management Fee Treatment

Many joint venture structures allow the developer to collect development fees and asset management fees from the project entity as a return on its management services, separate from its equity return. These fees are paid from the project's cost budget (they are a project cost, not a return of profit) and are deducted before the waterfall distributes remaining proceeds. The treatment of development fees in the final cost report and the waterfall calculation must be consistent: fees paid during the project as costs should not appear again as a distribution at wind-down.

The Senior Analyst confirms, as part of the accounting close, that all development fees and management fees that were approved in the project budget have been paid, that no unpaid fees are being claimed at wind-down that were not previously authorized in the budget, and that the fee amounts in the final cost report are consistent with the amounts specified in the joint venture agreement.

## 14.4 The Project Completion Audit

<div id="ol-ch14-3"></div>

Most joint venture and limited partnership agreements for condominium development projects require the preparation of audited financial statements for the project entity at or following project completion. The audit is performed by an independent external auditor (typically a chartered professional accountant firm) engaged by the developer in its capacity as general partner or managing partner. The audit serves several purposes: it provides LP investors with independent verification that the financial statements accurately reflect the project's costs and revenues; it provides the basis for the audited first-year condominium operating budget (the Section 43 budget, discussed in Chapter 13); and it is the documentary foundation for the developer's income tax filing for the project entity.

### Audit Scope and Timeline

The audit scope covers the project entity's complete financial history from inception through the last closing. It reviews all revenue recognition, cost accrual, cash receipt and disbursement records, financing transactions, and investor capital account balances. The auditor will request documentation for all significant transactions — construction contracts and change orders, purchase agreements and closing proceeds, financing agreements and interest calculations, and all agreements with third parties.

The timeline for the audit is typically set out in the joint venture agreement. Most agreements require that audited financial statements be delivered to investors within 90 to 120 days of the project's final closing. This timeline is achievable on a well-organized project where the financial records are current, all invoices are received and processed, and the cost accruals schedule is complete before the auditor begins their work. On projects where the accounting records have not been maintained in real time — where invoices are backlogged, closing adjustments have not been posted, or the cost accruals schedule was not prepared until after the audit began — the 120-day timeline is frequently missed, delaying investor distributions and eroding investor confidence.

### Revenue Recognition Under ASPE

For most private developer entities in Ontario, financial statements are prepared under Accounting Standards for Private Enterprises (ASPE), the Canadian accounting framework applicable to private companies. Under ASPE, revenue from the sale of condominium units is recognized at the point when the risks and rewards of ownership have transferred to the purchaser — which, for condominium sales in Ontario, occurs at the closing date when title is transferred and the purchase price is received. Deposits held in trust prior to closing are not revenue — they are liabilities on the developer's balance sheet until closing occurs.

The auditor will review each unit's closing date and confirm that revenue is recognized in the correct accounting period. For a project whose closings span two calendar years, the revenue recognition cut-off is a material audit area: units that closed on December 31 must be recognized in year one; units that closed on January 2 are year-two revenue. The Senior Analyst maintains the closing schedule with the exact closing date for each unit — not the scheduled date, but the actual date of title transfer as recorded in the Land Registry — as the basis for revenue recognition.

### Cost Accrual and the Completeness Assertion

The most significant audit risk on a development project's financial statements is the completeness of cost accruals — the risk that costs have been incurred but not yet invoiced, or that obligations exist which have not been recorded. The auditor's completeness testing will include review of the post-closing cost accruals schedule, inquiry of the construction manager regarding any outstanding invoices or claims, review of the condominium corporation's financial records for any reserve fund contribution arrears, and confirmation from legal counsel of any outstanding litigation or legal claims.

The Senior Analyst's preparation of a complete and well-documented post-closing cost accruals schedule (described in Section 14.1) is the primary control against the completeness risk. A schedule that is thorough, sourced to underlying documents, and reviewed by the development manager and legal counsel before the audit begins will reduce the auditor's testing burden, accelerate the audit timeline, and reduce the risk of post-close surprises.

## 14.5 Tax Considerations at Project Close

<div id="ol-ch14-4"></div>

The tax treatment of a condominium development project's income, and the tax obligations arising at project close, are among the most complex and consequential financial decisions of the close-out period. The Senior Analyst is not a tax advisor and does not provide tax advice — but understanding the framework of tax issues that arise at project close is essential for preparing accurate financial records and for anticipating the tax-driven timing and structuring decisions that the developer's tax advisor will recommend.

### Income Tax — Business Income vs. Capital Gain

The developer's profit from a condominium development project is generally characterized as business income rather than capital gain under Canadian income tax rules, because the developer is in the business of developing and selling real property. Business income is fully taxable at the applicable corporate income tax rate; capital gains are only 50% includable in income (the inclusion rate). The distinction matters significantly: business income tax on $10 million of profit at a combined federal-provincial rate of approximately 26.5% produces a tax bill of $2.65 million; if the income were capital gain, the inclusion would be $5 million and the tax approximately $1.325 million.

The characterization of development profit as business income is the default position for developers who build and sell condominium units in the ordinary course of business. However, the specific structure of the development entity, the developer's history, and the nature of any properties retained at project close can create planning opportunities or risks that the developer's tax advisor must evaluate. The Senior Analyst should not assume any particular tax characterization in the project completion accounting — the tax provision should be calculated by the developer's tax advisor and recorded as directed.

### HST Final Reconciliation

Ontario condominium developments are complex from an HST perspective because the project involves both HST-taxable supplies (the sale of new residential units) and HST-exempt supplies (certain aspects of the common elements). The developer collects HST on each unit sale, remits the tax on a net basis (input tax credits for HST paid on construction costs offset the HST collected on sales), and processes the new housing rebates assigned by qualifying purchasers.

At project close, the developer's HST final return reconciles the cumulative HST collected, the cumulative input tax credits claimed, the new housing rebates paid on behalf of qualifying purchasers, and any HST adjustments arising from construction cost credits, change orders, or closing adjustments. The net HST position — a payable or refund — must be settled with the Canada Revenue Agency before the developer's tax filings for the year of the final closing are complete.

The Senior Analyst's role is to ensure that the HST final reconciliation is completed with accurate unit-by-unit data: the total purchase price per unit, the HST collected, the rebate paid (if applicable), and the net HST amount per unit. This data, aggregated across all units, produces the inputs for the developer's HST advisor to complete the final return.

### Land Transfer Tax and Registration Costs

At each unit closing, Ontario Land Transfer Tax and (for properties within the City of Toronto) the Municipal Land Transfer Tax are paid by the purchaser. These taxes are not a developer cost — they are the purchaser's cost, collected by the developer's solicitor and remitted to the province and municipality. However, the statement of adjustments for each unit must correctly calculate the transfer tax on the purchase price, and any errors in the tax calculation can create closing day adjustments that must be tracked and reconciled.

## 14.6 Investor Reporting Obligations

<div id="ol-ch14-5"></div>

The developer's reporting obligations to its equity investors do not end with the project's financial close. They extend through the full distribution and wind-down period, and in most cases, they include formal reporting at specified intervals following the final closing. The content, format, frequency, and delivery timeline for investor reports are governed by the joint venture agreement or limited partnership agreement, and any failure to comply with these reporting obligations — even if the financial outcomes are positive — is a breach of the developer's contractual duties to its investors.

### Periodic Reporting During the Closing Period

In the weeks and months following condominium registration, as individual units close and construction loan repayments are made, investors expect regular updates on the project's financial progress. Most joint venture agreements require monthly or bi-monthly closing period reports covering: the number of units closed to date, total proceeds received, outstanding construction loan balance, any confirmed defaults and their status, and the projected final closing date. These reports are typically brief — two to four pages — but must be accurate and timely.

The Senior Analyst prepares the closing period investor report from the closing cash flow model, which is updated after each closing day. The report presents the actuals-to-date alongside the model's original projections, so investors can see both the absolute position and the variance from plan. Any material variance — a higher-than-projected default rate, a registration delay that extended the closing period, or a unit re-sale at below the original purchase price — is disclosed and explained. Investors who learn of material negative developments from sources other than the developer's own reporting will have diminished confidence in the developer as a partner, regardless of the ultimate financial outcome.

### The Project Completion Report

The project completion report is the comprehensive post-project investor report that synthesizes the final cost report, the return calculations, and the waterfall distribution schedule into a single narrative and financial document. It is the definitive account of the project's performance and is delivered to investors concurrent with (or shortly after) the final distribution of proceeds.

The project completion report typically contains the following sections:

**Executive Summary**: A brief narrative overview of the project's key outcomes — total revenue, total cost, gross margin, IRR and equity multiple versus pro forma targets, and the key variances that drove the difference between projected and actual performance. The executive summary is written for the investor's senior decision-makers, not for technical readers, and should communicate the essential story of the project's performance in three to five paragraphs.

**Revenue Analysis**: Total revenue by category (unit sales, upgrade revenue, parking and storage, deposit forfeitures net of re-sale costs), presented at the project level and compared to pro forma. Material variances in revenue — strong or weak upgrade capture, a higher- or lower-than-projected default rate, re-sale outcomes on defaulted units — are explained.

**Cost Analysis**: The final cost report (Section 14.1) presented in investor-facing format, with variances by major cost category and explanatory notes for material variances.

**Return Metrics**: Project-level IRR, equity multiple, and return on equity, compared to original underwriting targets and to the most recent underwriting update shared with investors during the construction period.

**Distribution Schedule**: The waterfall distribution calculation (Section 14.3) showing each investor's return of capital, preferred return, and promote distribution, and confirming that the distribution has been or will be made in accordance with the joint venture agreement.

**Post-Project Obligations Summary**: A summary of the developer's remaining obligations following the final distribution — warranty reserve balance and anticipated drawdown timeline, reserve fund contribution schedule, any outstanding litigation, and the estimated date of final wind-down.

### Final Distribution Mechanics

The final distribution of project proceeds — the event that returns capital and profit to investors — is the culmination of the close-out process and must be executed precisely in accordance with the joint venture agreement. The distribution timeline is typically triggered by the last closing, following a period during which the developer retains a reserve for post-closing costs (the warranty reserve, post-closing legal fees, and any contingent liabilities identified in the audit).

The Senior Analyst prepares the final distribution calculation, which shows, for each investor: opening capital account balance, return of capital component, preferred return component, promote component, and total distribution. The calculation is reviewed by the developer's legal counsel and the developer's accountant before wire transfer instructions are issued. The Senior Analyst retains the supporting calculation and all source documents (capital call records, closing proceeds confirmations, preferred return accrual schedule) in the project file for a minimum period as specified by the joint venture agreement and the developer's document retention policy.

## 14.7 Project Post-Mortem and Institutional Knowledge Capture

<div id="ol-ch14-6"></div>

The project post-mortem is the structured internal review of the project's outcomes — comparing what was projected at each stage of the project's development against what actually occurred, identifying the decisions and conditions that drove the most significant variances, and capturing lessons that can be applied to future projects. It is the process through which a development organization learns from experience, and its quality is directly proportional to the quality of the records kept throughout the project's life and the candour of the participants who contribute to it.

### The Post-Mortem Framework

A rigorous post-mortem examines the project across four dimensions: financial performance, schedule performance, product performance, and team and process performance.

**Financial performance** compares final IRR, equity multiple, gross margin, and per-unit revenue and cost against the original pro forma and the underwriting as it evolved through the project. For each material variance — a construction cost overrun, a schedule extension that inflated financing costs, a stronger- or weaker-than-projected average unit price — the post-mortem identifies the root cause and the decision point at which the variance was (or should have been) identified. A financing cost overrun caused by a six-month registration delay that was attributable to late site plan clearance is a process failure with a specific remedy (earlier engagement of the municipality on site plan obligations); a financing cost overrun caused by an unforeseen interest rate increase is a market risk event with different implications.

**Schedule performance** compares the original project schedule — from land closing through registration and final closing — against the actual schedule at each major milestone: construction start, building permit issuance, construction substantial performance, first occupancy, condominium registration, first closing, and last closing. For each milestone, the post-mortem records the original projected date, the most recent projected date at the point the milestone was achieved, and the actual date. The schedule variance narrative identifies the primary causes of schedule overruns and the earliest point at which each variance was predictable.

**Product performance** evaluates whether the project's design, suite mix, finishes, and amenity program delivered the market performance that was projected. Metrics include the average sale price per square foot achieved versus the underwriting assumption, the upgrade capture rate and average upgrade revenue per unit versus the marketing plan's projections, purchaser satisfaction levels as reflected in PDI deficiency volumes and warranty claim frequency, and market absorption — how quickly units were sold during the pre-sales program relative to the sales pace assumptions. Strong product performance is a signal that the design and market positioning decisions were well-calibrated; weak performance identifies where the next project's design brief should be revised.

**Team and process performance** examines the performance of the development team and its consultants and contractors. Which consultants delivered on time and on budget? Where did the consultant team create schedule risk — late drawings, delayed approvals, incomplete specifications? How did the construction manager manage the GMP contract — how much of the contingency was consumed, and was the final account settled efficiently? Were there major subcontractor defaults or claims? Was the marketing and sales team's performance consistent with their track record and the developer's expectations? This dimension of the post-mortem is the most sensitive because it involves assessments of individuals and firms, but it is also the most operationally valuable — it informs the developer's selection of team members for future projects.

### Capturing and Distributing Post-Mortem Findings

The post-mortem is an internal document. Its primary audience is the developer's senior leadership and the project team. Unlike the project completion report — which is an investor-facing financial summary — the post-mortem is candid about operational weaknesses and process failures, and its value depends on that candour. A post-mortem that records only positive findings is a missed learning opportunity.

The Senior Analyst contributes to the post-mortem by preparing the financial performance and schedule performance analyses — the quantitative backbone of the review. The development manager and the developer's principal lead the team and process performance dimensions and facilitate the post-mortem meeting with the extended project team.

Post-mortem findings are documented in a structured report and filed in the developer's project archive. For recurring issues — late occupancy permits, streetscape completion delays, specific subcontractor performance problems — the findings should be translated into procedural updates: earlier milestone triggers in the project schedule, pre-qualification updates to the approved subcontractor list, or revised contingency allocations for specific risk categories. A post-mortem that generates documented process improvements has the highest return on investment of any document produced in the project close-out phase.

### Project File Archiving

The project file — the complete record of all material documents from pre-development through close-out — must be organized, indexed, and archived before the project entity is wound down. The archive includes: all approvals and entitlement documents; the construction contract and all change orders; all purchase agreements and closing records; all financing documents; all consultant agreements and deliverables; all correspondence with the municipality, Tarion, and the condominium corporation; the final cost report; the project completion report; the post-mortem; and the audited financial statements.

The retention period for development project records is governed by the developer's internal policy, the joint venture agreement, and applicable legal requirements. Ontario's Limitations Act establishes a basic limitation period of two years for most claims, but construction defect claims and warranty claims can have longer limitation periods, and tax records must be retained for at least six years. A seven-year minimum retention period for all project records is the conservative standard adopted by most Ontario developers.

Digital archiving — with a consistent folder structure, a document index, and off-site backup — is standard practice. The Senior Analyst is typically responsible for organizing the project's financial records within the archive structure before the project file is closed.

## 14.8 Winding Down the Project Entity

<div id="ol-ch14-7"></div>

Most condominium development projects are structured in a special-purpose project entity — a limited partnership, a corporation, or a joint venture structure — that is created specifically for the project and is intended to be wound down after the project is complete. The wind-down of the project entity involves a series of legal, accounting, and administrative steps that must be completed in an orderly sequence.

### Final Accounting and Capital Account Reconciliation

Before the project entity can be wound down, all of its financial accounts must be closed. This requires that all outstanding payables be settled (or accrued with a cash reserve to fund future payment), all receivables collected, all bank accounts cleared, all warranty reserve funds disbursed or transferred to the developer's holding account, and all investor capital accounts reconciled to a zero balance following the final distribution.

For a limited partnership, the final capital account reconciliation is a formal accounting step: each limited partner's capital account — which has been reduced by distributions throughout the project and by any allocated losses — should equal zero after the final distribution. Any residual in a capital account (positive or negative) signals an error in the distribution calculation that must be corrected before wind-down.

### Dissolution of the Project Entity

Once all accounts are settled and capital accounts are reconciled, the project entity can be formally dissolved. For an Ontario limited partnership, dissolution requires filing a declaration of dissolution with the Ontario Business Registry. For a corporation, dissolution requires articles of dissolution filed with the appropriate corporate registry (federal or provincial depending on the corporation's jurisdiction). The developer's solicitor manages the dissolution filing; the Senior Analyst confirms that the financial conditions for dissolution are met before the solicitor proceeds.

The dissolution of the project entity does not extinguish the developer's warranty obligations or any contractual obligations that survive completion — those obligations run with the developer entity, not the project entity. The developer should confirm with its legal counsel that any post-closing obligations of the project entity have been properly transferred or indemnified before the project entity is dissolved.

### Residual Exposure After Wind-Down

Even after the project entity is dissolved and all investors have received their distributions, the developer retains residual exposure through several channels. Tarion warranty obligations run with the builder registration, not the project entity, so warranty claims continue to be administered through the developer's Tarion account for the full statutory warranty period. Any litigation arising from the project — deficiency claims from the condominium corporation, purchaser complaints, subcontractor claims — runs against the developer (and potentially against the project entity's principals) in accordance with the applicable limitation periods. The warranty reserve maintained by the developer (discussed in Chapter 13 and Section 14.1) provides the financial buffer for warranty-related costs; maintaining an adequate reserve and actively managing its drawdown is a post-wind-down financial management obligation that continues for years.

## 14.9 Common Failure Modes in Project Close-Out

<div id="ol-ch14-8"></div>

The close-out phase is not immune to the failure modes that affect earlier project phases, and some failures are specific to this final stage. The following patterns recur across development organizations and market cycles.

**1. Incomplete cost accruals at the time of investor reporting.** Developers who distribute proceeds to investors before all post-closing costs are identified and reserved — because they are under pressure to distribute quickly or have not completed the post-closing cost accruals schedule — expose themselves to either a clawback request from investors (if additional costs emerge) or absorbing post-closing costs from their own balance sheet. Preventive discipline: no final distribution is made until the post-closing cost accruals schedule is complete and reviewed by legal counsel.

**2. HST position errors on investor-purchaser unit closings.** Units purchased by investors who do not qualify for the new housing rebate require careful HST handling — the developer may be required to remit HST at the full rate without the rebate offset. Failing to identify these units in advance of closing creates HST exposure that can materialize as an audit assessment years after the project is complete. Preventive discipline: the occupancy/investor status of every purchaser must be identified before closing, with the HST treatment confirmed with the developer's HST advisor for any non-owner-occupied units.

**3. Preferred return calculation errors.** Waterfall calculations that use approximate capital contribution dates (month-end rather than the actual transfer date), or that apply simple interest instead of compounded interest (or vice versa, depending on the agreement), or that fail to account for partial-year periods correctly, produce preferred return figures that differ from the contractually correct amounts. Given that these calculations directly affect how much the developer earns in promote, errors are always contentious. Preventive discipline: the preferred return calculation is performed in a dedicated, auditable schedule, sourced to the actual capital call transfer dates confirmed by bank records, and reviewed by the developer's legal counsel and accountant before distribution.

**4. Post-mortem conducted without candour.** A post-mortem that records only the project's successes and attributes every overrun to unforeseeable external factors is a cultural failure. It produces a document that satisfies a procedural requirement without creating any institutional learning. The development organization that does not honestly evaluate its own process failures will repeat them. Preventive discipline: the developer's principal should set the expectation that the post-mortem is a learning exercise, not a performance review, and should ensure that the project team members who managed the areas with the largest variances lead the analysis of those areas.

**5. Delayed project completion audit.** Developers who allow the audit timeline to slip — because the financial records are not in order, because the post-closing cost accruals are incomplete, or because the construction holdback release is still outstanding — breach their reporting obligations to investors and delay the final distribution. The audit delay is typically attributable to problems that could have been prevented by maintaining current financial records throughout the project rather than deferring reconciliation to the close-out period. Preventive discipline: the Senior Analyst should maintain the project's financial records on a current basis throughout the project, with monthly reconciliations and an updated cost-to-complete, so that the close-out accounting is an incremental update rather than a complete reconstruction.

**6. Failing to track the turnover meeting and reserve fund contribution schedule.** Developers who close out their project entity without confirming that the reserve fund contribution obligations to the condominium corporation are fully tracked and funded may receive claims from the corporation years later. The reserve fund contribution schedule runs for two years following registration — on a project that registers in Year 1 and closes out in Year 2, the reserve fund obligations extend into Year 3. Preventive discipline: the reserve fund contribution schedule is built into the post-closing cost accruals schedule and funded from the warranty reserve before any final distributions are made.

**7. Dissolution before all obligations are transferred.** Dissolving the project entity while it still has live obligations — warranty obligations being administered through the entity, ongoing litigation, or unfulfilled reserve fund contributions — creates legal complications and potential personal liability for the developer entity's directors or partners. Preventive discipline: legal counsel must confirm that all material obligations of the project entity have been discharged or transferred before dissolution proceeds.

**8. Inadequate project archive.** A project archive that is incomplete, disorganized, or stored only on individual team members' personal drives (rather than a centralized, backed-up system) will not be retrievable when it is needed — typically when a warranty claim or legal dispute arises years after the project is complete. At that point, missing records are a serious liability. Preventive discipline: the project archive is organized, indexed, and transferred to the developer's central document management system before the project entity is dissolved, and the Senior Analyst confirms the completeness of the financial records component of the archive before sign-off.

## 14.10 Key Metrics and Terms — Reference Table

<div id="ol-ch14-9"></div>

The following table summarizes the metrics, instruments, and terminology introduced in this chapter.

| Metric / Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Final Cost Report** | The comprehensive reconciliation of every budget line item from pre-development through project close-out, comparing the original pro forma budget, the most recent approved budget, and actual costs incurred, with explanatory notes for all material variances. |
| **Post-Closing Cost Accruals Schedule** | A line-item schedule of all outstanding cost obligations following the last unit closing — including unpaid invoices, holdback releases, warranty reserve contributions, and reserve fund contributions — prepared before the accounting close is finalized. |
| **Gross Margin** | Total project revenue less total project costs (hard costs, soft costs, financing costs, marketing and sales costs, and carrying costs); the project's top-line profitability measure before fees and income taxes. |
| **Net Margin** | Gross margin less development and management fees and accrued income taxes; the after-fee, after-tax profit available for distribution under the waterfall. |
| **Return on Equity (ROE)** | Net project profit divided by total equity invested from inception through close-out; a simple ratio measure of the return earned on the capital at risk, not adjusted for the timing of investment or return. |
| **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)** | The discount rate at which the net present value of all equity cash flows (invested and returned) equals zero; the comprehensive measure of project financial performance that captures both magnitude and timing of returns. |
| **Levered IRR** | The IRR calculated on equity cash flows net of debt service, reflecting the actual financing structure employed on the project. |
| **Unlevered IRR** | The IRR calculated on total project cash flows as if the project were funded entirely with equity; a measure of the project's inherent real estate performance independent of leverage. |
| **LP IRR** | The IRR earned specifically by the limited partners after the promote structure has been applied; represents the investors' actual return net of the developer's promoted interest. |
| **Equity Multiple** | Total capital returned to investors divided by total capital invested; an intuitive measure of absolute return expressed as a multiple (e.g., 1.65x). |
| **Waterfall** | The sequential distribution structure defined in the joint venture or limited partnership agreement governing how project proceeds are allocated among investors and the developer, typically through tiers of return of capital, preferred return, catch-up, and promoted interest. |
| **Preferred Return** | The cumulative, compounding (or simple) return accruing on each investor's invested capital from the date of contribution to the date of distribution; the investor's hurdle rate that must be satisfied before the developer earns any promoted interest. |
| **Promote (Carried Interest)** | The developer's disproportionate share of project returns above the preferred return threshold; the developer's economic incentive for sourcing, managing, and executing the project. |
| **Catch-Up Provision** | A waterfall feature that allows the developer to receive a higher share of distributions above the preferred return level until the developer has reached its target promoted interest percentage relative to LP investors. |
| **ASPE** | Accounting Standards for Private Enterprises; the Canadian accounting framework applicable to most private developer entities, under which condominium unit revenue is recognized at the closing date when title transfers. |
| **Project Completion Report** | The comprehensive investor-facing document delivered following the final closing that synthesizes the final cost report, return metrics, waterfall distribution schedule, and post-project obligations summary. |
| **Post-Mortem** | The structured internal review of the project's financial, schedule, product, and team performance against original projections; the mechanism by which a development organization captures institutional learning from each completed project. |
| **HST New Housing Rebate** | The partial rebate of HST available to purchasers of new residential condominium units who use the unit as their primary place of residence; typically assigned by the purchaser to the developer at closing and reflected as a credit in the statement of adjustments. |
| **Capital Account** | The running balance of each investor's equity position in the project entity, reflecting initial capital contributions, any additional contributions, allocated income or loss, and distributions received; should reconcile to zero following the final distribution. |
| **Project Entity Wind-Down** | The formal dissolution of the special-purpose entity created for the development project, following settlement of all obligations, reconciliation of all capital accounts, and completion of all investor distributions. |

## AE-14.1 — Final Return Calculation and Waterfall Distribution

### Scenario

You are the Senior Analyst preparing the project completion report for a 22-storey, 204-unit condominium project in the City of Toronto. The project has now completed all closings and the final cost report has been prepared. The following financial summary has been compiled:

**Revenue Summary**

| Revenue Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Unit sale proceeds (204 units closed) | $183,600,000 |
| Upgrade revenue | $4,410,000 |
| Parking and storage locker sales | $3,060,000 |
| Net deposit forfeitures (3 defaulted units, net of re-sale costs) | $290,000 |
| **Total Revenue** | **$191,360,000** |

**Cost Summary**

| Cost Category | Pro Forma Budget | Final Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Land acquisition | $22,400,000 | $22,400,000 | — |
| Hard construction costs | $82,700,000 | $86,940,000 | +$4,240,000 |
| Soft costs | $14,300,000 | $14,890,000 | +$590,000 |
| Financing costs | $18,600,000 | $20,150,000 | +$1,550,000 |
| Marketing and sales costs | $7,200,000 | $7,060,000 | −$140,000 |
| Development fee (paid to developer) | $4,800,000 | $4,800,000 | — |
| Post-closing reserves (warranty, legal) | $1,200,000 | $1,200,000 | — |
| **Total Costs** | **$151,200,000** | **$157,440,000** | **+$6,240,000** |

**Capital Structure**

The project was funded as follows: the developer (general partner) contributed $8,400,000 in equity and the LP investors contributed $25,600,000 in equity — a 25%/75% split of the $34,000,000 total equity. The balance of the project cost was funded by the construction loan.

The joint venture agreement provides the following waterfall:
- Tier 1: Return of capital pro rata (GP 25%, LP 75%)
- Tier 2: Preferred return of 8% per annum, simple interest, accrued from the date of the initial capital contribution to the date of distribution; the total accrued preferred return on the LP capital (over the 4.5-year project period) is $9,216,000, and on the GP capital is $3,024,000.
- Tier 3: No catch-up provision.
- Tier 4: Promote: 70% to LP investors, 30% to GP developer.

### Questions

1. Calculate the project's gross margin (dollar amount and percentage of total cost) and compare it to the pro forma gross margin.
2. Calculate the project's levered IRR inputs and identify the net distributable proceeds available for the waterfall distribution.
3. Calculate the waterfall distribution for the GP and the LP investors, showing each tier separately.

---

## AE-14.1 — Solution

**Question 1 — Gross margin calculation and comparison to pro forma**

Gross margin excludes the development fee (which is a cost category) and the post-closing reserves (which are a cost accrual, not a return of profit). Both are correctly included in total costs.

Final gross margin: $191,360,000 − $157,440,000 = **$33,920,000**

Final gross margin as a percentage of total cost: $33,920,000 ÷ $157,440,000 = **21.5%**

Pro forma gross margin: $191,360,000 (using the same revenue, since the question establishes one revenue figure) − $151,200,000 = $40,160,000.

Note: The question implies a single revenue figure; in practice, the pro forma revenue would have its own projection. For this exercise we calculate the pro forma margin at the actual revenue level to isolate the cost variance effect.

Pro forma gross margin percentage: $40,160,000 ÷ $151,200,000 = **26.6%**

**The project underperformed the pro forma gross margin by 510 basis points** ($6,240,000 in cost overruns). The primary driver was the hard construction cost overrun of $4,240,000, followed by the financing cost overrun of $1,550,000 (consistent with a project that ran over schedule, extending the construction loan carry period), and the soft cost overrun of $590,000. The marketing savings of $140,000 were a modest partial offset.

**Question 2 — Net distributable proceeds**

Total revenue: $191,360,000
Less total costs (including development fee and post-closing reserves): $157,440,000
Gross margin / net distributable proceeds: **$33,920,000**

Note: The development fee of $4,800,000 has already been paid to the developer from the project budget during the construction and closing periods. It is not distributed through the waterfall — it is a cost. The waterfall distributes the $33,920,000 gross margin among the GP and LP investors.

Return of capital to be distributed through the waterfall: $34,000,000 (total equity contributed)

Total distribution required for waterfall: return of capital ($34,000,000) + preferred returns ($9,216,000 LP + $3,024,000 GP = $12,240,000) + promote distribution on remaining proceeds.

**Question 3 — Waterfall distribution**

**Tier 1 — Return of Capital**
Total capital contributed: $34,000,000 (LP: $25,600,000; GP: $8,400,000)

The project's net distributable proceeds are $33,920,000. This is *less* than the total capital contributed of $34,000,000. The project has not generated sufficient gross margin to return all invested capital plus a preferred return.

Wait — this requires re-examination. The gross margin is $33,920,000 and the total equity invested is $34,000,000. The gross margin represents the profit available for distribution *after* all project costs (including financing costs, which are the cost of the construction loan). The equity invested was the equity portion of the project funding; the debt was repaid through the construction loan paydown during closings. Therefore, the distributable proceeds are the gross margin of $33,920,000 — and these proceeds must first return capital before distributing profit.

Tier 1: Return of capital = $34,000,000 required; only $33,920,000 is available.

**The project's distributable proceeds are insufficient to return full invested capital.** After distributing all $33,920,000, there is a capital shortfall of $80,000 ($34,000,000 − $33,920,000).

Tier 1 distribution: All $33,920,000 is allocated as return of capital.
- LP receives: $33,920,000 × 75% = **$25,440,000** (against LP capital of $25,600,000 — shortfall of $160,000)
- GP receives: $33,920,000 × 25% = **$8,480,000** (against GP capital of $8,400,000 — slight excess of $80,000)

Correction: The pro-rata return means both LP and GP are short on a proportional basis. At $33,920,000 total and a 75/25 split: LP gets $25,440,000 (short $160,000 of its $25,600,000 capital); GP gets $8,480,000 (exceeds its $8,400,000 by $80,000). This anomaly arises from the 75/25 split producing a GP distribution above its contributed capital before LP is made whole on its capital — which is inconsistent with a pro-rata capital return. The correct interpretation of Tier 1 is that *each party* receives back its contributed capital first; the distributable amount is insufficient to do this in full for LP.

**Practical resolution**: The distributable proceeds of $33,920,000 are allocated entirely to return of capital. LP receives $25,440,000 (93.75% recovery of its $25,600,000 capital) and GP receives $8,480,000 (100.95% of its $8,400,000 capital) — but since Tier 1 is a pro-rata return, the mechanics strictly produce these amounts.

There are no proceeds remaining after Tier 1. **No preferred return, catch-up, or promote is distributable** — the project's returns are insufficient to fund any distribution beyond a partial return of LP capital.

**Summary of distributions:**

| Investor | Capital Invested | Tier 1 Distribution | Preferred Return | Promote | Total Distribution | Return Multiple |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LP Investors | $25,600,000 | $25,440,000 | $0 | $0 | $25,440,000 | 0.994x |
| GP Developer | $8,400,000 | $8,480,000 | $0 | $0 | $8,480,000 | 1.010x |
| **Total** | **$34,000,000** | **$33,920,000** | **$0** | **$0** | **$33,920,000** | **0.998x** |

The project returned 99.8% of invested capital in aggregate. LP investors sustained a net loss of $160,000 on $25,600,000 invested (a 0.6% loss). The GP's $80,000 capital excess in Tier 1 is a technicality of the pro-rata split; in practice, many joint venture agreements would cap the GP's Tier 1 return at its actual capital contribution, directing the excess to LP. The development fee of $4,800,000 paid to the GP during the project was the GP's primary economic return. This project is a cautionary financial outcome — a cost overrun of $6.24 million converted a project that should have generated a 26.6% gross margin into one that barely returned invested capital, with no preferred return or promote distributed.

## AE-14.2 — Post-Mortem Variance Analysis

### Scenario

The same project from AE-14.1 is undergoing its post-mortem review. The development manager has asked you to prepare the financial performance section of the post-mortem. The following additional information is available about the project's major variances:

The hard construction cost overrun of $4,240,000 broke down as follows: $1,850,000 in concrete formwork overruns on floors 14–22 caused by late architectural drawing revisions that required re-sequencing of the floor plate; $1,100,000 in mechanical system scope additions requested by the developer's principal during construction to upgrade the building's energy performance systems beyond the original specification; and $1,290,000 in general conditions overrun caused by a 14-week extension to the construction schedule resulting from the late drawing revisions.

The financing cost overrun of $1,550,000 was entirely attributable to construction loan interest during the schedule extension: the 14-week delay extended the outstanding construction loan period, producing additional interest at the average loan balance during the extension period.

The soft cost overrun of $590,000 was attributable to $340,000 in additional consultant fees for re-design work associated with the late drawing revisions and $250,000 in legal and registration costs above the original estimate, primarily related to a contested site plan condition that required additional negotiation and documentation.

The marketing savings of $140,000 resulted from a lower-than-projected co-operating broker commission pool (the project sold out in pre-sales without requiring the full broker incentive program originally budgeted).

### Questions

1. Categorize each variance as (a) preventable through better process or earlier decision-making, (b) elective developer decisions made during construction, or (c) external or market conditions. For each preventable item, identify the process improvement that would have reduced the variance.
2. Calculate the total financial cost attributable to the late drawing revision event and assess whether this event alone was the difference between a project that distributed a preferred return and one that did not.
3. What are the two most important process changes the developer should implement for its next project, based on this post-mortem?

---

## AE-14.2 — Solution

**Question 1 — Variance categorization**

**Concrete formwork overrun ($1,850,000) — Preventable.** Late architectural drawing revisions that require re-sequencing of an already-poured or actively forming floor plate are a classic consequence of issuing construction drawings before the design is fully coordinated. The root cause is typically an architectural design process that allowed the developer (or the developer's principal) to continue making design changes after the IFC (Issued for Construction) drawing package was released to the construction manager. The process improvement: establish a hard design freeze date prior to construction commencement, with a formal change control procedure requiring the developer's principal to approve the cost and schedule impact of any post-freeze revision before it is incorporated. A change that moves $1.85 million worth of formwork would have been subject to that approval — and may have been reconsidered.

**Mechanical system upgrades ($1,100,000) — Elective developer decision.** The decision to upgrade the energy performance systems during construction was a choice made by the developer's principal with full knowledge (presumably) that it was a scope addition. Elective decisions should be evaluated against their financial impact at the time the decision is made. Whether this decision was correct depends on whether the upgrade increased unit values or marketability by more than $1.1 million — a question that is worth examining in the product performance section of the post-mortem. If the upgrade generated no measurable value premium, it was a $1.1 million cost with no return; if it contributed to a faster sell-out or higher average price, it may have been justified. The process improvement: any developer-directed scope addition during construction is evaluated against a cost-benefit analysis prepared by the Senior Analyst before approval, with the financial impact modelled against the remaining pro forma headroom.

**General conditions overrun from schedule extension ($1,290,000) — Preventable (linked to item 1).** The general conditions overrun is a direct consequence of the 14-week schedule extension caused by the late drawing revisions. It is not an independent overrun — it is a downstream cost of the same preventable event. The process improvement is the same as for the formwork overrun.

**Financing cost overrun ($1,550,000) — Preventable (linked to item 1).** Again, a direct downstream consequence of the 14-week schedule extension. The additional construction loan interest is not an independent event — it is the financing cost of the schedule slip caused by late drawings.

**Soft cost overrun — re-design fees ($340,000) — Preventable (linked to item 1).** Consultant fees for re-design work are the third downstream cost of the late drawing revision event.

**Soft cost overrun — site plan legal costs ($250,000) — Partially preventable.** A contested site plan condition that required additional negotiation may reflect insufficient engagement with the municipality during the entitlement phase (preventable, in that earlier identification and resolution of contentious conditions reduces the risk of late-stage legal work) or a genuinely difficult condition that required litigation-adjacent negotiation (less preventable). The post-mortem should investigate whether the contested condition was identifiable earlier and whether earlier engagement would have reduced the legal cost.

**Marketing savings ($140,000) — Positive variance, external/market.** A market-driven outcome: the project sold out without the full broker incentive program. No process change is required; the original marketing budget was appropriately conservative.

**Question 2 — Financial cost of the late drawing revision event**

The late drawing revision event produced the following costs:
- Concrete formwork overrun: $1,850,000
- General conditions overrun (schedule extension): $1,290,000
- Financing cost overrun (schedule extension): $1,550,000
- Soft cost re-design fees: $340,000
- **Total attributable to late drawing revisions: $5,030,000**

If this $5,030,000 overrun had not occurred — if the design had been frozen before construction and no late revisions had been needed — the total cost would have been: $157,440,000 − $5,030,000 = $152,410,000.

The gross margin would have been: $191,360,000 − $152,410,000 = **$38,950,000** (versus the $33,920,000 actually achieved).

Under this scenario, would the preferred return have been distributed?

Total capital + preferred return required for Tier 1 and Tier 2: $34,000,000 + $12,240,000 = $46,240,000. The hypothetical gross margin of $38,950,000 is still short of $46,240,000 — so the preferred return would still not have been fully distributed.

However, the capital return position would have been fully funded ($38,950,000 > $34,000,000), and the partial preferred return distributable would have been: $38,950,000 − $34,000,000 = $4,950,000 to be applied against the $12,240,000 preferred return obligation. LP investors would have received $4,950,000 × 75% / ($12,240,000 × 75%) = approximately 40% of their preferred return.

**The late drawing revision event was the difference between a project where LP investors received a partial preferred return and one where they did not even recover their full capital.** The $5,030,000 in costs attributable to that single event consumed the entire margin between a partial-preferred-return outcome and a capital-loss outcome.

**Question 3 — Top two process improvements**

**First priority: Design freeze and change control discipline.** The analysis above demonstrates that $5,030,000 — nearly 80% of the project's total cost overrun — traces back to a single root cause: late architectural drawing revisions. Implementing a formal design freeze date, a written change order procedure for all post-freeze design changes, and a requirement that the Senior Analyst model the cost and schedule impact of every significant change before it is approved would have been the highest-return process improvement available on this project. On the next project, the developer should define the design freeze date in the architect's agreement as a contractual milestone, with a change management protocol that routes all post-freeze changes through the development manager for cost-impact review.

**Second priority: Independent cost-benefit approval for elective scope additions.** The $1.1 million mechanical upgrade during construction was an elective decision made without a documented cost-benefit evaluation. A standing policy that any developer-directed scope addition above a de minimis threshold (e.g., $50,000) requires a written approval package — cost impact, schedule impact, revenue or market positioning benefit, and net financial impact against the current pro forma — before proceeding would create the discipline to evaluate these decisions objectively. In this project's context, a $1.1 million scope addition when the project's remaining pro forma headroom was already under pressure from other overruns should have been subject to significant scrutiny.

## AE-14.3 — Investor Reporting and Preferred Return Calculation

### Scenario

You are preparing the project completion report for a separate completed project: a 17-storey, 148-unit condominium that achieved its last closing 6 weeks ago. The project was structured as a limited partnership. A single institutional LP invested $18,500,000 on Day 1 of the project (at land closing) and made a second capital contribution of $4,000,000 at the construction loan drawdown date, which was exactly 12 months after the first contribution. The GP contributed $6,500,000 at land closing. All capital contributions earn a preferred return of 7.0% per annum, simple interest, calculated from the date of each contribution to the date of distribution.

The project achieved the following final financial position:
- Total project revenue: $124,800,000
- Total project costs (including all fees and reserves): $99,200,000
- Gross margin / net distributable proceeds: $25,600,000
- Total investment period from first capital contribution to projected distribution date: 5.0 years (LP initial contribution), 4.0 years (LP second contribution), and 5.0 years (GP contribution).

The waterfall is: Tier 1 — return of capital pro rata; Tier 2 — preferred return as described; Tier 3 — promote at 75% LP / 25% GP.

### Questions

1. Calculate the total preferred return accrued for each capital contribution (LP initial, LP second, and GP).
2. Confirm whether the net distributable proceeds are sufficient to fund (a) full return of capital and (b) full payment of all accrued preferred return, and show the final waterfall distribution.
3. Draft the two-paragraph executive summary section of the investor completion report for the LP, disclosing the key return metrics.

---

## AE-14.3 — Solution

**Question 1 — Preferred return calculation**

Simple interest preferred return = Principal × Rate × Time

**LP initial contribution ($18,500,000 for 5.0 years at 7.0%):**
$18,500,000 × 0.070 × 5.0 = **$6,475,000**

**LP second contribution ($4,000,000 for 4.0 years at 7.0%):**
$4,000,000 × 0.070 × 4.0 = **$1,120,000**

**Total LP preferred return: $6,475,000 + $1,120,000 = $7,595,000**

**GP contribution ($6,500,000 for 5.0 years at 7.0%):**
$6,500,000 × 0.070 × 5.0 = **$2,275,000**

**Total preferred return (all parties): $7,595,000 + $2,275,000 = $9,870,000**

**Question 2 — Waterfall distribution**

Total capital invested: LP = $18,500,000 + $4,000,000 = $22,500,000; GP = $6,500,000; Total = $29,000,000.
Total distributable proceeds: $25,600,000.

**Tier 1 — Return of capital**
Required: $29,000,000. Available: $25,600,000. Shortfall: $3,400,000.
The distributable proceeds are insufficient to return all capital.

However — the gross margin of $25,600,000 represents profit available after the construction loan was repaid. The full $29,000,000 of equity was returned through the construction loan paydown during closings — the equity was invested to fund the project, the construction loan was the primary funding vehicle, and the equity capital is returned through the closing proceeds after the loan is retired. The gross margin is the *return on* the equity, not *including* the equity principal itself.

Correction of framework: In a typical condominium development waterfall, the distributable proceeds represent the profit available after all project costs (including debt service) have been paid. The equity principal has already been returned through the project's operations. The $25,600,000 is therefore profit, and the waterfall distributes it as follows, starting with return of capital as a notional first tier (since actual principal is already returned through closing proceeds), then preferred return, then promote.

Re-stated:
All $29,000,000 of capital was returned through the project's closing proceeds (this is the standard outcome in a fully closed-out condominium project). The distributable proceeds of $25,600,000 are available for preferred return and promote.

**Tier 2 — Preferred return**
Total preferred return required: $9,870,000. Available proceeds: $25,600,000. Fully funded.

After preferred return payment: $25,600,000 − $9,870,000 = $15,730,000 remaining for Tier 3.

Preferred return distributions:
- LP receives: $7,595,000
- GP receives: $2,275,000

**Tier 3 — Promote (75% LP / 25% GP)**
Remaining distributable: $15,730,000
- LP receives: $15,730,000 × 75% = $11,797,500

## Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the full arc of the project completion, accounting close, and investor reporting process — the phase that converts an active development project into a definitive financial record and returns capital and profit to the investors who funded it. The close-out process begins with confirming the revenue final position (unit-by-unit reconciliation of closing proceeds, upgrade revenue, and deposit forfeitures) and the cost final position (a comprehensive post-closing cost accruals schedule capturing every outstanding obligation). These inputs produce the final cost report — the authoritative reconciliation of every budget category against actual outcomes — and the project return calculations: gross margin, net margin, ROE, IRR, and equity multiple.

The investor distribution waterfall — the sequential structure through which project proceeds are allocated among investors according to the joint venture agreement — is the most consequential calculation of the close-out phase. Calculated precisely through its tiers of return of capital, preferred return, and promoted interest, the waterfall determines each investor's actual financial outcome and is the primary test of the developer's stewardship of investor capital. Errors in the waterfall calculation expose the developer to legal and reputational consequences that can outlast the project by years.

The project completion audit — performed by an independent auditor under ASPE — provides the documentary foundation for investor distributions, income tax filings, and HST final reconciliation. Audit readiness requires that the project's financial records be maintained on a current basis throughout the project's life, and that the post-closing cost accruals schedule be complete and reviewed before the audit begins. Delays in the audit timeline delay distributions and erode investor confidence.

The project post-mortem — the structured internal review of financial, schedule, product, and team performance against original projections — is the mechanism through which the development organization learns from each completed project. Its value is proportional to its candour: a post-mortem that honestly attributes cost overruns to their root causes and translates findings into documented process improvements is an investment in future project performance. The project file archive, organized and stored before the project entity is wound down, preserves the institutional record against which future warranty claims, legal disputes, and underwriting decisions will be measured.

The Senior Analyst's contribution to the close-out phase — methodical cost capture, rigorous waterfall calculation, accurate return reporting, and honest post-mortem analysis — is the final demonstration of the analytical discipline that has characterized the project from its inception. A well-executed close-out is not merely an administrative obligation; it is the evidence that the developer has managed its investors' capital with the precision and transparency that warrants their confidence in the next project.
