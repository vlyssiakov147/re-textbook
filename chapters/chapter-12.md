# Chapter 12: Marketing, Sales & Leasing

**Phase:** Pre-Development through Occupancy
**Prerequisite Chapters:** 2 (Market Research & Feasibility), 3 (Project Structuring & Capital Stack), 4 (Pre-Development & Entitlements), 9 (Budget Management & Cost Control), 11 (Risk Management & Insurance)

---

> **In Practice — Senior Analyst Perspective**
> Marketing, sales, and leasing are where the developer's capital deployment meets the revenue side of the ledger. Every assumption in the pro forma about absorption pace, unit pricing, lease-up velocity, and closing performance is ultimately tested here, and the Senior Analyst is the person who tracks whether the revenue side of the project is performing to plan. You will not be the person managing the sales centre, negotiating the commission structure with the broker, or approving the advertising campaign. You will be the person who knows, at any given moment, how many units have been firm-sold versus conditionally sold versus unsold, what the average revenue per square foot is running against the pro forma, how many deposits are in trust and at what amounts, how the closing schedule maps against the interest reserve, and whether the purchaser default rate on closings is within the range the project's underwriting assumed. In a condominium project, the sales program can run for three or more years before the first closing; in a purpose-built rental project, the lease-up begins at first occupancy and races against the construction loan's stabilization covenant. In both cases, revenue performance is not a lagging indicator of project success — it is a leading indicator of whether the project's capital structure holds. The Senior Analyst's job is to surface that picture accurately and to escalate when the picture is diverging from the plan in ways that have consequences for the project's financial position.

## 12.1 The Role of Marketing in the Development Lifecycle

<div id="ol-ch12-0"></div>

Marketing in the context of residential real estate development is not a single-phase activity confined to the period when units are offered for sale or lease. It is a discipline that begins during pre-development — when market research informs the project's unit mix, suite sizes, amenity program, and price positioning — and continues through the final closing or stabilized occupancy of the completed building. Marketing shapes the product as much as it sells it, and the developer that treats marketing as an execution task to be managed after design is finalized has already made some of its most important marketing decisions by default.

The distinction between the marketing function and the sales or leasing function is meaningful. **Marketing** is the broader activity of creating awareness, building the project's brand identity, establishing the project's competitive positioning in its target market, and generating the qualified prospect base from which sales or leases are made. **Sales** (for condominium projects) and **leasing** (for rental projects) are the transactional activities through which that prospect base is converted into executed agreements and, ultimately, into revenue. The two activities are closely related and often managed by the same team, but the distinction matters because their costs, their timing, and their financial treatment in the project's pro forma are different.

For the Senior Analyst, the marketing, sales, and leasing function produces three categories of financial output that flow into the project's budget and cost report: **revenue** (the proceeds of unit sales or lease commitments), **marketing and sales costs** (the expenditures required to generate those revenues), and **revenue timing** (the schedule of deposit receipt, closing proceeds, or rental revenue that determines the project's cash position through construction and occupancy). All three require active tracking, and divergences from pro forma in any of the three have cascading effects on the project's financial viability.

## 12.2 Pre-Sale Requirements and the Lender's Threshold

<div id="ol-ch12-1"></div>

For a condominium project in Ontario, the marketing program does not simply produce revenue — it produces the pre-sale threshold that the construction lender requires before it will advance funds. The construction lender's pre-sale requirement is typically the single most important marketing milestone in the project's lifecycle, because without it, construction cannot begin.

### The Pre-Sale Threshold

Construction lenders in the Ontario residential market typically require that a minimum percentage of units in the project be under firm purchase agreements — agreements under which the purchaser's rescission period has expired and the deposit is non-refundable — before the lender will commit to fund construction. The threshold varies by lender, market conditions, and project risk profile, but a range of 60% to 80% of total units is commonly encountered on high-rise condominium projects in the Greater Toronto Area.

The threshold is not simply a count of units — it is a count of firm agreements meeting specific criteria. Agreements that are still within their statutory rescission period under the *Condominium Act, 1998* (currently ten business days for Ontario condominium pre-sales, unless extended by the disclosure statement) are conditional, not firm, for lender purposes. Agreements that have not received the required Tarion disclosure filing may not count. Agreements with purchasers whose deposits have not yet cleared may not count. The Senior Analyst should understand the lender's specific counting methodology, because the marketing team's headline pre-sale number and the lender's qualifying pre-sale count can diverge, and the divergence matters when the construction loan closing date is being confirmed.

### Deposit Trust Requirements

In Ontario, deposits collected under condominium purchase agreements are regulated under the *Condominium Act, 1998*. The developer is required to hold purchaser deposits in a trust account with a qualified trustee (typically a law firm) until either the condominium is registered and closings occur, or the agreement is rescinded and the deposit is returned. The developer cannot access deposits in trust to fund pre-development costs or construction — the deposits are not the developer's money until closing.

The Senior Analyst should track the total deposits held in trust as an off-balance-sheet asset that will convert to closing proceeds when units close. The total deposit balance is a critical indicator of the project's revenue security: a large deposit balance represents a substantial financial commitment from purchasers and indicates that those purchasers have a meaningful financial stake in closing. A project with thin deposits per unit faces higher purchaser default risk at closing.

The deposit structure — the schedule of deposit payments between the time of agreement signing and the closing date — varies by project and market conditions. A common Ontario condominium deposit structure involves an initial deposit of 5% of the purchase price at signing, additional deposits totalling a further 15% to 20% over the period from signing to closing (often in tranches timed to construction milestones), with the balance payable at closing. The Senior Analyst tracks each tranche as it comes due, monitors trust account balances against expected receipts, and flags delinquencies to the development manager.

### Tarion Registration and Disclosure Obligations

Before pre-selling a condominium project in Ontario, the developer must be registered with Tarion Warranty Corporation and must file a disclosure statement with Tarion that describes the project's material attributes — the site, the unit descriptions, the purchase price, the common elements, the projected occupancy date, and the other material terms. The disclosure statement is also provided to each purchaser at or before the time of executing the purchase agreement, and the purchaser's rescission rights run from the date the disclosure statement is provided.

Material changes to the project — changes that would reasonably affect a purchaser's decision to buy — trigger the requirement to amend the disclosure statement and re-serve it on purchasers, which restarts the purchaser's rescission rights for the affected amendment. The development manager and legal counsel manage the disclosure process, but the Senior Analyst should understand its mechanics because material changes to the project's design, unit mix, or occupancy timing can trigger disclosure amendments that affect the pre-sale count and the project's construction loan closing readiness.

## 12.3 The Condominium Sales Program

<div id="ol-ch12-2"></div>

The sales program for a condominium project is a multi-year undertaking that typically begins 12 to 24 months before construction start and runs through the final closing of the last unit after substantial performance. It has a defined structure, a sequence of phases, and a set of financial outputs that the Senior Analyst tracks throughout.

### The Sales Centre and Presentation Materials

The sales centre is the developer's primary marketing venue — the physical environment in which prospective purchasers experience the project and are converted from prospects to buyers. Sales centres for high-rise condominium projects are typically substantial investments: a dedicated space (often in the project building's ground floor or in a nearby street-front location), furnished with scale models, renderings, material samples, and a representative model suite. The cost of a sales centre for a major GTA condominium project can range from $500,000 to $2 million or more depending on its size, fit-out quality, and the developer's brand positioning.

The marketing and sales budget includes all costs of the sales program: sales centre build-out and operation, model suite construction (often built within the building itself at the completion of one of the lower floors), advertising, digital marketing, printed materials, sales event production, broker relations, and sales commissions. The total marketing and sales budget for a large GTA high-rise condominium project typically runs from 2% to 4% of projected gross revenue, with significant front-loading in the pre-launch and launch phases when awareness and prospect generation activity is highest.

### Sales Launch Phases

Condominium sales programs in the GTA market typically unfold in a defined sequence of launch phases, each serving a specific strategic purpose:

**Platinum and VIP Broker Launch**: Before the public launch, the developer offers access to a curated group of platinum brokers — real estate agents with strong investor-purchaser networks and a track record of delivering qualified buyers quickly. The platinum launch allows the developer to generate a substantial volume of agreements quickly, building momentum and providing early validation of pricing assumptions. Platinum brokers typically receive premium commission terms (sometimes a flat amount per unit above standard) in exchange for their early commitment and their purchaser volume.

**Public Launch**: After the broker launch, the project is opened to the general public through the sales centre, advertising, and open-house events. The public launch is typically the point at which the developer's marketing investment in advertising, digital channels, and public relations produces the broadest prospect exposure.

**Ongoing Sales**: After the initial launch velocity subsides, the sales program moves into an ongoing phase of prospect qualification, presentation, and conversion for the remaining unsold units. The pace of ongoing sales depends on market conditions, the project's competitive positioning, and the developer's pricing strategy.

The Senior Analyst tracks each phase's sales velocity and compares it to the project's pro forma assumptions. A launch that underperforms the pro forma in its first 60 days is an early signal that requires immediate analysis — is the issue pricing, product, marketing reach, or market conditions?

### The Purchase Agreement

The purchase agreement is the binding contract between the developer and the unit purchaser. For a condominium project, the purchase agreement is typically prepared by the developer's solicitor and is structured to protect the developer's rights while meeting the statutory requirements of the *Condominium Act, 1998* and the regulations thereunder.

Key commercial terms in the purchase agreement from a Senior Analyst perspective include: the unit identifier (stack and suite number), the unit type and approximate size (typically described with the caveat that final measurements will govern), the purchase price, the deposit schedule, the projected occupancy date (with the developer's rights to extend occupancy and the limits on those rights), the interim occupancy fee (addressed in Section 12.4 below), the finishing selections process, and the rights and obligations on default by either party.

The purchase agreement's terms regarding the projected occupancy date and the developer's extension rights are particularly significant. Ontario's *Condominium Act* regulates the developer's rights to extend the projected occupancy date and requires that purchasers be notified of extensions within specified timescales. Excessive or repeated extensions can trigger purchaser rescission rights in some circumstances, and the legal framework here is technical and evolving. The Senior Analyst tracks the projected occupancy date schedule and ensures that any amendments are properly documented and that the sales team's occupancy timeline communications are consistent with the legal team's disclosure filings.

### Commission Structures

Commissions paid to real estate brokers and agents on condominium pre-sales are a significant component of the marketing and sales budget. In the GTA market, commission structures for new condominium sales typically range from 3% to 5% of the purchase price per unit, with variations based on the launch phase (platinum brokers often receiving higher rates), the unit type (parking and locker commissions may be structured separately), and any volume or performance incentives.

Commissions are typically paid in two tranches: a portion at firm sale (after rescission period has expired and deposit is cleared) and the balance at closing. The Senior Analyst tracks commission accruals and payments against the budget, ensuring that commission expenses are being recognized in the correct period and that the total commission exposure is accurately reflected in the project's financial position.

## 12.4 Interim Occupancy and Condominium Registration

<div id="ol-ch12-3"></div>

The period between a purchaser's initial occupancy of their unit and the registration of the condominium corporation — the legal event that converts the building from a single-title property owned by the developer into a registered condominium with individual unit titles — is one of the distinctive features of Ontario condominium development that has no equivalent in other project types. Understanding its mechanics is essential for any Senior Analyst working on a high-rise condominium project.

### The Interim Occupancy Period

When a unit is sufficiently complete for occupancy but the condominium has not yet been registered, the purchaser occupies the unit under an **interim occupancy** arrangement governed by the *Condominium Act, 1998*. During interim occupancy, the purchaser is in possession of the unit and is responsible for its care, but the developer still holds title to the building. The purchase price has not yet been paid — closing occurs at or after registration, not at interim occupancy.

Because the developer is holding title and carrying the building's financing costs during interim occupancy, the Act provides a mechanism for the developer to collect an interim occupancy fee from each occupying purchaser. The interim occupancy fee is intended to approximate the carrying costs attributable to that unit and is calculated under a prescribed formula: the monthly interest on the unpaid purchase price balance (at the interest rate stated in the purchase agreement or the prescribed rate, whichever is lower), plus the monthly contribution to the condominium's estimated common expense budget, plus the monthly property tax instalment for the unit.

Crucially, the interim occupancy fee is capped at the prescribed calculation — the developer cannot charge a higher fee, and the fee is not considered rent. The fee does not constitute income to the developer in the way that rental revenue does; it is an offset to carrying costs, not a revenue stream. For the Senior Analyst, interim occupancy fees are tracked as a financing line item that partially offsets the construction loan interest and operating costs during the registration period, not as gross revenue.

### Registration and Closing

Condominium registration — the filing of the declaration, description, and bylaws with the Land Registry Office that creates the condominium corporation and its unit titles — typically occurs several months after substantial performance, following the completion of all inspections, the preparation of the condominium documents, and the municipality's confirmation of all outstanding site plan and development agreement obligations.

Once the condominium is registered, the developer issues **notices of closing** to purchasers, triggering the closing process for each unit. Closings typically occur on a stacked schedule over a period of several weeks, with the developer's solicitor coordinating the transfer of title, the application of deposits held in trust, the paydown of the construction loan, and the discharge of the construction mortgage.

The registration-to-closing period is a critical cash flow event for the project. The developer has been carrying the construction loan through the entire construction period, interim occupancy period, and registration period — typically two to three years or more — without receiving the bulk of the project's revenue. When closings occur, the deposit proceeds held in trust are applied to the purchase price, the balance is paid by the purchasers' mortgages or cash, and the construction loan is repaid from the closing proceeds. The Senior Analyst models the closing schedule and the corresponding loan paydown in detail, tracking whether closings are occurring on the projected timeline and whether any purchaser defaults are affecting the paydown pace.

### Purchaser Default at Closing

A purchaser who fails to close on the scheduled date is in default under the purchase agreement. The developer's remedies typically include forfeiture of the purchaser's deposit (which the developer retains), the right to re-sell the unit to a new purchaser, and the right to sue the defaulting purchaser for any loss on re-sale.

Purchaser default rates at closing vary significantly with market conditions. In a rising market, defaults are rare — purchasers who bought at pre-construction prices are closing into units worth more than they paid. In a flat or declining market, some purchasers who bought at peak pricing find their units worth less at closing than the purchase price, and the incentive to default is real. The Senior Analyst models the project's closing performance under a range of default assumptions, ensuring that the project's financial projections reflect a realistic assessment of closing risk rather than assuming 100% closing of all firm agreements.

## 12.5 The Purpose-Built Rental Leasing Program

<div id="ol-ch12-4"></div>

For a purpose-built rental project — a building developed with the intention of holding it as a rental income property rather than selling individual units — the revenue model is fundamentally different from the condominium model. There are no pre-sales, no deposit trusts, and no closings. Revenue is generated through executed lease agreements with tenants, and the project's financial success depends on achieving the projected stabilized occupancy at the projected rents within the projected lease-up timeframe.

### Lease-Up: The Revenue Race Against the Financing Clock

The period from first occupancy to stabilized occupancy — the point at which the building has achieved the occupancy rate and the rent levels assumed in the permanent financing underwriting — is called the **lease-up period**. During lease-up, the building is generating partial revenue (as units are occupied) but is not yet generating the full revenue assumed in the pro forma. The project continues to carry the construction loan, whose term typically includes a defined lease-up period after substantial performance during which the developer is expected to reach stabilization.

The construction lender's stabilization covenant defines the occupancy rate and revenue level the project must achieve within the lease-up period to trigger conversion to permanent financing (or, on CMHC-insured construction loans, to trigger the insurance premium advance and the term phase of the loan). A typical stabilization covenant for an Ontario purpose-built rental project might require 90% to 95% physical occupancy and a minimum debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) as measured by actual net operating income divided by annual debt service.

Failure to achieve stabilization within the loan's lease-up period is a potential covenant breach that can trigger loan default remedies. The lease-up pace and the rent levels being achieved are therefore not simply operational metrics — they are covenant compliance metrics with direct implications for the project's financing structure.

### Leasing Fundamentals

The leasing program for a purpose-built rental building involves several components:

**Leasing team selection**: The developer selects either an in-house leasing team or a third-party property management and leasing firm to manage the leasing program. In the GTA rental market, the majority of large purpose-built rental projects are managed by professional third-party operators with dedicated leasing staff. The leasing management agreement defines the scope of services, the fee structure (typically a combination of a monthly management fee based on collected revenue and a per-unit leasing fee for new tenancies), and the performance targets.

**Lease-up marketing**: Purpose-built rental buildings in Ontario market to prospective tenants through a combination of listing platforms (Rentals.ca, Zumper, Kijiji, and the operator's own website are primary channels), digital advertising, broker relations, and signage. The marketing for a rental building is a sustained lower-cost program rather than the concentrated launch-event model of condominium sales. The leasing team begins marketing activity typically 60 to 90 days before the first units are ready for occupancy, building a prospect pipeline that allows occupancy to begin immediately upon first available units.

**Lease form and terms**: Residential tenancies in Ontario are governed by the *Residential Tenancies Act, 2006* (RTA). All residential lease agreements must use the province's Standard Lease form (mandated since 2018), and the terms of the tenancy are substantially governed by the RTA regardless of what the lease says. The RTA establishes protections for tenants that significantly constrain the developer's rights as landlord, including limits on rent increases for in-place tenants (annual guideline increases set by the province), restrictions on eviction grounds, and requirements for maintaining the unit in a good state of repair.

**Rent-free periods and inducements**: In a soft leasing market, the operator may offer rent-free periods (one or two months of free rent on a 12-month lease, for example) or other inducements to attract tenants quickly. These concessions reduce the effective rent received and must be tracked against the pro forma assumptions. The Senior Analyst accounts for concessions by calculating the **effective rent** (the cash collected over the lease term divided by the term length) rather than the face rent stated in the lease, and comparing effective rents to pro forma net effective rents.

### The Rent Roll and Occupancy Tracking

The **rent roll** is the foundational financial document of a rental building's operating performance. It is a unit-by-unit listing of all suites in the building, with the current lease status (occupied, vacant, or under lease but not yet occupied), the monthly rent, the lease start and end dates, and the rent due date for each unit. The rent roll is updated at least monthly and is the primary document reviewed by the project's lender (through its project monitor or asset manager) to assess lease-up performance.

The Senior Analyst compiles or reviews the rent roll monthly and computes the following metrics:

**Physical occupancy rate** — the percentage of units that have a signed lease and an occupying tenant, typically expressed as a trailing 30-day average.

**Economic occupancy rate** — the percentage of potential gross rent that is actually being collected after vacancies, concessions, and arrears. Economic occupancy is always lower than physical occupancy and is the more meaningful measure of revenue performance.

**Average rent per suite** and **average rent per square foot** — tracked by suite type (studio, one-bedroom, one-bedroom plus den, two-bedroom, etc.) against pro forma assumptions.

**Net Operating Income (NOI)** — rental revenue less operating expenses (property taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, management fees, and other operating costs). NOI is the income metric against which the permanent financing's DSCR covenant is tested.

**Vacancy loss** — the amount of potential gross rent lost to vacant suites, expressed as a dollar amount and as a percentage of potential gross revenue.

**Bad debt** — the amount of rent receivable that is not expected to be collected from current or former tenants, net of any security deposit applied.

## 12.6 The Sales and Leasing Budget — Financial Treatment

<div id="ol-ch12-5"></div>

The marketing, sales, and leasing function generates both costs and revenues, and the Senior Analyst's responsibility is to ensure that both sides of this ledger are accurately tracked in the project's budget and cost report.

### Marketing and Sales Costs — Condominium

The marketing and sales budget for a condominium project is typically carried as a soft cost category within the project pro forma, separate from hard construction costs but part of the total project cost. The major line items include:

**Sales centre build-out**: The capital cost of constructing, furnishing, and equipping the sales centre, including model suites. This cost is typically expensed as incurred or amortized over the pre-sales period, depending on the developer's accounting treatment.

**Advertising and promotion**: Media spend across digital, print, outdoor, and event channels. These costs are typically front-loaded in the pre-launch and launch phases.

**Commissions**: Brokerage commissions paid on firm sales. These are typically accrued at the time a sale becomes firm (after rescission period expiry) and paid in the agreed tranche structure — a portion at firm sale and the balance at closing. The accrual at firm sale is important; the developer has an obligation to pay the commission at closing even if that closing is two years away.

**Sales centre operations**: Ongoing operating costs of the sales centre during the sales program — rent (if the sales centre is in a leased space), staffing, utilities, and maintenance. These are period costs expensed as incurred.

**Legal — sales program**: Solicitor costs for preparing and updating the purchase agreement, disclosure statements, Tarion filings, and responding to purchaser inquiries. These are tracked separately from general project legal costs.

The total marketing and sales cost budget is typically expressed as a percentage of gross revenue in the pro forma and tracked against that benchmark throughout the sales program. Cost overruns in the marketing budget are a budget issue with direct implications for project margin.

### Marketing and Sales Costs — Rental

For a purpose-built rental project, the marketing costs are structured differently. The pre-development marketing (market research, brand development, website) is a soft cost in the project budget. The lease-up marketing costs are typically modelled as an operating expense in the lease-up period operating budget rather than as a capital cost, and are funded from the construction loan's operating cost reserve or from equity.

Leasing commissions for rental buildings are typically paid per executed lease — a one-time payment per unit leased, expressed as a fixed dollar amount per unit or as a fraction of one month's rent. These commissions are smaller per unit than condominium sale commissions but are paid on a faster cycle as units are leased during lease-up.

### Revenue Recognition — Condominium

Under standard accounting treatment for condominium development, revenue from unit sales is recognized at closing — the date on which title transfers and the developer receives the purchase price proceeds. Revenue is not recognized at the time the purchase agreement is signed or at the time the agreement becomes firm, even though from a project management perspective those are the significant commercial milestones.

Interim occupancy fees are not revenue recognition events; they are carrying cost offsets.

For financial reporting purposes, the project's revenue position prior to closing is represented by the deposit trust balance and the contract backlog (the total value of firm purchase agreements whose closings have not yet occurred). The Senior Analyst should be clear about the distinction between the project's revenue backlog (future revenue from firm agreements) and recognized revenue (proceeds of completed closings), and should ensure that project financial statements accurately reflect this distinction.

### Revenue Recognition — Rental

For a purpose-built rental project, revenue is recognized on a cash basis as rent is collected, or on a straight-line basis over the lease term under applicable accounting standards. The project's revenue performance is measured against the pro forma's projected rent schedule, and the gap between actual and projected revenue in the lease-up period is the key financial metric tracked by the Senior Analyst and reported to the lender.

## 12.7 Sales Velocity, Absorption, and the Pro Forma

<div id="ol-ch12-6"></div>

The pro forma underpinning a residential development project contains explicit assumptions about how quickly units will be sold or leased — the absorption rate — and at what prices or rents. These assumptions are derived from the market research undertaken in pre-development, and they drive the project's cash flow projections, financing structure, and return expectations. When sales or leasing performance deviates from those assumptions, the Senior Analyst's job is to identify the deviation early, quantify its financial impact, and support the management team's response.

### Measuring Sales Velocity

Sales velocity for a condominium project is typically measured as the number of firm sales per week or per month over a defined period. The relevant period is the active sales phase (from launch through close of the primary sales program), not the entire project lifecycle.

The pro forma's velocity assumption determines the timing of milestone achievements: when the lender's pre-sale threshold will be reached, when the construction loan will close, when construction will start, and when the last unit will be sold. A velocity shortfall — sales coming in below the pro forma pace — pushes all of those milestones forward, which in turn extends the project's duration, increases interest costs, and may require additional marketing expenditure to stimulate demand.

The Senior Analyst tracks the weekly and monthly sale count, the cumulative sales and the percentage of total units sold, and the weighted average sale price per square foot against pro forma assumptions. The velocity trend — whether the pace is accelerating, stable, or decelerating — is often as important as the point-in-time count, because a decelerating pace signals that the available demand pool is being exhausted and that the project may face a prolonged tail of unsold inventory.

### Price Performance

The average sale price per square foot achieved in the sales program should be tracked against the pro forma assumption and against comparable competitive sales. Price performance has two dimensions: the absolute level (are average prices above or below the pro forma?) and the mix effect (are higher-priced units or lower-priced units selling disproportionately?).

The mix effect is important because a project might be selling at prices that look consistent with the pro forma on an average basis but is achieving that average because it has sold mostly small, lower-priced units and is leaving the larger, higher-priced units unsold. If the remaining unsold inventory is disproportionately weighted toward larger, higher-priced units in a softening market, the effective average price on the remaining inventory may be lower than the pro forma assumed.

The Senior Analyst tracks both the overall blended price per square foot and the price performance by suite type (studio, one-bedroom, one-bedroom plus den, two-bedroom, three-bedroom, and penthouses), comparing actual achieved prices against the pro forma suite-type assumptions.

### Absorption and the Competition

A project does not absorb in isolation — it absorbs in the context of competing supply. The Senior Analyst should maintain a **competitive survey** — a tracking document that monitors new unit releases, pricing, and sales velocity at competing projects within the relevant trade area. The competitive survey supports two functions: it provides context for the project's own velocity and pricing performance (a slowdown that reflects market-wide conditions is different from a slowdown that reflects project-specific competitive disadvantage), and it provides early warning of competitive supply conditions that were not anticipated in the pro forma.

For a purpose-built rental project, the competitive survey tracks available comparable rental units in the market, prevailing rent levels by suite type, and vacancy rates at competing buildings. A lease-up that is proceeding slower than pro forma in a market where competing buildings are also running elevated vacancies suggests a market-level condition; the same underperformance in a market with tight vacancies at competitors suggests a project-specific issue.

## 12.8 Purchaser Communications and Closing Readiness

<div id="ol-ch12-7"></div>

For a condominium project, the period between the signing of a purchase agreement and the closing of that agreement can span two to four years. During that period, the developer has an obligation to maintain the purchaser's engagement, confidence, and preparation for closing. A purchaser who is well-informed about construction progress, occupancy timing, finishing selections, and closing requirements is more likely to close on time and less likely to exercise rescission rights or to default.

### Occupancy Date Communications

The *Condominium Act, 1998* and Tarion's rules establish a framework for communicating projected occupancy dates to purchasers. The developer is required to provide purchasers with a projected occupancy date (typically stated as a specific date or a defined range) and must provide notice within specific timescales if that date is to be extended. Extensions within certain defined periods and of limited duration do not trigger purchaser rescission rights; extensions that exceed those parameters can give purchasers the right to rescind their agreements and receive their deposits back.

The Senior Analyst tracks the current projected occupancy date, the history of extensions, and the construction schedule's projected completion against the legal framework's permitted extension parameters. A construction delay that pushes occupancy beyond the developer's permitted extension parameters is not just a construction issue — it is a legal issue that can produce a wave of purchaser rescissions and a fundamental disruption to the project's revenue model.

### Finishing Selections

Most condominium purchase agreements give purchasers the right to make selections from a defined menu of finishes — flooring type, kitchen cabinet colour, countertop material, and similar items. Finishing selections are collected by the developer's design team (or by a designated design consultant) at a defined point during the construction period, typically 12 to 18 months before the projected occupancy date, so that the selected finishes can be ordered and installed within the construction schedule.

The finishing selections process has a cost dimension that the Senior Analyst must track. The base purchase price includes a standard finish package; upgrades selected above the base are upgrade charges that generate additional revenue for the developer. Upgrades also generate additional cost — the upgraded materials must be procured and installed — and the net margin on upgrades is typically tracked separately from base unit margin. Uncollected upgrade revenue (purchasers who have selected upgrades but whose upgrade agreements have not been fully executed) is a risk that the Senior Analyst should flag.

### Pre-Closing Walkthroughs and the PDI

The Pre-Delivery Inspection, or PDI, is a walk-through inspection of the unit conducted jointly by the developer's representative and the purchaser prior to the unit's interim occupancy. The PDI is a statutory requirement under the Tarion program and serves two purposes: it gives the purchaser the opportunity to identify deficiencies that the developer must address before or after occupancy, and it establishes the baseline condition of the unit for warranty purposes.

The PDI has operational implications for the construction and quality control teams (deficiency identification drives finishing work priorities) and for the development management team (PDI scheduling drives the occupancy timeline for each floor). The Senior Analyst tracks the PDI schedule against the construction schedule and flags any floor or unit groupings where PDI scheduling is behind the pace needed to support the projected occupancy timeline.

## 12.9 Broker Relations and the Co-Operating Broker Network

<div id="ol-ch12-8"></div>

For a condominium project in the GTA market, the co-operating broker network — the pool of registered real estate agents and brokers who bring purchasers to the project in exchange for a commission — is the primary channel through which the majority of units are sold. An effective developer maintains a strong broker relations program as a standing business development function, not as a project-specific activity.

### The Broker Relations Program

Broker relations for a new condominium project typically involve: an organized database of active agents in the target market, structured communication to those agents about the project's availability, launch timing, and features, organized broker events (preview events, VIP launch nights), and a reliable, prompt commission payment record. A developer with a reputation for paying commissions on time and in full has a structural advantage in the broker market; agents remember which developers treat them well.

The platinum and VIP launch structure described in Section 12.3 is built on broker relations. The developer cultivates relationships with the 50 to 100 brokers who consistently represent the most active investors and end-user buyers in the new condominium market, and those relationships are the primary driver of early launch velocity.

The Senior Analyst's role in broker relations is limited but defined: tracking commission payments against the budget and flagging any disputes or delinquencies to the development manager. Commission disputes — situations where a broker claims a commission that the developer disputes — require legal involvement and can become material if the disputed amounts are significant.

### In-House Sales Teams

Some developers maintain in-house sales teams who sell directly to purchasers without a co-operating broker, keeping the commission in-house as a margin improvement. In-house sales models are most effective for developers with strong brand recognition and an existing purchaser database from prior projects — buyers who have purchased with the developer before are more likely to purchase again without the involvement of an agent. For a developer with a less established brand or in a new market, the in-house model sacrifices market reach in exchange for commission savings, and the tradeoff must be evaluated carefully.

## 12.10 Common Sources of Marketing and Sales Failure (and How to Prevent Them)

<div id="ol-ch12-9"></div>

The patterns of marketing and sales failure that produce material financial impact on development projects tend to recur across market cycles. The following are the most consequential.

**1. Pricing the pro forma, not the market.** Setting pre-sale pricing based on what the pro forma requires to achieve its return rather than what the market evidence supports is one of the most common — and most expensive — errors in residential development. A project priced above the market will experience slow velocity, forcing the developer to choose between reducing prices (and margin) or extending the sales period (and carrying costs). Preventive discipline: ground pro forma pricing in current comparable sales evidence and competitive survey data, not in working backwards from a target return.

**2. Underestimating the marketing budget.** Marketing budgets that are thin relative to project scale or market difficulty produce insufficient prospect generation, slow velocity, and extended sales programs. The temptation to cut the marketing budget as a cost-saving measure is almost always self-defeating — the carrying cost of unsold units over an extended sales period far exceeds the marginal marketing cost that would have sold them earlier. Preventive discipline: establish the marketing budget based on comparable project experience and market conditions, not as a residual after other costs are funded.

**3. Failing to track the broker pre-sale count accurately against the lender's qualifying criteria.** A developer who believes it has met the lender's pre-sale threshold because its headline sale count is above the percentage, but has not verified that those sales qualify under the lender's counting methodology, risks a construction loan closing delay. Preventive discipline: establish the lender's counting methodology at loan commitment and apply that methodology to the sale count tracking throughout the pre-sale program.

**4. Neglecting deposit collection discipline.** Purchase agreements are firm in name only if the required deposit tranches are not being collected on schedule. A purchaser who is allowed to fall behind on deposit payments without formal default process is a purchaser who is signalling potential closing default. Preventive discipline: track deposit collection against the schedule for every active agreement, escalate delinquencies promptly, and enforce default provisions when purchasers fail to make required payments.

**5. Inadequate occupancy date communication.** Purchasers who receive insufficient or inconsistent communication about occupancy timing become anxious, which translates into rescission inquiries, legal complaints, and social media complaints that damage the project's reputation with other purchasers and future prospects. Preventive discipline: establish a structured occupancy update communication program, ensure the communications are prepared by legal counsel or reviewed for compliance with Tarion and statutory disclosure requirements, and maintain a consistent and transparent communication posture with purchasers throughout the construction period.

**6. Missing the lease-up covenant.** A purpose-built rental project that fails to achieve stabilization within the construction loan's lease-up covenant period is in a potential default scenario that is expensive to cure. The cure options typically include paying down the construction loan with additional equity to reduce the DSCR target, negotiating an extension of the stabilization period with the lender, or reducing rents to achieve occupancy at the cost of revenue — all of which are financially damaging. Preventive discipline: monitor lease-up performance monthly against the covenant benchmark from day one of occupancy, escalate any tracking shortfall to the development manager and the lender relationship manager, and develop a response plan before the covenant breach actually occurs.

**7. Disconnection between the sales program and the construction schedule.** Pre-selling units that depend on construction decisions not yet made (suite sizes that have not been finalized, finish specifications that will change, amenity programs that are subject to value engineering) creates disclosure obligations and purchaser dissatisfaction when the built product differs from what was sold. Preventive discipline: coordinate closely between the sales team, design team, and construction manager so that what is being sold is what is being built, and manage any changes through the disclosure amendment process.

**8. Ignoring closing default risk in the financial model.** A pro forma that assumes 100% closing of all firm sale agreements — with no provision for default — is a pro forma that will disappoint. Even in strong markets, default rates of 2% to 5% are common; in soft markets, default rates can be substantially higher. Preventive discipline: model the project's financial position under a realistic closing default assumption, maintain the contingency reserve with the default scenario in view, and track the pre-closing indicators (deposit delinquencies, purchaser inquiries about rescission, competitive market price movement against purchase prices) that signal elevated default risk.

**9. Over-reliance on investor purchasers.** Projects with a high proportion of investor purchasers (buyers purchasing for rental income rather than owner-occupancy) face higher closing default risk in a soft market, because investors are more likely to make economically rational default decisions when closing prices have moved against them. Projects with diversified purchaser profiles — a mix of end-users and investors across different suite types — have more resilient closing performance. Preventive discipline: track the purchaser profile composition as part of the sales analytics and flag any disproportionate concentration in investor purchasers, particularly in smaller suite types that are the dominant investor vehicle.

**10. Inadequate tracking of the revenue-to-cost relationship.** A project that is selling units but whose revenue per square foot is declining (because remaining inventory is in less desirable positions or suite types) while costs are increasing (due to construction overruns) is a project whose margin is being compressed from both directions. The Senior Analyst's discipline is to track the margin on remaining-to-close agreements against the project's current cost forecast, not against the original pro forma, and to escalate if the remaining margin is being eroded to a level that threatens the project's return targets or its ability to repay the construction loan.

## 12.11 Key Metrics and Terms — Reference Table

<div id="ol-ch12-10"></div>

The following table summarizes the metrics, instruments, and terminology introduced in this chapter for reference during quizzes, applied exercises, and on-the-job application.

| Metric / Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Absorption Rate** | The pace at which units in a project are sold or leased over a defined period, typically expressed as units per month. |
| **Pre-Sale Threshold** | The minimum percentage of units under firm purchase agreements required by the construction lender before advancing construction funds. |
| **Firm Sale** | A purchase agreement in which the statutory rescission period has expired and the purchaser's deposit is non-refundable. |
| **Deposit Trust** | The regulated account in which purchaser deposits are held by a qualified trustee until closing or rescission, as required under the Condominium Act, 1998. |
| **Rescission Period** | The statutory period during which a condominium purchaser may cancel their agreement and receive a full deposit refund without penalty; currently ten business days in Ontario. |
| **Tarion Disclosure Statement** | The document, filed with Tarion and delivered to each purchaser, that describes the material attributes of the condominium project and triggers the purchaser's rescission rights. |
| **Interim Occupancy** | The period under Ontario condominium law during which a purchaser occupies their unit before condominium registration and title transfer at closing. |
| **Interim Occupancy Fee** | The prescribed carrying cost fee paid by a purchaser to the developer during the interim occupancy period; calculated as the sum of interest on the unpaid purchase price, monthly common expense contribution, and monthly property tax instalment. |
| **Condominium Registration** | The legal event — filing of the declaration, description, and bylaws — that creates the condominium corporation and its individual unit titles, enabling closings to occur. |
| **Closing** | The transaction at which title to a condominium unit transfers from the developer to the purchaser in exchange for the purchase price (less deposits already in trust). |
| **Lease-Up Period** | The period from first occupancy to stabilized occupancy for a purpose-built rental project; the period during which the building is filling with tenants. |
| **Stabilization** | The point at which a rental project achieves the occupancy rate and NOI level required by the construction lender's covenant to trigger conversion to permanent financing. |
| **Stabilization Covenant** | The lender's contractual requirement specifying the occupancy and NOI benchmarks the project must achieve within a defined post-occupancy period. |
| **Physical Occupancy Rate** | The percentage of suites in a rental building that are occupied by tenants under signed leases. |
| **Economic Occupancy Rate** | The percentage of potential gross rent actually collected after adjusting for vacancies, concessions, and arrears. |
| **Net Operating Income (NOI)** | Rental revenue less all operating expenses (excluding debt service and capital expenditures); the income measure used to value income-producing properties and test DSCR covenants. |
| **Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)** | NOI divided by annual debt service; the primary financial covenant tested at stabilization on permanent rental financing. |
| **Rent Roll** | Unit-by-unit listing of all suites in a rental building with lease status, monthly rent, and lease term information; the primary document tracking lease-up and operating performance. |
| **Effective Rent** | The cash collected over a lease term divided by the number of months in the term; accounts for rent-free periods and other concessions to produce the actual revenue rate per suite. |
| **Vacancy Loss** | The amount of potential gross rent lost due to unoccupied suites, expressed as a dollar amount or as a percentage of potential gross revenue. |
| **Bad Debt** | Rent receivable from current or former tenants that is not expected to be collected, net of any security deposits applied. |
| **Pre-Delivery Inspection (PDI)** | The statutory pre-occupancy walkthrough conducted jointly by the developer and the purchaser to identify deficiencies and establish the baseline condition of the unit for warranty purposes. |
| **Upgrade Revenue** | Additional revenue generated when purchasers select finish upgrades above the base package included in the purchase price. |
| **Sales Velocity** | The pace at which purchase agreements are executed in a condominium project's sales program, typically measured as firm sales per week or per month. |
| **Competitive Survey** | Ongoing tracking document monitoring comparable competing projects' unit availability, pricing, and velocity; used to contextualize the subject project's own performance. |
| **Commission Accrual** | Recognition of the commission obligation at the time a sale becomes firm, regardless of the timing of the actual commission payment (typically split between firm sale and closing). |
| **Platinum Launch** | The initial pre-public offering of condominium units to a curated group of high-volume real estate brokers, typically at premium commission terms, to generate early sales momentum. |
| **Purchaser Default** | A purchaser's failure to close on the scheduled closing date; the developer's remedies typically include deposit forfeiture and the right to re-sell the unit. |
| **Marketing and Sales Budget** | The total planned expenditure for the sales program, including sales centre build-out, advertising, commissions, and ongoing operations; typically expressed as a percentage of projected gross revenue. |

## AE-12.1 — Pre-Sale Threshold Analysis

### Scenario

You are the Senior Analyst on a 28-storey condominium project with a total of 320 units. The construction lender requires 65% firm sales before it will issue a formal loan commitment and proceed to construction loan closing. The project has been in active sales for 11 weeks since its public launch.

The sales team has provided you with the following status summary:

| Category | Units |
|---|---|
| Agreements executed, still within 10-business-day rescission period | 24 |
| Agreements executed, rescission period expired, deposit fully cleared | 162 |
| Agreements executed, rescission period expired, deposit cheque pending clearance | 18 |
| Agreements executed, rescission period expired, initial deposit received but second tranche (due at 30 days) not yet received | 11 |
| Conditional agreements (financing condition outstanding) | 9 |
| Verbal commitments from platinum brokers, no executed agreement yet | 14 |
| **Total reported as "sold" by marketing team** | **238** |

The project has a total unit count of 320. The lender's qualification criteria (as stated in the commitment letter) require: (a) rescission period expired, (b) deposits cleared and held in trust in the required amounts, and (c) no outstanding conditions.

### Questions

1. How many units qualify as firm sales under the lender's criteria, and what is the qualifying pre-sale percentage?
2. Which categories require clarification or resolution before they can count toward the threshold?
3. What is the gap between the lender's threshold and the current qualifying count, and what does this imply for the construction loan closing timeline?

---

## AE-12.1 — Solution

**Question 1 — Qualifying pre-sale count**

Applying the lender's three-part criteria (rescission expired, deposits cleared and in trust, no outstanding conditions):

- Rescission period still running (24 units): **Do not qualify.** The rescission period has not expired; these purchasers may still rescind.
- Rescission expired, deposits fully cleared (162 units): **Qualify**, subject to confirmation that the trust account reflects the cleared deposit amounts. These are the project's core firm sale count.
- Rescission expired, deposit cheque pending clearance (18 units): **Do not qualify yet.** The deposit has not cleared; until the funds are confirmed in trust, the lender's criterion (b) is not met. These will qualify once cheques clear, which is typically 2 to 5 business days. Flag for re-assessment within the week.
- Rescission expired, second deposit tranche not received (11 units): **Do not qualify.** The required deposit amount is not fully in trust. These purchasers are technically in default on their deposit obligations; the development manager should be notified and the default process reviewed. These units may become problem agreements if the purchasers do not cure the default.
- Conditional agreements with financing conditions (9 units): **Do not qualify.** Outstanding conditions mean these are not firm. These will qualify if and when conditions are satisfied; the timeline depends on purchaser financing, which is outside the developer's control.
- Verbal commitments, no executed agreements (14 units): **Do not qualify.** No written agreement exists; verbal commitments have no legal status. Do not include these in any count for lender purposes.

**Qualifying count: 162 units. Qualifying pre-sale percentage: 162 ÷ 320 = 50.6%.**

**Question 2 — Categories requiring clarification or resolution**

The 18 units with pending cheque clearance are the most readily resolvable — these should qualify within a few business days if the cheques clear without issue. Confirm with the trust solicitor that there are no returned cheques in this group.

The 11 units with second deposit tranche delinquency require active follow-up. The default provision in the purchase agreement should be reviewed to confirm whether formal default notice is required, what the cure period is, and what the developer's remedies are. Some of these purchasers may respond to a reminder; others may be walking away quietly and should be identified promptly so their units can be re-released.

The 9 conditional agreements need status from each purchaser or their agent on the financing condition timeline. A condition that has been outstanding for more than 30 days without update is a concern.

The 24 units in rescission will become firm (or rescind) within approximately two weeks from their respective agreement dates; monitor closely but no action is required unless rescission notices are received.

**Question 3 — Gap analysis and closing timeline implications**

The lender's threshold is 65% of 320 = **208 qualifying units**. The current qualifying count is **162 units**. The gap is **46 units**.

In the most optimistic near-term scenario: the 18 pending-clearance deposits clear this week (bringing the count to 180), and some portion of the 24 rescission-period agreements firm up without rescission (adding those to the count over the next two weeks). A realistic outcome of 15 to 18 firming from the current rescission group would bring the count to approximately 195 to 198 — still 10 to 13 units short of the 208 threshold.

If weekly velocity is tracking at approximately 20 new firm sales per week (consistent with the 162 firmed over 11 weeks after accounting for the pipeline categories), the gap of roughly 10 to 13 units is approximately two to three additional weeks of sales activity, assuming the velocity is maintained.

The implication for the construction loan closing timeline: assuming the developer and lender need approximately 4 to 6 weeks from threshold achievement to coordinate the loan closing (title insurance, survey update, legal documentation, lender's final draw conditions), the construction loan closing is realistically 6 to 9 weeks away from today. Any velocity deterioration or unexpected rescissions will push this out further. The construction start date should be communicated to the construction manager as contingent on loan closing, and the construction schedule should be reviewed to confirm whether the 6-to-9-week outlook creates any procurement or mobilization constraints.

## AE-12.2 — Lease-Up Performance Review

### Scenario

You are the Senior Analyst on a 22-storey purpose-built rental project that achieved substantial performance 4 months ago. The building contains 268 suites across five suite types. The construction loan includes a stabilization covenant requiring 93% physical occupancy and a DSCR of 1.15x within 12 months of the first occupancy date. The first occupancy date was 4 months ago; the covenant deadline is therefore 8 months from today.

The pro forma projected lease-up at a pace of 24 units per month, reaching stabilization at month 11. The current rent roll shows the following:

| Suite Type | Total Units | Occupied | Monthly Face Rent (per unit) | Effective Rent (per unit, net of concessions) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Studio | 42 | 38 | $1,850 | $1,695 |
| 1-Bedroom | 84 | 71 | $2,400 | $2,210 |
| 1-Bed + Den | 52 | 40 | $2,750 | $2,530 |
| 2-Bedroom | 74 | 53 | $3,200 | $2,920 |
| 3-Bedroom | 16 | 9 | $4,100 | $3,740 |
| **Total** | **268** | **211** | — | — |

Monthly operating expenses are running at $96,000 per month (all categories combined). Annual debt service on the construction loan is $1,820,000.

### Questions

1. Calculate the current physical occupancy rate and assess it against the pace needed to meet the stabilization covenant.
2. Calculate the current monthly NOI and annualized DSCR. Does the project meet the covenant today? What is the DSCR gap?
3. What analysis would you prepare for the development manager regarding the 8-month runway to covenant compliance?

---

## AE-12.2 — Solution

**Question 1 — Physical occupancy and required pace**

Current occupied units: 38 + 71 + 40 + 53 + 9 = **211 units**
Total units: 268
**Current physical occupancy: 211 ÷ 268 = 78.7%**

The covenant requires 93% physical occupancy at 12 months. At 93%, the target occupied unit count is 268 × 0.93 = 249.2, rounded to **250 units**.

Units remaining to lease to meet covenant: 250 − 211 = **39 units**
Time remaining: **8 months**

Required pace from today: 39 units ÷ 8 months = **approximately 5 additional units per month net of any turnover.**

The pro forma assumed 24 units per month during lease-up. The project has leased 211 units in 4 months, implying an average pace of 52.75 units per month — well ahead of the pro forma velocity in early lease-up. However, early lease-up velocity tends to be front-loaded; the remaining 57 vacant units (268 − 211) are likely the harder-to-lease inventory (note the relatively lower occupancy in 3-bedroom and 2-bedroom suites). The pace requirement of 5 net units per month is modest if vacancy is concentrated in suites with adequate demand, but requires scrutiny if the remaining vacant suites face structural demand limitations.

**Question 2 — Current NOI and DSCR**

Monthly effective rent by suite type:
- Studios: 38 × $1,695 = $64,410
- 1-Bed: 71 × $2,210 = $156,910
- 1-Bed + Den: 40 × $2,530 = $101,200
- 2-Bed: 53 × $2,920 = $154,760
- 3-Bed: 9 × $3,740 = $33,660

**Total monthly effective rent: $510,940**

Monthly operating expenses: $96,000
**Monthly NOI: $510,940 − $96,000 = $414,940**
**Annualized NOI: $414,940 × 12 = $4,979,280**

Annual debt service: $1,820,000
**Current annualized DSCR: $4,979,280 ÷ $1,820,000 = 2.74x**

The current DSCR of 2.74x significantly exceeds the covenant threshold of 1.15x. However, the DSCR at the covenant test date will be calculated on the actual NOI at that time, not on today's NOI. If the DSCR covenant is tested on a trailing 12-month NOI basis, the calculation will improve as vacant units are leased. If it is tested on a stabilized forward NOI basis (which some lenders use), then the test depends on the projected fully-occupied NOI, which should also be well above 1.15x given the current trajectory.

**DSCR gap:** There is no gap at current performance — the project exceeds the 1.15x threshold. The binding covenant concern is the **physical occupancy threshold (93%)**, not the DSCR.

**Question 3 — Analysis for the development manager**

The development manager needs an 8-month outlook that addresses both covenant metrics and the key risks to covenant achievement.

The analysis should include:

**Occupancy trajectory**: At the required pace of 5 net units per month, the covenant should be achievable with significant runway. However, the pace analysis should be suite-type specific. The 3-bedroom suites (7 vacant of 16 = 56.3% vacant) and 2-bedroom suites (21 vacant of 74 = 28.4% vacant) have significantly higher vacancy than studios (10.5%) and 1-bedrooms (15.5%). The remaining leasing challenge is concentrated in the larger suites, which are typically harder to lease in the GTA rental market and are more price-sensitive. The analysis should model the required leasing pace for the 3-bedroom and 2-bedroom inventory specifically, not assume that blended vacancy rates will resolve uniformly.

**Concession analysis**: Effective rents are running at a discount to face rents across all suite types, with discounts of approximately 8% to 9%. If these concessions reflect free-rent periods that are winding down for earlier-leased units, the annualized NOI will improve as those periods expire. If the concessions are structural (ongoing inducements needed to attract and retain tenants), they represent a permanent drag on NOI that the DSCR calculation must reflect. The analysis should identify whether the concession rate is improving, stable, or worsening as the lease-up progresses.

**Turnover risk**: Physical occupancy of 78.7% at month 4 is strong, but some early-lease tenants will begin reaching the end of their initial lease terms within the next 8 months. If any of those tenants do not renew, the physical occupancy gains will be partially reversed. The leasing team should be tracking upcoming lease expirations and renewal status.

**Remediation options if trajectory deteriorates**: If the pace of new leases slows, the developer has several options: increasing marketing intensity (additional listing spend, broker incentives, open house events), adjusting concession levels (deeper free-rent periods to attract tenants more quickly, accepting a lower effective rent in exchange for faster occupancy), re-assessing the pricing of the hardest-to-lease suite types, and (as a last resort) approaching the lender proactively with a status update and a recovery plan. The analysis should include a sensitivity scenario showing what happens to the covenant timeline if net new leases average 3 per month versus 5 versus 8, so the development manager can see the range of outcomes clearly.

## AE-12.3 — Closing Performance and Default Risk Assessment

### Scenario

A 24-storey condominium project has registered its condominium and issued notices of closing to all purchasers. The project has a total of 296 units, all of which were under firm purchase agreements at the time the construction loan closed. Closings are scheduled over a 6-week period.

At the end of week 3 of the closing period, the following status has been compiled by the developer's solicitor:

| Category | Units |
|---|---|
| Closings completed (title transferred, proceeds received) | 148 |
| Scheduled for this week (week 4), closings confirmed by purchaser's solicitor | 41 |
| Scheduled for week 4, purchaser's solicitor has not responded to confirm | 18 |
| Scheduled for weeks 5 and 6, no status issues identified | 67 |
| Purchaser has notified developer of inability to close; deposit forfeiture process initiated | 8 |
| Purchaser solicitor has requested an extension of 30 days beyond scheduled closing | 6 |
| No current status; purchaser not responding to solicitor communications | 8 |
| **Total** | **296** |

The average purchase price across all units is $785,000. The construction loan outstanding balance at the start of closings was $118,000,000. The construction loan bears interest at prime plus 1.25% (currently 7.10% per annum). The construction lender has a standard provision requiring the loan to be reduced to $0 by the later of the final scheduled closing date or 45 days after substantial performance.

### Questions

1. What is the current financial exposure from the units in the "no response" and "extension requested" categories, and what actions should the developer take?
2. Based on the information available, model the projected construction loan balance at the end of week 6 (the final scheduled closing date) under a base case and a stress case.
3. What is the daily interest cost on the construction loan, and what is the financial cost of a one-month delay in resolving the outstanding closing issues?

---

## AE-12.3 — Solution

**Question 1 — Exposure analysis and recommended actions**

The 6 units requesting 30-day extensions and the 8 units with no response represent 14 units of potential closing risk, in addition to the 8 confirmed defaults already in the forfeiture process.

**Units requesting 30-day extension (6 units):** These purchasers may be experiencing financing difficulty (their pre-approved mortgages may have expired, their lender may have changed conditions, or their financial position may have changed). Alternatively, some may be attempting to delay a closing they intend to ultimately avoid. The developer should immediately: (a) confirm whether the purchase agreement permits extensions and under what conditions; (b) require the purchaser's solicitor to provide documentation of the reason for the extension request; (c) consult the developer's solicitor on whether granting the extension without security (such as an additional deposit) creates any legal risk; and (d) confirm with the construction lender whether the loan maturity will accommodate a 30-day extension on these units. Six units at an average purchase price of $785,000 represents approximately $4.7 million of delayed closing proceeds — material, but manageable if the construction loan has adequate term remaining.

**Units not responding to solicitor communications (8 units):** Non-responding purchasers are a more serious concern than extension-requesters, because non-response provides no information about whether the purchaser intends to close. The developer's solicitor should send formal default notice to each non-responding purchaser in accordance with the purchase agreement's notice provisions, starting the clock on the cure period. If these purchasers do not respond to formal default notice, the developer should proceed with the deposit forfeiture process in parallel with attempting to re-list and re-sell the units. At an average of $785,000 per unit, 8 units represent approximately $6.3 million of potentially unrecoverable purchase price (to be replaced by re-sale proceeds, which may be higher, at market, or lower depending on market conditions at the time of re-sale).

**Total at-risk exposure at this point in the closing period:** 8 confirmed defaults + 8 non-responsive + 6 extension requests = 22 units, or 7.4% of the total project. The 8 confirmed defaults will produce deposit forfeiture and re-sale programs; the other 14 are in an uncertain status that could resolve positively or negatively.

**Question 2 — Projected loan balance at end of week 6**

**Base case assumptions:** 148 closings completed; 41 week 4 closings all complete; 67 weeks 5-6 closings all complete; 8 confirmed defaults produce forfeiture only (no closing); 6 extension-requesters do not close by week 6 (30-day delay); 8 non-responsive units do not close by week 6.

Units expected to close by week 6: 148 + 41 + 67 = **256 units**
Units not closing by week 6: 8 defaults + 6 extensions + 8 non-responsive = **22 units** (but note: the 18 unconfirmed week 4 closings are already counted in the 41 week 4 closings — re-reading the scenario, the 41 and 18 appear to be separate categories within week 4. Adjusting: confirmed week 4 = 41; unconfirmed week 4 = 18 — assume these are at risk. Revising base case to include only confirmed week 4 closings.)

**Revised base case (conservative):** 148 completed + 41 confirmed week 4 + 67 weeks 5-6 = 256 closings, plus 18 unconfirmed week 4 that may or may not close. For base case, assume 15 of 18 close. Base case closings by week 6: 271.

Average purchase price: $785,000
Gross closing proceeds (271 units): 271 × $785,000 = $212,735,000

Construction loan outstanding: $118,000,000. If closing proceeds exceed the loan balance (as they clearly do at $212M vs $118M), the loan is repaid in full well before all closings complete. The loan pays out after approximately 150 to 155 closings (150 × $785,000 = $117.75M), meaning the loan should be fully retired in week 4 if closings proceed on schedule.

**Stress case:** Assume all 22 at-risk units (8 defaults + 6 extensions + 8 non-responsive) do not close, and 10 of the 18 unconfirmed week 4 close. Stress case closings: 148 + 41 (confirmed) + 10 (unconfirmed) + 67 (weeks 5-6 unaffected) = 266 closings. Proceeds: 266 × $785,000 = $208,810,000 — still far exceeds the $118M loan. The loan is fully retired in both cases.

The risk is not loan repayment in this scenario (the closing proceeds substantially exceed the loan in all cases) — the risk is the **timing** of the loan paydown against the loan's maturity date, and the **interest cost** of any extension required for the non-closing units.

**Question 3 — Daily interest cost and delay expense**

Annual interest at 7.10% on $118,000,000: $118,000,000 × 0.071 = $8,378,000 per year
Daily interest cost: $8,378,000 ÷ 365 = **$22,953 per day**

However, as noted above, the loan balance will be paid down rapidly as closings complete. After 150 closings (approximately mid-week 4 at the current pace), the loan balance reaches zero. The relevant daily interest calculation is therefore on the outstanding balance as it reduces, not on the full $118M for the entire period.

More relevant: the cost of a 30-day delay on the 6 extension units. These units represent $4.71M of closing proceeds. During the 30-day extension, the construction loan (assuming the main loan has been retired from other closings) is at zero, so there is no direct interest cost to the developer on a construction loan basis. The relevant cost is whether the developer has already received all other closing proceeds and distributed them, and whether the 30-day extension creates any residual financing costs or whether the extended units must be carried at the developer's cost of capital. At a developer blended cost of capital of approximately 8% to 10% per annum, a 30-day extension on $4.71M represents a carrying cost of approximately $31,400 to $39,300 for the month — a real cost, but not a major financial event compared to the overall project.

The more significant financial risk from the non-closing units is the cost of re-sale: re-listing fees, marketing costs, commissions, and the risk that the re-sale price is below the original purchase price (in which case the shortfall, less the forfeited deposit, is an unrecovered loss). For 8 confirmed defaults at a deposit of approximately 20% of purchase price ($157,000 per unit), the developer retains approximately $1.256M in forfeited deposits. If re-sale proceeds match the original purchase price, the developer is net positive by the deposit amount. If re-sale proceeds are below the original price (say, by 5%), the developer's net recovery is reduced but the forfeited deposits substantially absorb the loss for reasonable price declines.

## Chapter Summary

Marketing, sales, and leasing are the disciplines through which the developer converts its capital-intensive construction program into the revenue stream that determines the project's financial outcome. This chapter has examined the mechanics of the Ontario condominium pre-sale program — the pre-sale threshold, the deposit trust framework, the Tarion registration and disclosure requirements, the purchase agreement structure, and the post-construction period from interim occupancy through condominium registration and closings. It has addressed the purpose-built rental leasing program — the lease-up period and its covenant obligations, the leasing team and marketing structure, the rent roll and occupancy metrics, and the financial treatment of concessions and vacancy. It has covered the financial mechanics of the sales and leasing budget — marketing costs, commission structures, revenue recognition timing, and the relationship between sales performance and the project's capital structure.
