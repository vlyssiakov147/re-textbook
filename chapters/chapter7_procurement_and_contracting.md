# Chapter 7: Procurement & Contracting

**Phase:** Construction
**Prerequisite Chapters:** 5 (Project Team Assembly), 6 (Design & Architecture Process)

---

> **In Practice — Senior Analyst Perspective**
> Procurement and contracting decisions are made by senior development staff — the VP of Development, the project manager, and legal counsel. As a Senior Analyst, you are unlikely to be negotiating contracts or running tender processes. However, the contracts executed during procurement directly determine the structure of every draw package you will prepare. The contract defines what gets billed, in what format, at what milestones, and under what conditions the lender will advance funds. Understanding procurement means you can read a contract's financial provisions intelligently, reconcile draw requests against the contracted scope, and flag discrepancies before they become lender issues. It also means you understand why certain line items appear in the budget the way they do — and what risks are hiding behind them.

## 7.1 What is Procurement in Real Estate Development?

Procurement is the process by which a developer selects, engages, and contracts the parties who will physically deliver the project. It is where the design leaves the drawing board and becomes a legal and financial commitment. Every decision made during procurement — who builds the project, under what contract, at what price, with what risk allocation — shapes the financial performance of the project from the first draw to the final closeout.

The term procurement covers a spectrum of activities: selecting a delivery model, preparing tender documents, running a competitive bid process, evaluating proposals, negotiating contracts, and executing agreements. On a large high-rise project, procurement is not a single event — it is an ongoing process that begins with engaging the construction manager during pre-construction and continues through the letting of the last trade package months into construction.

### Why Procurement Decisions Are Irreversible

Procurement decisions are among the most consequential and least reversible choices in the development process. Once a construction management agreement is signed and a contractor is mobilized, switching delivery models or replacing the primary contractor mid-project is extraordinarily expensive and disruptive. The cost of a poor procurement decision compounds over the entire construction period — a contractor with inadequate supervision capacity, poor subcontractor relationships, or a misaligned incentive structure will generate friction, delays, and cost overruns for every month of construction.

This irreversibility is why experienced developers invest heavily in the front end of procurement — in defining scope clearly, in evaluating contractors rigorously, and in negotiating contracts carefully — before any commitment is made. The time spent at procurement stage is almost always recovered many times over in avoided disputes and cost overruns.

### The Relationship Between Procurement and the Budget

The construction budget presented to the lender at loan closing is built on procurement assumptions. Hard cost estimates from the quantity surveyor assume a certain delivery model, a certain level of market competition in the tender, and a certain scope definition. When the actual procurement produces results that diverge from those assumptions — whether favourably or unfavourably — the budget must be reconciled.

This reconciliation is the Senior Analyst's terrain. Understanding how procurement assumptions feed into the budget, and how actual tender results compare to those assumptions, is essential for accurate draw package preparation and investor reporting.

## 7.2 Delivery Models — CM, GC, and Design-Build

The delivery model determines the contractual structure of the entire construction phase — who holds what contracts, who bears what risks, and how costs flow to the developer. Chapter 5 introduced the three primary delivery models at a conceptual level. This chapter examines them in greater depth from the perspective of their procurement implications.

### Construction Management (CM)

Under a Construction Management model, the developer engages a Construction Manager who procures and manages all trade contractors on behalf of the developer. There are two variants:

**CCDC 5A — CM as Agent:** The CM acts as the developer's agent. All trade contracts are held directly by the developer. The CM manages those contracts but does not bear financial exposure for trade costs — costs flow through to the developer at actual cost. The CM earns a fee for their management services, typically expressed as a percentage of construction cost (commonly 3% to 6% on GTA high-rise projects), plus reimbursement for their general conditions costs (site supervision staff, site office, equipment).

**CCDC 5B — CM as Constructor:** The CM holds all trade contracts in their own name. The developer contracts only with the CM, who then subcontracts to trades. The CM bears more direct accountability for trade performance but the developer loses some direct visibility into trade costs. CCDC 5B is chosen when the developer wants a single point of contractual responsibility and has a leaner internal team.

**The open-book principle:** Under both CM variants, the developer has access to all trade pricing and actual costs — there are no hidden markups. This transparency is a defining feature of CM delivery and is why it dominates GTA high-rise residential development. When a trade comes in over estimate, the developer sees exactly what happened and why.

**CM procurement timing:** One of the most significant advantages of CM delivery is that the CM can be engaged during pre-construction — before the design is complete — to provide cost estimating and constructability advice. This early engagement allows the design to be developed with cost implications in real time, reducing the risk of large cost surprises at tender.

### Fixed-Price General Contractor (GC)

Under a fixed-price GC model, the developer engages a single General Contractor who takes on full contractual responsibility for delivering the project at an agreed lump-sum price. The GC holds all subcontracts and manages all trades directly. The developer's primary contract is with the GC only.

The key feature of fixed-price GC delivery is cost certainty: once the contract is signed, the GC bears the risk of trade cost increases, scope ambiguities, and productivity shortfalls. If the mechanical subcontractor's costs increase, that is the GC's problem — not the developer's. This cost certainty is valuable but comes at a price: GCs build contingency into their lump-sum price to compensate for the risks they are absorbing. In competitive markets, that contingency may be relatively thin; in tight labour markets or periods of high uncertainty, it can be substantial.

Fixed-price GC delivery requires a substantially complete set of construction documents before tendering — the GC cannot price a lump-sum contract against incomplete drawings. This makes it poorly suited for projects with iterative entitlement processes where design continues to evolve during permitting. It is more commonly used for simpler building types or for developers who have the internal discipline to complete the full design before going to market.

The CCDC 2 is the standard stipulated price contract used for fixed-price GC delivery in Canada.

### Design-Build

Under a Design-Build model, a single entity — the Design-Builder — is responsible for both the design and the construction of the project. The developer contracts with the Design-Builder based on a performance specification describing what the building must achieve, rather than a set of completed drawings.

Design-Build transfers significant design risk to the Design-Builder but also transfers significant design control. The Design-Builder will optimize the design for constructability and cost within the performance specification — which may or may not produce the design outcome the developer envisioned. For high-rise residential projects where design quality is a direct driver of market positioning and regulatory approval, surrendering design control to the Design-Builder is often an unacceptable trade-off.

Design-Build is more commonly used in Ontario for industrial, institutional, and infrastructure projects where performance specifications are well-established and design quality is less variable. It appears occasionally in residential development for standardized building types where the developer has a precise specification and wants maximum cost certainty.

### Choosing a Delivery Model

The choice of delivery model is driven by four factors: the developer's internal capacity, the completeness of the design at the time of procurement, the developer's risk tolerance, and the specific characteristics of the project.

| Factor | Favours CM | Favours Fixed-Price GC | Favours Design-Build |
|--------|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Design completeness | Incomplete / evolving | Complete | Performance spec only |
| Developer team capacity | Strong internal team | Leaner team acceptable | Minimal design involvement |
| Cost transparency | Maximum | Limited | Limited |
| Cost certainty | Lower | Higher | Higher |
| Project type | High-rise residential | Simpler building types | Industrial / institutional |
| Schedule flexibility | Higher | Lower | Higher |

## 7.3 The Tendering Process

Tendering is the process of soliciting, receiving, and evaluating competitive proposals from contractors or trade subcontractors. A well-run tender produces reliable pricing, a competitive field, and a defensible selection decision. A poorly run tender produces unreliable pricing, disputes, and sometimes litigation.

### Tender Document Preparation

The quality of a tender is determined largely by the quality of the tender documents. Tender documents define what contractors are being asked to price — ambiguous or incomplete documents produce unreliable and incomparable bids.

A complete tender package for a CM-delivered high-rise typically includes:

**Instructions to Tenderers** — the rules of the tender process: submission deadline, format requirements, questions procedure, right to reject, and evaluation criteria. Clear instructions to tenderers reduce the risk of non-compliant submissions and disputes about process.

**Scope of Work** — a detailed description of what the tendering contractor is responsible for, including where their scope begins and ends relative to other contracts. Scope gaps — areas that neither the primary contractor nor any other party is clearly responsible for — are one of the most common sources of change orders and disputes. Finding and closing scope gaps before tender is far cheaper than resolving them in the field.

**Drawings and Specifications** — the design documents that define what is to be built. The completeness of drawings and specifications at the time of tender directly determines the reliability of the bids received. Tendering on incomplete documents forces contractors to include contingencies that may not materialize — or to exclude scope that turns up as a change order.

**Form of Contract** — the contract form that the successful tenderer will be required to execute. Contractors price risk into their bids based on contract terms — onerous contract provisions (broad indemnities, unlimited liability, aggressive liquidated damages) will produce higher prices as contractors price the risk they are being asked to absorb.

**Supplementary Conditions** — modifications to the standard CCDC contract terms that reflect the specific requirements of the project, lender, and municipality.

**Pricing Form** — a structured template that all tenderers must complete in the same format, enabling meaningful comparison between bids. A pricing form that allows contractors to present costs in their own format produces bids that are difficult to compare and easy to manipulate.

### Pre-Qualification

On large or complex projects, developers often pre-qualify tenderers before issuing the full tender package. Pre-qualification involves assessing contractors' financial capacity, relevant experience, current workload, bonding capacity, safety record, and key personnel. Only contractors who meet the pre-qualification criteria receive the full tender documents.

Pre-qualification serves two purposes. First, it protects against receiving a low bid from a contractor who lacks the capacity to deliver — a contractor who wins a bid they cannot execute creates far more damage than a higher bid from a capable contractor. Second, it focuses the developer's evaluation effort on a manageable field of qualified candidates rather than an open market response.

### Tender Period Management

During the tender period — the time between issuing documents and receiving submissions — contractors will identify ambiguities, inconsistencies, and gaps in the tender documents and submit formal questions. Managing this process well is important:

All questions and answers should be circulated to all tenderers simultaneously through formal addenda. A tenderer who receives information that others do not has an unfair pricing advantage — and the developer may face disputes about what was and was not part of the contract if the information was not shared universally.

The tender period should be long enough for contractors to price the documents properly. Compressing the tender period to accelerate procurement typically produces contingency-laden bids or non-compliant submissions — neither outcome serves the developer.

### Tender Receipt and Opening

Tenders should be received in a controlled manner — sealed submissions by a specified deadline — and opened in the presence of the developer's team and often their legal counsel. The opening process should be documented carefully. In competitive public procurement, tender openings are typically public; in private development procurement, they are typically confidential.

## 7.4 Evaluating Bids — Price vs. Value

Selecting a contractor based solely on the lowest price is one of the most reliably expensive procurement decisions a developer can make. The lowest bid is frequently not the best value — and on complex high-rise projects, the cost of a poor contractor decision compounds for every month of construction.

### Bid Levelling

Before any meaningful price comparison can be made, bids must be levelled — adjusted to a common scope and assumption basis. Contractors frequently interpret scope differently, include or exclude items inconsistently, and make different assumptions about allowances and contingencies. A bid that appears $500,000 lower than its competitors may simply have excluded a scope item that the others included.

Bid levelling is typically performed by the cost consultant (quantity surveyor) in collaboration with the developer's project manager. The process involves:

1. Reviewing each bid against the tender scope to identify inclusions and exclusions
2. Adjusting each bid to a normalized scope by adding back excluded items at market rates
3. Identifying qualifications and assumptions that affect comparability
4. Producing a levelled comparison that shows the true cost of each proposal on a like-for-like basis

The levelled comparison is the document on which the selection decision is made — not the raw bid submissions.

### Evaluating General Conditions

General conditions — the costs of running the construction site (supervision, site office, temporary facilities, safety, equipment) — vary significantly between contractors and are a major driver of total project cost on a high-rise project. A contractor who proposes lean general conditions may be planning to understaff site supervision, which produces cost savings on paper but delays, quality problems, and increased change orders in practice.

Evaluating general conditions requires understanding what staffing levels and site resources the contractor is proposing, whether those resources are appropriate for the project complexity and duration, and how they compare to what the contractor actually deployed on comparable recent projects.

### Key Personnel

The quality of the superintendent and project manager assigned to the project is arguably more important than the total bid price on a complex high-rise. A highly capable superintendent who catches problems early, manages subcontractors effectively, and maintains schedule discipline will deliver better financial outcomes than a cheaper contractor with a less capable site team.

Procurement evaluation should always include an assessment of the specific individuals proposed — not just the firm's general track record. It is standard practice to require contractors to name their proposed superintendent and project manager in their bid submissions and to commit to keeping those individuals on the project for the duration.

### References and Track Record

Reference checks on recent comparable projects are an essential part of contractor evaluation. The questions to ask are not generic — they should focus on the specific risks of the proposed project: How did they manage schedule? How did they handle change orders? What was their relationship with subtrades like? Did they maintain the key personnel they committed at award? Would you use them again?

A contractor who performed well on a 12-storey wood-frame building may or may not be capable of delivering a 30-storey concrete tower. Relevant comparable experience is a prerequisite, not just a preference.

## 7.5 Contract Types and Key Provisions

The contract is the legal instrument that governs the entire construction relationship — it defines scope, price, schedule, risk allocation, payment terms, change management, dispute resolution, and termination rights. On a major development project, the construction contract is one of the most consequential documents the developer will execute.

### Standard CCDC Contracts

The Canadian Construction Documents Committee (CCDC) publishes standard form construction contracts that are widely used across Canada. The primary CCDC forms relevant to Ontario development are:

**CCDC 2** — Stipulated Price Contract. Used for fixed-price GC delivery. The GC commits to deliver the project for a lump sum. Risk of cost increases above that sum (absent owner-directed scope changes) lies with the GC.

**CCDC 5A** — Construction Management Contract (CM as Agent). Used when the CM manages trade contracts as the developer's agent. Defines the CM's fee, general conditions scope, and the agent relationship.

**CCDC 5B** — Construction Management Contract (CM as Constructor). Used when the CM holds trade contracts directly. Provides a single point of contractual responsibility.

**CCDC 14** — Design-Build Stipulated Price Contract. Used for design-build delivery.

CCDC contracts are balanced documents — they allocate risks between owner and contractor in ways that reflect industry consensus on who is best positioned to manage each risk. Supplementary conditions modify the standard terms to reflect project-specific requirements, lender requirements, and the developer's commercial preferences.

### Key Contract Provisions

**Scope Definition:** The scope of work clause defines exactly what the contractor is obligated to deliver. Ambiguity in scope definition is the primary source of contract disputes. A well-drafted scope provision uses drawings and specifications as the primary scope definition documents, clearly establishes what is included and excluded, and addresses foreseeable grey areas explicitly.

**Contract Price and Payment:** The contract price provision defines the total compensation payable to the contractor and the mechanism for calculating progress payments. On CM contracts, the price is typically cost-plus-fee with a defined fee structure. On stipulated price contracts, the price is fixed subject to adjustments for owner-directed changes and specified contingencies.

**Schedule:** The contract schedule provision establishes the substantial performance date and the interim milestone schedule. Liquidated damages provisions — predetermined financial penalties for schedule delay — may be included on projects where the developer has specific date-driven financial exposure (such as a construction loan maturity date or a pre-sold condominium closing schedule).

**Change Order Process:** The change order provision defines how changes to scope, price, and schedule are authorized and priced. A well-drafted change order clause requires written authorization before work proceeds, establishes a pricing mechanism (typically cost-plus or agreed unit rates), and sets deadlines for submission and response. Change order disputes are one of the most common sources of construction litigation — clear process provisions reduce but do not eliminate this risk.

**Substantial Performance:** The substantial performance provision defines when the project is considered substantially complete for purposes of triggering the lien period, releasing the holdback, and commencing the deficiency correction period. This definition should align with the Ontario Construction Act's statutory definition.

**Dispute Resolution:** Most CCDC contracts provide for a tiered dispute resolution process: negotiation, then mediation, then arbitration or litigation. Arbitration is typically faster and more private than litigation and is the preferred resolution mechanism for construction disputes on private development projects.

**Termination:** The termination provisions define the developer's rights to terminate the contract for cause (contractor default) and for convenience (developer's decision to stop the project). Termination for convenience provisions are important on projects where the developer may need to pause or cancel due to market conditions or financing issues.

### Bad-Boy Carve-Outs

Construction loans typically require bad-boy carve-outs in the loan agreement — provisions that convert the otherwise non-recourse loan to full personal recourse if the developer engages in specified bad-faith conduct, including material misrepresentation in draw requests or reporting documents. The Senior Analyst's work product — draw packages and investor reports — is explicitly covered by these provisions. Accuracy in financial reporting is not just a professional obligation; it is a legal one.

## 7.6 Subcontractor and Trade Procurement

On CM-delivered projects, trade procurement is a structured process that mirrors the GC tender process at the trade package level. The CM prepares trade tender packages, solicits bids from qualified subcontractors, evaluates submissions, and recommends awards to the developer for approval.

### Trade Package Structure

A high-rise residential project is typically divided into 15 to 30 trade packages, each covering a defined scope of work corresponding broadly to the CCDC MasterFormat division structure covered in Chapter 5. Major trade packages on a GTA high-rise typically include: concrete structure and formwork; rebar supply and installation; masonry; structural steel; building envelope and cladding; windows and glazing; mechanical (plumbing, HVAC, fire protection); electrical; elevators; millwork and interior finishes; landscaping.

The way trade packages are defined — their scope boundaries, the interface between packages, and the allocation of shared costs — directly affects the quality of pricing received and the frequency of scope gap disputes during construction. Experienced CMs develop standard trade package structures over many projects that minimize interface ambiguity.

### Subtrade Pre-Qualification

For major trade packages, subtrade pre-qualification mirrors the process for the primary contractor: financial capacity, relevant experience, key personnel, bonding capacity, and safety record. For smaller packages, pre-qualification may be lighter — a review of the firm's portfolio and a reference check.

### Sole Source vs. Competitive Tender

Most trade packages are competitively tendered — multiple subtrades are invited to submit bids and the award is made based on price and qualifications. However, some items may be sole-sourced: a single supplier is engaged without competition. Sole sourcing is justified when only one supplier can provide the required product or service (a specialized system or proprietary technology), when the developer has a strong existing relationship with demonstrated performance, or when the competitive field is so thin that running a tender would produce only one meaningful submission.

Sole source procurement requires explicit justification and developer approval — it is not a decision the CM should make unilaterally. On LP-structured projects, institutional investors may have specific requirements around competitive procurement that must be respected.

## 7.7 Bonding, Insurance, and Risk Transfer

Bonding and insurance are the financial instruments through which procurement decisions translate into project risk protection. They are conditions of both the construction contract and the construction loan.

### Performance Bonds

A performance bond is a surety instrument issued by a bonding company (surety) that guarantees the contractor will complete the bonded contract. If the contractor defaults — through insolvency, abandonment, or material breach — the surety is obligated to either complete the contract itself or pay the developer the reasonable cost of completing the work through another contractor.

Performance bonds are typically required on trade packages above a defined threshold (commonly $2,000,000 on GTA high-rise projects) and are a standard condition of construction financing on projects above a certain size. The premium is typically 0.8% to 1.5% of the bonded contract value and is a project cost that flows through to the developer.

Performance bonds do not protect against poor quality workmanship or design deficiencies — they protect only against contractor non-performance.

### Labour and Material Payment Bonds

A labour and material payment bond guarantees that the bonded contractor will pay their subcontractors and suppliers. If the contractor fails to pay a subtrade, the subtrade can make a claim on the payment bond rather than registering a lien against the developer's property.

Payment bonds do not eliminate lien risk entirely — as discussed in Chapter 5, the Ontario Construction Act gives lien rights to all parties in the construction pyramid regardless of the contractual structure. However, payment bonds provide a funded mechanism for subtrades to recover unpaid amounts that reduces the practical likelihood of liens being registered against the developer's land.

### Builder's Risk Insurance

Builder's risk insurance covers the project against physical loss or damage during construction — fire, flood, wind, vandalism, accidental damage. The developer is the named insured, and the construction lender is named as additional insured. Maintaining a current builder's risk certificate with the lender named as additional insured is a standing condition of the construction loan and must be tracked throughout the construction period.

Builder's risk coverage typically extends from the commencement of construction to substantial performance. It does not cover design errors, contractor performance, or consequential losses from delay.

### General Liability Insurance

Contractors are required to maintain comprehensive general liability insurance throughout the construction period. The developer and lender are typically named as additional insureds on the contractor's policy. Minimum coverage limits are specified in the contract and are a condition of the construction loan.

### Professional Errors and Omissions Insurance

Design consultants — architects, structural engineers, MEP engineers — maintain professional errors and omissions (E&O) insurance that covers claims arising from design errors. E&O insurance is distinct from general liability: it covers the financial consequences of professional negligence, not physical damage or bodily injury. Confirming that all design consultants maintain adequate E&O coverage is a due diligence step that should occur at contract execution.

## 7.8 Procurement in the Ontario Context

Several features of the Ontario construction market and legal framework shape procurement decisions in ways that differ from other jurisdictions and from US-focused reference materials.

### The Ontario Construction Act

Ontario's Construction Act (formerly the Construction Lien Act) governs lien rights, holdback obligations, and the dispute resolution framework for construction projects in Ontario. Key provisions with direct procurement implications include:

The **statutory holdback** requirement — the owner must retain 10% of each progress payment and hold it in trust until the lien period expires following substantial performance. This holdback cannot be waived by contract and must be reflected in the payment structure of every trade contract and the draw package format submitted to the lender.

The **lien period** — 45 days from the date of publication of the Certificate of Substantial Performance. No holdback can be released while a valid lien is registered, and the 45-day period must be tracked carefully for each contract.

The **prompt payment** provisions — introduced in the 2019 amendments to the Construction Act — establish mandatory timelines for payment from owner to contractor (28 days from invoice) and from contractor to subtrade (7 days after receiving payment). Failure to comply with prompt payment obligations creates specific legal remedies. These timelines directly affect the draw cycle: if draw packages are submitted late or processed slowly, the developer may breach prompt payment obligations.

### Workplace Safety and Insurance Board (WSIB)

All contractors and subcontractors performing work on Ontario construction projects must be registered with the WSIB and maintain current clearance certificates. WSIB clearance certificates confirm that the contractor has no outstanding premium arrears and that workers on the project are covered. Lenders require current WSIB clearances as a condition of each draw advance, and they must be collected from all contractors and subtrades before payment is released.

### Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA)

Ontario's OHSA establishes the regulatory framework for workplace health and safety on construction projects. The developer, as the project owner, has specific obligations under OHSA including the requirement to retain a registered constructor who is responsible for health and safety on the project. The CM or GC typically serves as the constructor. Understanding the OHSA framework is relevant to procurement because it shapes the safety management obligations that must be reflected in the construction contract.

## AE-7.3 — Identifying Scope Gaps in a Tender Package

### Scenario

You are reviewing a tender package for the mechanical trade on a 22-storey purpose-built rental tower. The mechanical scope of work states: "Supply and install all HVAC systems, plumbing rough-in, domestic hot water systems, and fire suppression sprinkler systems within the building envelope."

A separate electrical trade package states: "Supply and install all electrical systems, including power distribution, lighting, emergency systems, and EV charging infrastructure."

The CM has flagged that three items may have scope gaps. Review the following items and assess whether each falls clearly within a defined scope, falls clearly outside all scopes, or represents a genuine gap requiring clarification:

1. The connection from the building's domestic water service to the municipal water main in the street
2. The thermostats and zone control panels for individual suite HVAC units
3. The power supply from the electrical panel to the mechanical room equipment

### Questions

1. For each of the three items, identify whether it is covered, excluded, or a gap.
2. Why is it critical to resolve scope gaps before tender rather than during construction?
3. What document should be issued to all tenderers once the gaps are resolved?

---

## AE-7.3 — Solution

**Item 1 — Municipal Water Service Connection**

This is likely a **scope gap**. The mechanical scope covers systems "within the building envelope," which excludes the external service connection. The civil trade package (if one exists) may cover this, but if there is no civil package, no contractor is responsible. The cost of the municipal connection — which may require road cutting, permit fees, and utility coordination — needs to be explicitly assigned to a specific trade or the civil scope needs to be added to the tender package.

**Item 2 — Thermostats and Zone Control Panels**

This is likely a **scope gap** between mechanical and electrical. The thermostats control mechanical HVAC equipment, suggesting they fall within mechanical scope. However, they require low-voltage electrical wiring and connection to control panels, suggesting electrical involvement. Without explicit language, both trades may exclude this item — each assuming the other is responsible. The scope documents need to explicitly assign thermostat supply, installation, and wiring to one trade with the other providing the required interface.

**Item 3 — Power Supply to Mechanical Room Equipment**

This is likely **covered** by the electrical scope — "power distribution" within the building would normally include distribution panels and connections to major mechanical equipment. However, confirming this requires reviewing whether the electrical scope explicitly includes mechanical equipment connections or whether it stops at the electrical panel. If ambiguous, an addendum should clarify.

**Why Resolve Gaps Before Tender**

Scope gaps discovered during construction become change orders — unplanned costs that draw from contingency and create schedule disruption. The cost of a change order includes not just the direct cost of the work but also the administrative cost of processing the change, potential delay if the work is on the critical path, and the friction it creates between the contractor and the project team. The same scope item that could be priced cleanly at tender for $45,000 may become a $75,000 change order during construction when it is urgent and the contractor has no competitive pressure.

**Resolution Document**

A formal **addendum** should be issued to all tenderers simultaneously, clearly resolving each gap. The addendum becomes part of the contract documents and all bids submitted after the addendum is issued must reflect the clarified scope.

## AE-7.4 — Bid Levelling Exercise

### Scenario

Three construction management firms have submitted proposals for a 28-storey purpose-built rental tower with a hard construction cost estimate of $95,000,000. The proposals are summarized as follows:

| Item | Firm A | Firm B | Firm C |
|------|--------|--------|--------|
| CM Fee | 4.5% | 3.8% | 4.2% |
| CM Fee ($) | $4,275,000 | $3,610,000 | $3,990,000 |
| General Conditions | $9,200,000 | $7,400,000 | $8,600,000 |
| Total CM Cost | $13,475,000 | $11,010,000 | $12,590,000 |
| Proposed Superintendent | 22 yrs high-rise | 8 yrs mid-rise | 15 yrs high-rise |
| Schedule Methodology | CPM schedule included | General phasing only | CPM schedule included |
| Bonding Capacity | $150M | $60M | $120M |
| References — comparable towers | 4 completed | 1 completed | 3 completed |

Firm B's proposal notes that their general conditions budget excludes a dedicated safety coordinator, assumes sharing of a project manager across two simultaneous projects, and does not include a tower crane operator.

### Questions

1. Before comparing total costs, what adjustments are needed to level the bids?
2. After levelling, which firm represents the best value and why?
3. What is the risk of selecting Firm B based on lowest total cost?

---

## AE-7.4 — Solution

**Question 1 — Levelling Adjustments**

Firm B's general conditions budget requires three additions before it can be compared on a like-for-like basis:

- **Dedicated safety coordinator**: On a 28-storey high-rise, a dedicated safety coordinator is not optional — OHSA obligations and lender requirements make this a mandatory resource. Market rate: approximately $120,000 per year for a 30-month project = $300,000.
- **Shared project manager**: A project manager shared across two projects provides approximately half the oversight of a dedicated PM. To normalize, add the cost of a dedicated PM for the project duration: approximately $180,000 per year x 2.5 years = $450,000 incremental cost.
- **Tower crane operator**: Excluded from Firm B's general conditions but required for the project. Approximate cost: $280,000 for the crane operation period.

Levelled Firm B general conditions: $7,400,000 + $300,000 + $450,000 + $280,000 = **$8,430,000**

Levelled Firm B total CM cost: $3,610,000 + $8,430,000 = **$12,040,000**

| Item | Firm A | Firm B (Levelled) | Firm C |
|------|--------|-------------------|--------|
| CM Fee | $4,275,000 | $3,610,000 | $3,990,000 |
| General Conditions | $9,200,000 | $8,430,000 | $8,600,000 |
| **Total (Levelled)** | **$13,475,000** | **$12,040,000** | **$12,590,000** |

**Question 2 — Best Value Assessment**

After levelling, Firm B remains the lowest cost option at $12,040,000 — approximately $550,000 below Firm C and $1,435,000 below Firm A. However, cost is only one dimension of the evaluation.

Firm C represents the best overall value on a balanced assessment:

- Superintendent with 15 years of high-rise experience versus Firm B's 8 years on mid-rise — directly relevant to a 28-storey concrete tower
- CPM schedule methodology versus Firm B's general phasing — meaningful difference in schedule management capability
- $120M bonding capacity versus Firm B's $60M — adequate headroom for a $95M project; Firm B's capacity is marginal
- Three comparable tower references versus Firm B's one — a thinner track record at this building type

The $550,000 cost difference between Firm B (levelled) and Firm C is approximately 0.6% of total project cost. On a 30-month construction project, a one-month schedule delay costs far more than $550,000 in carrying costs alone.

**Question 3 — Risk of Selecting Firm B**

The risk of selecting Firm B at the unlevelled price is that the $1,600,000 apparent saving evaporates entirely once the excluded resources are added — the levelled cost is only $550,000 below Firm C. Beyond cost, Firm B's limited high-rise experience and shared project manager structure create execution risks that compound over 30 months of construction. An underqualified site team generates RFI backlogs, missed coordination issues, subtrade management problems, and schedule drift — all of which eventually appear as cost and time overruns that dwarf the initial price advantage.

## AE-7.5 — Reviewing a Contract Clause

### Scenario

Your legal counsel has flagged the following clause in a proposed supplementary condition to the CCDC 5A agreement with the CM:

> *"The Construction Manager shall not be liable to the Owner for any delay, disruption, or additional cost arising from the acts, omissions, or defaults of any Trade Contractor, subcontractor, or supplier, whether or not such party was recommended by the Construction Manager, provided that the Construction Manager has exercised reasonable diligence in the management of such party."*

### Questions

1. What risk does this clause transfer to the developer and why is it significant?
2. Under what circumstances might the developer be willing to accept this clause?
3. What counteroffer language might the developer's counsel propose?

---

## AE-7.5 — Solution

**Question 1 — Risk Transfer**

This clause substantially limits the CM's liability for trade contractor performance — which is arguably the CM's primary function. Under this language, if a mechanical subcontractor causes a three-month schedule delay through poor performance, the developer cannot look to the CM for compensation even if the CM recommended that contractor — as long as the CM exercised "reasonable diligence." The practical effect is that the developer bears all financial consequences of trade contractor default, delay, or poor performance.

This is significant because on a CM-delivered project, the developer's primary recourse for trade performance issues runs through the CM. If the CM's liability is capped at "reasonable diligence," the developer's ability to recover delay costs from the CM is severely constrained. The developer is exposed to the full financial impact of trade contractor failures — cost overruns, delay damages, deficiency correction — with limited contractual recourse.

**Question 2 — When Might the Developer Accept This Clause**

The developer might accept this clause in limited circumstances: where the CM's fee is very competitive and the clause is presented as the commercial trade-off for the lower fee; where the CM has an exceptionally strong track record and robust subtrade relationships that make trade failure unlikely; or where the developer has strong performance bonds on all major trade packages that provide an alternative recovery mechanism for trade default.

In practice, institutional developers and their lenders rarely accept this clause without significant modification.

**Question 3 — Counteroffer Language**

Developer's counsel might propose:

> *"The Construction Manager shall exercise continuous and active supervision of all Trade Contractors and shall be responsible for the performance of all Trade Contractors recommended by the Construction Manager. The Construction Manager shall promptly notify the Owner of any trade contractor performance concern and shall take all reasonable steps to remedy such concerns at the Construction Manager's cost where the concern arises from the Construction Manager's failure to adequately supervise or to recommend a qualified contractor."*

This language preserves the CM's liability for their core supervision function and for recommending unqualified contractors, while acknowledging that not all trade failure is the CM's fault. The key additions are: active supervision obligation, notification requirement, and clear liability where the CM's own conduct (inadequate supervision or poor contractor recommendation) contributed to the problem.

## Chapter Summary

Procurement is the bridge between design and construction — the point at which concepts become contracts and financial commitments. The delivery model chosen sets the risk allocation framework for the entire construction period. The tender process determines whether the project enters construction with reliable pricing and qualified contractors. The contracts executed during procurement define the financial mechanics that govern every draw package and every budget variance for the next two to three years.

For a Senior Analyst whose work is centred on draws, budget tracking, and investor reporting, procurement is not an abstract process — it is the source of every line item in the construction budget. Understanding how contracts are structured, how trade packages are organized, how change orders are authorized, and how bonding and insurance protect the project gives the analyst the context needed to read draw packages intelligently, identify discrepancies, and report accurately to lenders and investors.

Ontario's Construction Act framework — statutory holdback, lien periods, prompt payment obligations — imposes specific procedural requirements on the payment process that have no equivalent in many other jurisdictions. These requirements are not administrative details; they are legal obligations that shape how every draw must be structured and processed.
